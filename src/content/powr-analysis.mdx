**What it does:** Indonesia's oldest and largest independent power producer selling directly to industrial customers. Since 1993 it has built, owned and operated gas, coal and (now) solar and biomass generation behind its own transmission and distribution network inside five adjacent industrial estates east of Jakarta (Jababeka, MM-2100, EJIP, Hyundai Inti Development, Lippo Cikarang — 5,375 ha, Bekasi Regency), under an exclusive "IUPTLU terintegrasi" licence that makes it a private power utility (PPU) rather than a PLN customer or a merchant generator. It sells the same power two ways: retail, at a premium price, to more than 2,500 industrial tenants of those estates (2025: 90.0% of revenue), and wholesale, at a much lower price, to state utility PLN under a legacy take-or-pay contract (10.0% of revenue). All operations and all revenue are in Indonesia; the Company reports in US dollars but bills customers in Rupiah with a monthly FX adjustment.

**Why this report exists:** the estates POWR serves — Cikarang, and neighbouring Karawang — have become one of Indonesia's two hyperscale data-center clusters, and data centers have gone from 2% of POWR's industrial kilowatt-hours in 2019 to 9% in 2025 and 12% in mid-2026, with the Company guiding to 14% / 17% / 20% by 2026F / 2027F / 2028F. Section 3.7 builds a bottom-up earnings model for that growth, cross-checked against an independent sell-side model (Stockbit), because POWR does not itself disclose data-center revenue, margin or customer count.

**Market:** Rp1,020/share (14-Sep-2026, most recent price point found; see §3.6 for the caveat on staleness) × 15,851,499,100 shares outstanding (ex-treasury) = **Rp16.17tn ≈ US$905m market cap** [calculated, IDR/US$17,856]. Trailing P/E ≈13.0x, dividend yield ≈8.2% (stockanalysis.com, 14-Sep-2026).

**Period covered:** FY2013–FY2015 from the 2016 IPO prospectus; FY2018–FY2025 audited (six annual reports, FY2020–FY2025 in hand plus the FY2018–2020 comparative inside AR2020); 1Q25/1Q26 and 1H25/1H26 from investor decks. Web research filled in tariff regulation, gas policy, the data-center landscape and peer/analyst context.

**Sources in hand:** six annual reports (AR2020 676pp, AR2021 596pp, AR2022 466pp, AR2023 474pp, AR2024 468pp, AR2025 438pp), the 2016 IPO prospectus (602pp, Indonesian), and seven investor-presentation decks (FY2021 through 1H2026). Full source table at the end.

**Conventions:** US$ million unless stated (the Company's functional and reporting currency). "Industrial" = the >2,500 tenants of the five estates, billed directly by POWR; "PLN" = the state utility, POWR's only wholesale customer. `[calculated]` marks every figure derived here rather than printed in a filing. MVA ("energized capacity") is the contracted/offtake capacity a customer has purchased, not its actual draw — the distinction matters throughout §2.6 and §3.7.

## THE ONE THING TO UNDERSTAND FIRST

**POWR is a low-growth, low-leverage, high-payout legacy power utility that has, almost by geographic accident, become a toll-taker on Indonesia's data-center boom — and it tells you the toll volume but not the toll rate.**

Strip out data centers and the rest of the business has barely grown in five years: non-data-center industrial volume has been flat at roughly 3.1–3.3 TWh a year since 2021 [calculated from deck and AR series, §2.6], PLN volume is shrinking as a legacy take-or-pay contract runs down, and the installed generation fleet has been unchanged at 1,144 MW of gas and coal since 2017 (only lately supplemented by 45.7 MWp of solar, 70 MW of biomass co-firing and a 50 MW gas-engine project under construction). Against that flat base, the Company discloses a second, fast-growing customer class — data centers — in unusual detail on one axis and near-total silence on the other:

- **What POWR discloses:** energized data-center capacity by year (32 MVA in 2019 → 227 MVA at end-2025 → a Company-guided 303/385/447 MVA for 2026F/2027F/2028F, a stated 34% CAGR 2019–2028F) and data centers' share of industrial kilowatt-hours sold (2% in 2019 → 9% in 2025 → 12% in 1H2026 → a guided 14%/17%/20% for 2026F/2027F/2028F) [AR2025 p.192, 197; decks FY21–1H26, §2.6 and §3.7].
- **What POWR does not disclose, in any of the six annual reports or seven decks:** the number of data-center customers, their names, the tariff or contract terms offered to them (capacity charge vs energy charge, minimum-take, tenor), data-center revenue or margin, or — critically — the **load factor** implied by that capacity. Working back from the one year the Company does split industrial revenue finely enough (FY2025: data centers were 9.1% of industrial kWh but 16.5% of industrial MVA), data-center load factor is running at roughly **16%**, against roughly **29%** for all other industrial customers combined [calculated, §3.5, §3.7]. That gap — customers paying to reserve capacity years before they draw the power — is both the opportunity (headroom for volume to catch up to contracted capacity with no new capex) and the central uncertainty (if the ramp stalls, or if data-center customers are billed mostly on the capacity charge rather than the energy charge, the kVA growth the Company advertises will not convert one-for-one into revenue).

Three further facts complete the picture. First, **POWR's own forecasting record on data centers is patchy**: the 2022 deck (published under a different management cycle) guided to 160 MVA for 2022; actual was 106 MVA. The 2023 deck repeated the same missed 160 MVA target for 2023; actual was 130 MVA. Only from 2024 did guidance and outcome converge (FY2024 deck: 225 MVA for 2025; actual 227 MVA) [DECKS_notes §9a]. Second, **2025 was not actually a clean year to read the trend from**: an "unprecedented" gas-supply disruption (Pertamina EP's Subang field shut down unplanned in August 2025, compounding a PGN shortfall that had been running since February 2024) forced diesel substitution, pushed fuel cost per kWh sold from 6.58¢ to 7.10¢, and cut consolidated net profit 4.4% even as industrial revenue grew [AR2025 p.176, 156]. Third, the **company itself is controlled, not widely held**: three private holding companies (Udinda Wahanatama, Brasali Industri Pratama, Pentakencana Pakarperdana), whose principals occupy four of six Board of Commissioners seats and the CEO's chair, jointly hold 83.76% of the shares; free float is roughly 13–15% [AR2025 p.118, 122] — governance context that matters when reading a nine-figure remuneration line against a net profit that fell in 2025 (§1.3–1.4).

So the investment question this report tries to answer is not "does POWR benefit from Indonesia's data-center boom" — it plainly does, and is one of the very few ways to buy that theme on the IDX — but **how much, at what margin, and how fast**, given that the Company will not say. Section 3.7 builds that number three independent ways and triangulates a range.

**A candid ownership history survives only in the 2016 prospectus** and is worth recording because none of the six annual reports repeats it. The company was founded in 1991 by six individuals in roughly equal shares (Sutanto Joso, Sudwikatmono, Budi Brasali, Ismail Sofyan, Ibrahim Risjad, Fonny Handayani Dwi Putro Widodo); by 1994 those stakes had been folded into holding companies, several of which merged in 1997–2003, and — the one fact with no trace in any later filing — **PT Jababeka Infrastruktur held 21% of the Company from March 1997 to August 2003**, when it sold out to PT Polamegah Utama [PRO p.108–110]. Sutanto Joso separately sat on the board of PT Kawasan Industri Jababeka (the estate developer) from 1989 to 2002. Today's three-way 30/27/27 split traces to a December 2009 transaction in which the last outside holder, Polamegah, sold its remaining stake asymmetrically to Udinda (which got the larger share) [PRO p.111]. No current equity tie between POWR and the Jababeka estate developer is disclosed, though POWR still pays "developer fees" to (unnamed) estate developers worth a steady ~0.9% of industrial revenue every year since at least 2013 (§1.4).

## 1.4 Related party transactions

**POWR's audited financial statements carry no related-party-transactions note at all** — an unusual absence for a company whose three controlling shareholders' principals sit on its Board. The MD&A states in every year read, in near-identical language, that there were "no material transactions containing conflicts of interest and/or transactions with affiliated parties and/or related parties" [AR2025 p.203, and equivalently in every prior AR]. The only related-party-flavoured disclosure across six annual reports is key-management compensation (§1.3) and the statement that all fuel suppliers and all customers are third parties.

That said, several recurring items sit close enough to the ownership structure to flag, none of them concluded by the Company:

- **"Developer fees"**, paid to the (unnamed) developers of the five estates, have run at a strikingly stable **0.8–0.9% of industrial revenue every year from 2013 to 2025** [calculated: US$3.0m/1990-15 → US$4.49m/2025], which reads as a formula-driven, long-standing arrangement rather than an arm's-length negotiated fee, but the counterparties and the formula are never named.
- **BLJ's 0.002% minority holder, "Janti Joso Brasali,"** carries a surname that spans both the Joso and Brasali controlling families — recorded, not concluded, since the Company gives no further detail.
- **Sutanto Joso's board seat at PT Kawasan Industri Jababeka (1989–2002)** and the three commissioners' concurrent seats at Metropolitan Land Tbk / Metropolitan Kentjana Tbk (a separate Jabodetabek property group) sit adjacent to POWR's own estate/property-adjacent business without any disclosed transaction.
- **A 2016-vintage related-party gas transporter, GPS ("Gas Pipeline Services"),** controlled by the same shareholder group, charged POWR US$0.36/MMBtu on Pertamina-sourced gas at IPO — the last year this appeared in any document we hold; by AR2020 the Company states flatly that there is "no related-party supplier," so either the arrangement lapsed or GPS's ownership changed. Neither is stated.
- **The single litigation exposure that touches a related party**: the Supreme Court's 2023 decision holding POWR, Pertamina Gas and the Ministry of ESDM jointly and severally liable to PT Gasindo Pratama Sejati for Rp175.74bn (about US$11.1m) over a reserve gas-pipeline permit; POWR paid its US$3.7m share in 2024 and recorded a right of claim against its co-defendants, recovered in 2025 [AR2024 Note 26; AR2025 p.170].

### Transmission and distribution

~60 circuit-km of 150 kV transmission (101 towers) linking the three plant sites, feeding ~2,000 circuit-km of 20 kV distribution across the five estates via an 18-bay 150 kV switchyard and eight 60/80 MW distribution transformers at MM-2100 [AR2025 p.152, 154, 160]. Network losses have stayed below 1.0% every year for at least five years (0.5% in 2025) — an efficient, short-haul, industrial-grade network, not a sprawling one.

Fuel is now more than half of revenue, up from 49.7% a year earlier and from 42% as recently as 2020 — the single most important line in the P&L, and the one the Company gives the least numeric detail on. **The 2025 margin story is entirely a fuel story**: fuel cost per kWh sold rose from 6.58¢ to 7.10¢ (+8%) on flat volume because diesel substitution during the gas disruption cost US$22.0m more than the year before (US$4.0m → US$26.0m), while gas itself actually cost 1.7% less and coal 3.2% less [AR2025 p.176–177, 259–267]. Company-defined EBITDA margin fell from 37.0% to 33.8% as a direct result.

**The tariff mechanism — what is and is not disclosed.** Across six annual reports and seven decks, the Company states only: (1) a "Government-approved electricity tariff formula" allows the fuel component to be passed through to customers [FS Note 31f]; (2) customer bills carry a monthly Rupiah/US-dollar adjustment; (3) customers post a security deposit of roughly one month's estimated bill, pay within 18 days (PLN: 30 days), and incur a 2%/month late fee. **Never disclosed, in any year**: whether industrial customers are billed on a two-part tariff (a capacity/kVA charge plus an energy/kWh charge, which the very existence of "energized capacity" contracts strongly implies) or on energy alone; the fuel-index formula itself; any minimum-take or contract-tenor terms, for ordinary industrial customers or for data centers specifically; and the realised tariff in Rupiah or cents per kWh (computable only by dividing revenue by volume, never printed). The 2016 IPO prospectus is again the exception: it discloses a **capacity charge of Rp9,000/kVA/month against a regulatory cap of Rp15,000**, plus a per-kWh usage charge with full gas-price pass-through [PRO p.165–166] — a structure management said at the time it deliberately underpriced (relative to the cap) "to encourage FDI." Whether that two-part structure, or anything like it, still applies a decade later is not confirmed by any subsequent filing.

## 2.5 Distribution — how the product is sold

- **Industrial:** direct billing to >2,500 tenants of the five estates, covering 96.6% of estate occupants [AR2025 p.166]. No named-customer concentration disclosure exists for industrial customers (unlike PLN, no industrial customer is named or sized) — a genuine gap, since it would show whether the data-center ramp is one or two large hyperscale accounts or many mid-sized colocation operators. Churn is negligible (0.4% in 2025) and bad-debt experience nil for industrial customers.
- **PLN:** a single wholesale offtake contract, POWR's only customer disclosed above 10% of revenue (10% in 2025, 14% in 2024) [AR2025 p.420].
- **Green layer:** I-REC sales (renewable certificates, 79,580 MWh in 2025 vs 6,018 MWh in 2023 — a 13x rise in two years, though absolute revenue is not disclosed and is almost certainly immaterial, likely \<US$0.1m at typical Indonesian I-REC prices of ~US$1–2/MWh) [calculated, illustrative] and rooftop solar leased directly to customer sites, accounted for as finance leases rather than owned generation.

### Who is actually buying the data-center power — from public sources, not POWR's own disclosure

POWR never names a data-center customer. Public industry sources (datacentermap.com, company press releases, trade press; treat as lower-confidence than the filings) identify a cluster of hyperscale/colocation operators with facilities inside or adjacent to POWR's estates, most prominently **DCI Indonesia** (named as a logo partner in POWR's own FY2021–FY2022 decks, before the Company stopped naming customers), **EdgeConneX** (formerly GTN Data Center, acquired May 2022, expanding a Lippo Cikarang campus toward a reported 200+ MW), **Datacomm Diangraha** and **MettaDC** (Jababeka), and a planned Keppel/IndoData facility at MM-2100. A materially larger cluster of announced hyperscale projects (NeutraDC/Telkom, Digital Edge, Princeton Digital Group, DAMAC/EDGNEX — collectively over 1 GW of announced capacity) sits in the neighbouring **GIIC estate at Deltamas, which is PLN-served, not a POWR estate** — a distinction worth holding onto, because headline "Cikarang data-center capacity" figures in trade press routinely blend POWR's addressable market with a much larger PLN-served cluster next door [web research, single-sourced trade press — see §2.7 caveats].

## 2.7 Demand

**Data-center demand — the story that matters.** Indonesia's national data-center electricity demand is projected by the energy ministry (ESDM) to grow from roughly 1.1 GW in 2025 to 2.1 GW in 2026, 2.7 GW in 2027, 3.5 GW in 2028 and 5.2 GW by 2033/34, with officials stating the demand will be served by "PLN holding, PLN Batam and other [private] wilayah usaha" — i.e. explicitly including PPUs like POWR, with no policy signal (as of the sources found) forcing data centers onto PLN exclusively [ESDM, 19-Nov-2025, via investortrust.id]. Globally, JLL projects data-center capacity nearly doubling from 97 GW (2025) to ~200 GW (2030), and CBRE projects Asia-Pacific capacity growing from 32 GW (2025) to 57 GW (2030), a 12% CAGR — both cited in POWR's own 1H2026 deck as the backdrop for its Cikarang/Karawang positioning [PRES_1H26 p.19]. **Caveat, important for sizing POWR's addressable market**: a large share of the announced hyperscale pipeline for "Cikarang" (NeutraDC, Digital Edge, Princeton Digital, DAMAC/EDGNEX — collectively over 1 GW announced) sits in the GIIC/Kota Deltamas estate, which is supplied by PLN directly via dedicated substations, not by POWR — so national and even "Cikarang cluster" data-center capacity headlines materially overstate POWR's own opportunity [web research; see §2.5].

**Industrial demand more broadly** tracks Indonesian manufacturing investment into the five estates; POWR's own non-data-center industrial volume has been flat (~3.1–3.3 TWh/yr) since 2021 even as new-vehicle wholesales and general industrial activity have fluctuated, suggesting the estates are close to fully built out for conventional tenants and that **incremental estate demand growth is now almost entirely a data-center phenomenon** [calculated, DECKS_notes §9b].

**PLN demand** is structurally shrinking: the 300 MW of original contracted capacity (two 150 MW tranches) is down to a single 150 MW tranche expiring May 2031, with no indication of renewal or replacement.

## 2.8 Supply

**Competing private power utilities in Indonesia** operating a similar direct-to-industrial model are not systematically disclosed by POWR and were not comprehensively researched here (a gap); POWR describes itself as covering 96.6% of tenants within its own five estates, implying near-monopoly supply within its licensed area but no visibility into whether new estates nearby are served by competing PPUs or directly by PLN.

**PLN itself** is both customer and, at the margin, competitor: any data-center operator can in principle be served directly by PLN (as the GIIC cluster next door demonstrates) rather than by POWR, so POWR's advantage is specifically the estates where its IUPTLU licence is exclusive, not a general Indonesian data-center power monopoly.

**Land supply** inside POWR's five estates is a genuine constraint on the upside case: more than 40% of the combined 5,375 ha estate area remains unelectrified per the Company's own disclosure, framed as headroom, but data-center customers are also described as having "acquired land for future expansion" — i.e., land banking by anchor tenants, which could either accelerate or (if land is held speculatively without building) decouple contracted-capacity headlines from real near-term load.

## 3.7 Scenario: the data-center earnings model

This is the section the report exists to answer. POWR discloses a capacity path and a volume-share path for data centers but not a tariff, a margin, or a customer count — so the model below is built bottom-up from what is disclosed, using three convergent approaches, and presented as a range rather than a point estimate.

### Step 1 — anchor on what FY2025 actually shows

Data centers were **9.1% of industrial kWh sold in 2025** (the one year the Company's sector pie chart is granular enough to isolate) [AR2025 p.161, mapped against PRES_FY25 p.6/p.19]. Applied to 3,455 GWh of industrial sales: **~314 GWh of data-center consumption**. At the industrial-average realised tariff of 14.42¢/kWh (the Company does not disclose a DC-specific tariff, so this is the working assumption, flagged as the model's single biggest source of error): **~US$45.3m of 2025 data-center revenue**, or about **8.2% of total company revenue**. At the 2025 fuel cost of 7.10¢/kWh, the gross spread is ~US$23.3m — roughly **12% of company-defined FY2025 EBITDA** [all calculated].

### Step 2 — project forward on the Company's own guided path

The Company guides data centers to **14% / 17% / 20%** of industrial kWh for 2026F/2027F/2028F (stated CAGR 34% for energized MVA, 2019–2028F) [AR2025 p.48, 197; PRES_FY25 p.19]. Holding non-data-center industrial volume flat at its 2021–2025 average of ~3,144 GWh/yr (the demonstrated pattern — see §2.7) and solving for total industrial volume at each data-center share:

| | 2026F | 2027F | 2028F |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data-center share of industrial kWh (Company guidance) | 14% | 17% | 20% |
| Implied total industrial GWh [calc: 3,144/(1−share)] | 3,656 | 3,788 | 3,930 |
| **Implied data-center GWh** | **512** | **644** | **786** |

### Step 3 — cross-check against the independent (Stockbit) model

Stockbit's total-sales-based data-center share (7%/12%/14% of *total* sales, a different denominator that includes PLN) implies data-center GWh of 291 (2025) / **525 (2026F)** / **645 (2027F)** on their own total-sales forecast — within 3% of the Company-guidance-based figures above (512 and 644) despite starting from an entirely different base and a different analyst. **This convergence is the strongest evidence in the whole report that the ~500–650 GWh 2026–27 range is a reasonable central estimate**, even though neither source discloses the tariff needed to turn it into profit.

### Step 6 — where this model is weakest, stated plainly

1. **The DC-specific tariff is an assumption, not a disclosed fact.** If data centers are billed predominantly on a capacity (kVA) charge with a low usage component — plausible, given the low observed load factor (§3.5) — then applying the industrial-average energy tariff to DC GWh could meaningfully overstate revenue in the ramp-up years and understate it once utilisation matures. The Company has never published a DC-specific rate card.
2. **Load factor could re-rate either direction.** If contracted MVA keeps growing faster than actual draw (as it has since 2023 — DC load factor fell from ~23% in 2019–22 to ~13–16% in 2023–25), the base case's implied load-factor recovery to ~21–22% by 2028F may be optimistic; if instead hyperscale tenants ramp faster than colocation tenants historically have (plausible given the AI-driven nature of the current pipeline), the bull case could understate revenue.
3. **Marginal opex is an assumption**, not derived from any disclosed DC-specific cost line; it is inferred from the fact that reserve margin absorbs most incremental volume without new fixed capex.
4. **The "flat non-DC industrial volume" assumption** is well-supported by 2021–2025 data but is itself an extrapolation.

## 3.8 Roadmap and what to watch

1. **Data-center share of industrial kWh, quarterly.** The single most important number the Company already discloses; watch for whether 1H26's 12% (already ahead of the FY2025 pace of 9%) continues to run ahead of the Company's own 14% FY2026 guidance.
2. **Gas-supply normalisation.** The PEP "Akasia Bagus" arrangement and PGN well-depletion recovery are the swing factor for whether fuel cost per kWh reverts toward 6.6¢ or stays near 7.1¢ — worth roughly US$0.5¢/kWh × 4,150 GWh ≈ US$20m of pre-tax profit either way.
3. **The unexplained US$28.7m substation project** — watch AR2026 for whether it is finally described as data-center-related infrastructure, which would be the first hard evidence of DC-specific capex.
4. **1H2026's tax-rate spike** (33.1% vs 21.7% in 1H25) — unexplained in every source read; watch whether it reverses or becomes the new normal, since it alone explained the entire 1H26 net-profit decline despite operating profit growth.
5. **Free-float compliance** by the March 2027 IDX deadline — the treasury-share sell-down could add modest sustained selling pressure through 2026–27 independent of fundamentals.
6. **2026 final dividend** (not yet declared in the documents read) as a read on whether the Company intends to keep payout near 95% given the tightened 2.0x leverage cap and the need to fund the gas-engine and substation projects.
7. **Named data-center customers/customer count** — a disclosure the Company could add at low cost and has not; its absence is the main reason this report's central number carries a ±40% range rather than a point estimate.
8. **Disclosure fixes to ask for:** DC customer count and named operators; DC-specific tariff terms; gas price (US$/MMBtu) and volumes; generation GWh by plant; the EBITDA definition; per-person board remuneration; the purpose of the substation CIP project; the FY2025 final dividend; reconciliation of the US$7.4m gas-incident insurance claim referenced in investor decks but absent from the audited financial statements.

# SUMMARY VERDICT

**What it is.** POWR is a debt-light, high-payout, single-segment power utility whose legacy business — selling gas- and coal-fired electricity to industrial tenants of five Jakarta-adjacent estates, plus a shrinking wholesale contract with PLN — has not grown its bottom line since 2019. Layered on top of that flat base is a genuinely fast-growing data-center customer class that the Company is happy to describe in capacity terms (MVA, guided to nearly double by 2028) and much less happy to describe in economic terms (no disclosed tariff, margin, or customer count).

**What it earns through the cycle.** Excluding the estimated data-center contribution, the rest of the business generated roughly US$47–60m of net income in FY2025 [calculated: US$72.1m total less the ~US$12–25m range of data-center contribution depending on which case is applied retrospectively] — a business earning a high-teens operating margin on a fully-depreciated, low-capex asset base, funded almost entirely by equity, and paying out 85–95% of profit as dividend. On today's ~US$905m market cap that legacy business alone, at ~13x trailing earnings for the whole company, is not expensive for a monopoly-like, investment-grade-rated utility yielding over 8%.

**What has to be true for the data-center upside to work.** Three things, in order of how much the model depends on them: (1) the Company's own 14%/17%/20% industrial-kWh-share guidance for 2026–28 has to hold, or come close — its forecasting record on this specific metric is mixed (two clear misses in 2022–23, on-target from 2024); (2) whatever tariff data centers actually pay has to be reasonably close to the industrial average used throughout this model, since no disclosed rate card confirms or refutes that assumption; (3) gas supply has to stabilise, because every dollar of margin the data-center ramp generates can be offset by continued diesel substitution at the fuel-cost line.

**What would break it.** A repeat of 2022–23's forecasting pattern (guided capacity growth that does not convert to guided volume growth) would leave data centers contributing closer to the bear case's US$12–13m/year than the base case's US$26–40m — not existential for a company this conservatively financed, but a meaningful drag on the re-rating the stock has already enjoyed through 2026. A prolonged gas-supply crisis, on top of a stalled DC ramp, would be the genuine downside case, given fuel is now 53% of revenue and the single biggest swing factor in every year examined.

**Position.** On the filings, POWR looks like one of the few clean, liquid ways to buy Indonesia's data-center power-demand theme on the IDX, at a reasonable multiple for the legacy business and a real — if wide — range of optionality on top from data centers, worth plausibly US$230–520m of incremental market value by 2028 in the base case alone (§3.7). The asymmetry is that almost all of the uncertainty in that number is disclosure-driven, not business-driven: the Company knows its own data-center revenue and tariff and has chosen not to publish either. A reader underwriting this stock is, in effect, underwriting management's capacity guidance and its fuel-pass-through claim, with only an independent sell-side model (itself single-sourced) as a cross-check.

*This is an analytical view of the business from its filings, not investment advice. I'm not a financial advisor.*
