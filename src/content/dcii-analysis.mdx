**What it does:** owns, builds and operates carrier-neutral colocation data centres in Greater Jakarta, plus one in Surabaya. Sells reserved electrical capacity (measured in MW of IT load) to cloud providers, banks, telcos and enterprises on multi-year contracts, together with interconnection, office "flexspace" and on-site technician services. It also operates data centres it does not own, for a share of their revenue.

**Where:** DCI H1 Cibitung (Kabupaten Bekasi, Jawa Barat) · DCI Platform H2 Karawang · DCI Platform E1 Jakarta (Ariobimo Sentral, Kuningan) · DCI E2 Surabaya · land under development at Bintan (Kepulauan Riau). Head office: Equity Tower 17F, SCBD Lot 9, Jakarta.

**Listed:** IDX, ticker **DCII**, 6 January 2021 at Rp420. **2,383,745,900 shares** outstanding — unchanged since listing (no split, no rights issue, no buyback, no bond). Last price **Rp203,700** (24 Aug 2026); market capitalisation **≈ Rp485 trillion**, the third-largest company on the IDX. Never paid a dividend.

**Period covered:** FY2017 – FY2025 audited, plus 1H2026 from public reporting.

**Sources in hand:** IPO prospectus (Jan 2021, 265pp), Annual Reports FY2020–FY2025 (2,073pp), Public Expose decks 2021, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026 (49 slides). Full inventory in the Sources table at the end. All figures **Rp million** unless stated; `[calculated]` marks anything derived.

## THE ONE THING TO UNDERSTAND FIRST

**DCII reports 128 MW of data centre capacity. It owns roughly 82 MW of it. The other ~46 MW — the entire H2 Karawang campus and the entire E1 Jakarta building — sits on the balance sheets of two private companies whose President Commissioner is Otto Toto Sugiri, DCII's own President Director.**

*One qualification, up front, because the rest of this section depends on it.* The filings establish that **DISM and DISP own those buildings** — AR2025 says so in terms ("DISM merupakan anggota DCI Platform yang **memiliki fasilitas pusat data**"), the accounts record DCII being charged "for the **use of DISM's data center building** by the Company", and E1's Uptime Institute certificates are issued to PT DC Indonesia Sukses Perkasa rather than to DCII. They also establish that **Otto Toto Sugiri is President Commissioner of both** [AR2025 p.121]. What no filing anywhere discloses is **who actually owns DISM and DISP.** They are classified only as "manajemen kunci yang sama / the same key management". Press reporting describes DISM as a **Salim Group** entity and the H2 campus as a Salim co-development. So the accurate statement is *chaired by* DCII's President Director, with the economic ownership undisclosed and probably Salim-linked — not *owned by* him. Where this report says "chairman-affiliated", read it that way.

The structure works in both directions at once, and both directions run through the same man:

Three consequences follow, and they reframe the whole company.

**1. The "128 MW" and the "market share" numbers are not comparable to peers.** When Digital Edge or Princeton Digital says 29 MW or 120 MW, that is capacity on their own books earning their own return on their own capital. DCII's 128 MW mixes 82 MW of owned capacity with 46 MW of rented-in capacity on which it earns a resale spread, plus an operating fee. Every per-MW ratio in this report is therefore computed twice — once on the reported 128 MW, once on owned capacity — and the two answers differ by roughly 50%.

**2. The FY2025 revenue growth is substantially a rental arbitrage, not a build.** The "Jasa sewa colocation" line inside cost of revenues went from **Rp35,465m to Rp232,583m — up 556% in one year** [AR2025 p.516]. DCII commissioned only one new building of its own in 2025 (JK6, 36 MW, RFS Q1). The rest of the +40.1% revenue growth came from taking related-party capacity onto its own P&L and reselling it. That is a real business — it is what a platform operator does — but it is a gross-margin-dilutive one, and it is exactly why the EBITDA margin fell from 65.3% to 61.0% and gross margin from 58.3% to 54.1%.

**3. The arrangement is disclosed, but only recently and only partly.** The 10–15% revenue-share mechanism first appears in the FY2025 report [AR2025 pp.198–199, Note 32i]. AR2024 named DISM and DISP as related parties and said only "Pendapatan usaha & beban pokok pendapatan". No transfer-pricing benchmark, no independent valuation, no expiry date and no minimum-volume commitment is disclosed for any of it. The counterparties are not consolidated and their financial statements are not public. Section 10 treats this as the primary red flag.

Everything else about DCII — the 13-year unbroken uptime record, the Tier IV certification, the 22% tax rate that is really 15%, the 0.0001%-a-day trading float, the Rp17 trillion bank facility that is 2.3× the balance sheet — is downstream of understanding that "DCI Platform" is a brand covering three balance sheets, only one of which shareholders own.

## 1.3 History — recap

Read as one line, the record says this:

**Thirteen years of technical execution wrapped around a persistently opaque ownership perimeter.** The operating record is exceptional and verifiable: from 3 MW to 128 MW, zero critical incidents since 2013, the first Tier IV in Southeast Asia, the first Tier IV Gold TCOS, greenfield-to-service in under twelve months on both JK6 (36 MW) and E2 (9 MW) — a build speed that most APAC operators cannot match. That is a real, hard-won operating moat, and it is why regulated banks and hyperscalers will sign.

Alongside it runs a second pattern that never resolves. The company was foreign-held through a Singapore vehicle for its first decade and was still 99.996% Singapore-held on the day the prospectus was signed. It bought land from its own founders one month before listing at a ~30% premium to what it paid an arm's-length seller six days later. Its controlling shareholder sold 11% of the company to Anthoni Salim in a negotiated crossing four months after the IPO. It has since built roughly a third of its branded capacity inside private companies chaired by its own President Director, rents that capacity back, and reports the whole thing as one platform. It has never paid a dividend, never split the stock despite two public consultations on doing so, and trades on a float that turns over about US$29,000 a day against a US$30bn market capitalisation.

Neither pattern cancels the other. The engineering is genuinely first-rate. The corporate perimeter is genuinely porous. An investor is buying both.

## 1.4 Owner's character

Read only from the record, not asserted:

**They build fast and they build early.** Otto Toto Sugiri founded PT Sigma Cipta Caraka in 1989 (Indonesia's pioneer IT services firm, later TelkomSigma — now listed among DCII's competitors), co-founded PT Indointernet in 1994 (one of Indonesia's first ISPs, now the listed EDGE), and founded DCI in 2011. Marina Budiman, Djarot Subiantoro and Jui Chihtra Gani all came out of Sigma Cipta Caraka too. DCII is the third company these people have built in the same industry. That is why a 12-month greenfield-to-RFS build is credible from them and would not be from a first-time operator.

**They spend on certification, not on marketing.** FY2025 marketing expense was **Rp5,297m — 0.21% of revenue** [AR2025 Note 25]. Promotion and marketing was Rp3,444m; travel Rp1,359m. Against that, the certification stack runs to fourteen items (Tier IV Design, Tier IV Facility, TCDD, TCCF, Tier IV Gold TCOS, ISO 9001/14001/27001/27701/45001/50001, SOC 1 & 2, PCI DSS, TVRA, B Corp, EcoVadis Silver, plus audited compliance with OJK 11/2022 and the IDX data centre guidelines). The company's stated marketing method is "direct offers to prospects" and **"referrals from the existing customer base"** — the same sentence, word for word, in 2020, 2021, 2024 and 2025. This is a business sold on procurement checkboxes and reputation, not on advertising, and management behaves accordingly.

**They retain everything.** No dividend has ever been paid, in fifteen years of existence and six as a listed company. The stated dividend policy at IPO was "up to 20% of net profit, starting FY2022" [PROS p.90]; that policy has never been implemented. Each year since 2023 the AGM has approved exactly **Rp5,000m to the statutory reserve** and retained the remainder "for data centre expansion" — Rp791,713m of FY2024 profit was retained on that basis in April 2025. The statutory reserve stands at Rp25,000m against a Rp59,594m target, 41.9% of the way there `[calculated]`.

**They pay themselves through EBITDA-linked phantom equity, not through dividends.** Directors' remuneration is set by the Board of Commissioners under delegation from the AGM, and includes a **Long Term Incentive in Phantom Shares**: units vesting over three years, priced at **a multiple of EBITDA valuation** using a methodology the Commissioners approve, paid in cash in year four. FY2024 Board of Directors remuneration was **Rp28,779m** against Rp4,150m for the Commissioners — the executive is paid ~6.9× the supervisory board `[calculated]`. FY2025 discloses the Commissioners' Rp4,387m but **not the Directors' figure at all**, which is a disclosure regression from the prior year. Key-management compensation in the accounts — which is the broader figure — was **Rp37,258m in 2025** (Rp24,869m to key management who are also shareholders, Rp12,389m to others) [AR2025 Note 32].

Note what the LTI is indexed to. Phantom units priced off an **EBITDA multiple** reward growing EBITDA in absolute terms. Renting in third-party capacity and reselling it grows EBITDA in absolute terms while diluting margin. The incentive is aligned with the volume-over-margin strategy management explicitly adopted in 2025.

**They disclose late, and they withdraw disclosure.** The pattern is consistent enough to be a characteristic:

| Item | Disclosed | Then |
|---|---|---|
| Related-party 10–15% revenue-share mechanism | first quantified in AR2025 | — |
| Bank loan interest margins (JIBOR + 0.45% / + 0.35%) | quantified in AR2024 | **replaced by "plus margin"** in AR2025 |
| Facility No. 71 (Rp200,000m working capital) | disclosed in AR2024 | **absent from AR2025**, unexplained |
| Directors' remuneration | Rp28,779m in AR2024 | **not disclosed** in AR2025 |
| Beban operasi lain component table | a table in AR2024 | **prose only** in AR2025 |
| Market-share source | Structure Research, cited 2020–2024 | **dropped entirely** in AR2025 |
| Cabinet counts (7,500 Cibitung, 1,280 EDGE) | AR2021 only | **never repeated** |
| Contracted % of installed capacity ("91%") | AR2021 only | **never repeated** |

**They behave conservatively with the balance sheet, right up until they don't.** DSCR has never been below 1.94× against a 1.25× covenant; debt-to-equity fell from 1.95× (2020) to 0.35× (2024) against a 3.0× ceiling; the company was free-cash-flow positive in 2022 and 2023 and repaid debt. Then in FY2025 capex went to Rp1.72tn, debt rose 77%, and in May 2026 a **Rp17 trillion facility — 2.3× the entire balance sheet** — was signed. Fitch expects Rp10–15tn of capex a year in 2026–27. The conservative phase is over; whether the same discipline survives an 8× step-up in spending is the open question.

### The agreements, one by one [AR2025 Note 32, pp.525–538]

**PT Datacenter Indonesia Sukses Makmur (DISM) — the big one.** Two agreements.
- *Master Service Agreement, 16 April 2021* — DCII provides services to DISM on order letters.
- *Data Center Facility Operation and Support Agreement, 18 June 2021* — DISM is building a data centre; DCII manages the preparation of operational processes, provides building-monitoring and security expertise, manages day-to-day activities of the facility **"but not the operation"**, conducts periodic performance review, and works jointly with DISM on generating deals and contracts. Fee: **US$500,000 one-time technical consulting fee plus 10–15% per month of DISM's monthly recurring revenue** from all facilities at the data centre. MRR includes colocation, cross-connect and interconnection; **excludes power pass-through**. Amended 1 December 2023 to **10% for billable capacity of 10 MW and above, 15% below 10 MW**, on DISM facilities "registered as DCI Platform members".

### How to read the RPT block

Two economically distinct things are bundled in these tables and should be separated.

**(a) The fee business is small, high-margin and defensible.** DCII earns **Rp42,511m of fee and revenue-share income from DISM and DISP** (Rp56,846m across all related parties) on assets it did not fund. On any measure this is a good business: near-zero incremental cost, no capital, and it extends the brand's footprint. **(b) The rent business is large, margin-dilutive and new.** Rp264,787m of related-party cost of revenues in FY2025, up 14.6× in a year, is not a fee arrangement — it is DCII buying wholesale capacity from its chairman's private companies and reselling it retail. # 02 — MODEL

## 2.1 The product category — a primer, because this is a new industry

Before the segments, the physics. Everything in this report hangs off one sentence:

> **A colocation data centre is a power-delivery and heat-removal utility that rents itself out by the kilowatt on long contracts.**

### Power is the product; space is the packaging

A server rack is a steel frame roughly 0.6 m × 1.1 m × 2 m — about **one square metre of floor**. What separates a cheap rack from an expensive one is not its footprint but **how many kilowatts you may draw through it**. Every watt drawn must be (1) delivered from the grid through transformers and switchgear, (2) backed up by UPS and batteries so it never drops, (3) backed up again by diesel generators for a grid failure, and (4) **removed as heat**, because essentially 100% of the electricity going into a server comes back out as heat.

Steps 1–4 *are* the building. None of them scale with square metres; all of them scale with kilowatts. Hence the conventions:

- **Capex is quoted in US$ per MW of IT load** — not per m².
- **Revenue is quoted in US$ per kW per month** — not per m².
- Floor area is a by-product. At 5 kW/rack you need ten times the floor plate per MW that you need at 50 kW/rack.

"10,000 m² of data centre" tells you almost nothing. "**20 MW of IT load, Tier IV, 82% utilised**" tells you everything. This is why DCII quotes itself in MW and why every table in this report does the same.

### The four business models, and which one DCII is in

| Model | What the customer buys | Deal size | Contract | Who DCII is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Retail colocation** | A rack, half-rack or cage with a power commitment | 1 rack – 500 kW | 1–3 yrs | partly |
| **Wholesale colocation** | An enclosed suite or a whole data hall, priced per kW reserved | 500 kW – 20 MW | 5–10 yrs | **mostly, and increasingly** |
| **Hyperscale build-to-suit / powered shell** | A purpose-built building to one tenant's spec | 20–200 MW | 10–15 yrs, triple-net | JK6 resembles this |
| **Cloud (IaaS/PaaS)** | Compute and storage as a service | per VM / GPU-hour | consumption | **no — these are its customers** |

The first three are **landlord businesses**. The fourth is a technology business. AWS, Azure, Google Cloud and Alibaba are DCII's *tenants*, not its competitors — a distinction worth holding onto, because their capex announcements are DCII's demand, not its threat, right up until they decide to self-build.

Retail commands a large premium per kW because of granularity and service. Digital Realty's own Q2 2026 signings show the gradient cleanly: **0–1 MW leases at ~US$280/kW/month versus >1 MW at ~US$157/kW/month**, a 78% gap on its blended book; separately disclosed, the retail premium runs **88% in the Americas, 73% in APAC and 61% in Europe**. Any operator drifting from retail to wholesale sees revenue per MW fall and margin percentage fall, even as absolute profit rises. **Hold that thought for §7.5 and the FY2025 margin compression.**

### What is on the invoice

| Line | What it is | Typical price |
|---|---|---|
| Space + power (base rent) | Reserved kW of IT capacity plus the footprint | US$150–475/kW/month |
| Metered power | Actual kWh drawn, at cost or cost-plus | pass-through in wholesale; bundled in retail |
| **Cross-connect** | A physical fibre or copper patch between two customers inside the building | US$100–300/month each |
| Remote hands ("smart hands") | The operator's technicians touching your kit | US$100–200/hour |
| Setup / rack-and-stack | One-off | US$500–3,000 |

Cross-connects matter far beyond their price. A patch cable has near-zero marginal cost and ~100% gross margin, but its real function is **switching cost**: once 200 of your counterparties, carriers and cloud on-ramps are one cable away inside a building, leaving means re-establishing 200 relationships. This is the network-effect moat in colocation, and it is why Equinix's churn is 1.8% a quarter. DCII sells this as "Interconnection Service" and "DCI Internet Exchange"; it does not disclose the revenue or the count, which is a real gap (§ Missing Data).

### The three capacity numbers you must never confuse

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Installed / commissioned** | MW physically built, tested and energised. What the capex bought. |
| **Contracted (booked)** | MW under signed contract, including capacity not yet delivered or ramped. |
| **Billed (utilised)** | MW actually generating revenue this month. |

The gap between contracted and billed is **backlog** — the highest-quality forward visibility in the business. DCII disclosed all three exactly once, in the prospectus, at 31 August 2020: **installed 22,200 kVA, contracted 17,593 kVA, online 16,690 kVA** — 79.2% contracted, 75.2% online. It has disclosed a contracted ratio once since (**91% of installed capacity contracted at end-2021**), and nothing at all since then. Fitch's July 2026 review states a **contracted-to-shell ratio above 90%**, which is the only current datapoint and comes from the rating agency, not the company.

### Tier certification — why DCII's certificates are the product

The Uptime Institute grades the *topology* of the power and cooling systems:

| Tier | Redundancy | Uptime | Annual downtime | Build cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I Basic | N | 99.671% | 28.8 hrs | — |
| II Redundant components | N+1 | 99.741% | 22.0 hrs | — |
| **III Concurrently maintainable** | N+1, one active path | 99.982% | 1.6 hrs | **US$9–14m/MW** |
| **IV Fault tolerant** | 2N or 2N+1, both paths live, compartmented | **99.995%** | **26.3 min** | **US$14–22m/MW** |

Tier III means you can take any single component offline for planned maintenance without dropping the load. **Tier IV means any single unplanned failure, at any time, is survived automatically**, with physical compartmentation so a fire in one path cannot reach the other. The premium is roughly **US$5–8m/MW (+30–55%)** and is spent almost entirely on electrical redundancy — a second transformer, a second generator yard, a second UPS plant, and the walls between them.

Certification comes in three flavours: **Design Documents** (paper review), **Constructed Facility** (on-site testing — the credible one), and **Operational Sustainability / TCOS** (ongoing operations). DCII holds all three, and TCOS at **Gold**, first in Southeast Asia. For a bank, an exchange or a Monetary Authority of Singapore-regulated entity, the certificate is the procurement checkbox that permits the signature. That is the commercial function.

The SLA is a separate, contractual promise — typically **99.999%** ("five nines", 5.3 minutes a year), with penalties as service credits of **25–200% of monthly base rent** for falling below, up to 500% for connection outages. The asymmetry is brutal: a 26-second outage under a hyperscale SLA can cut a facility's annual NOI by ~6.7%; an hour can erase 40%. **This is why "we have never had an outage in thirteen years" is not marketing — it is a balance-sheet asset**, and it is DCII's single strongest claim.

### Unit economics of the asset class

```
Worked example: a 50 MW wholesale facility, triple-net, at US$150/kW/month
  Contracted capacity            50 MW = 50,000 kW
  Rent           50,000 × $150 × 12                 = $90.0m/yr
  less management + ground lease                    = ($5.0m)
  less capital reserves                             = ($5.0m)
  Stabilised NOI                                    = $80.0m/yr  ≈ $1.6m per MW
  Development cost @ $11.5m/MW × 50                 = $575m
  YIELD ON COST = 80 / 575                          = 13.9%
  Exit value @ 6.0% cap rate                        = $1.33bn
  Value created                                     = $755m
```

**Build at a 10–13% yield on cost; own an asset the market values at a 5.5–7% cap rate.** That spread, capitalised, is the entire value-creation engine of the industry, and it is why operators raise capital to build rather than harvesting cash — and why DCII pays no dividend. Digital Realty is building 769 MW at an expected **11.9% stabilised yield**, 64% pre-leased.

Three consequences matter for reading DCII's accounts:

1. **A new hall is dilutive for 12–24 months.** On commissioning, depreciation starts in full, interest stops being capitalised, fixed opex runs at near-full level, and revenue is near zero and ramps. A fast-growing operator therefore shows *falling* margins even when every individual asset is improving. Separate the stabilised book from the pipeline.
2. **EBITDA margins are 45–60% and net income is small.** Equinix runs ~53%, Digital Realty ~55% — but net income is only 11–22% of EBITDA. The gap is depreciation (US$11m/MW of 20–30-year assets) and interest. Net income under IFRS tells you little about cash.
3. **You cannot compare EBITDA margins across operators without knowing who books the power.** An operator that resells electricity shows a bigger revenue line and a lower margin percentage for identical economics. Normalise to **EBITDA per MW**, not margin percentage. DCII **absorbs** power (see §2.2), so its margin is structurally lower than a pure triple-net landlord's and its PUE is its own problem.

### The AI shift, in one table

| Cooling | How | Density supported | PUE |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional air (CRAC/CRAH + containment) | Chilled air, hot-aisle containment | **4–8 kW/rack**, ~15 kW max | 1.4–2.0 |
| Rear-door heat exchangers | Water coil on the back of the rack | 20–40 kW/rack | 1.2–1.4 |
| **Direct-to-chip liquid** | Coolant through cold plates on the GPU | **40–100 kW/rack** | 1.1–1.3 |
| Immersion | Servers submerged in dielectric fluid | 100–250 kW/rack | 1.02–1.10 |

An NVIDIA **GB200 NVL72** rack draws **~120–132 kW** — fifteen to thirty times what the world's existing colocation floor was designed for. Above ~40 kW/rack, liquid cooling is not an optimisation, it is the only option. Crucially, **retrofit is mostly not viable**: the binding constraint on an old building is the utility feed, the substation and the generator yard, not the cooling. You cannot put 130 kW racks into a hall whose incoming capacity was sized for 8 kW. **AI capacity is therefore overwhelmingly new-build** — which favours whoever can build fastest with power already secured. That is the specific reason DCII's sub-12-month build record and its Cikarang Listrindo supply matter more in 2026 than they did in 2020.

And the split that decides where Indonesia sits: **training** campuses (500–1,000 MW, latency-insensitive) follow the cheapest electrons on earth and will not come to Java. **Inference** (20–100 MW, 20–50 ms metro latency budget) must sit near the users. Indonesia's 229 million internet users plus its financial-sector data-residency rules make it an inference-and-enterprise market. That is the structural case, and it is a real one.

Five things to take from that table.

**(a) Electricity plus depreciation is the business.** Together they were **52.7% of cost of revenues in 2019, rising to 84.1% in 2022 and 79.9% in 2023, then falling to 63.2% in 2025** as the rent-in line arrived `[calculated]`. Electricity itself has run in a narrow **14.4–19.2% of revenue band** across seven years (14.4% in 2019, 19.2% in 2022, 18.0% in 2025) — consistent with power being repriced through to customers within the bundled price rather than absorbed at a fixed rate.

**(b) DCII absorbs power; it does not pass it through as a landlord.** All electricity is bought from **PT Cikarang Listrindo Tbk** — an independent power producer at Cibitung, **not PLN**. In FY2025 Cikarang Listrindo supplied **Rp396,786m = 86.7% of the electricity line** `[calculated]`; in earlier years it was 100%. Because DCII buys the power and sells a bundled kW price, **PUE is a direct gross-margin driver for shareholders, not for tenants**. That makes DCII's refusal to publish a PUE number more significant than it looks (§ Missing Data). One external report puts JK5's PUE at 1.5 (2021); Indonesian best-in-class in a tropical climate is 1.4–1.6, since free cooling is unusable year-round.

**(c) "Material instalasi" is a capex-cycle line hiding inside cost of sales.** It is the physical fit-out of a customer's cage or suite, and it tracks *new capacity commissioned in the year*, not the installed base. It was Rp116,371m in the JK3/JK5 build year of 2020, Rp5,571m in the quiet year of 2022, Rp132,038m in 2024, Rp40,265m in 2025. **Gross margin swings of five percentage points come from this line alone and mean nothing about the underlying business.**

**(d) The FY2021 margin expansion was an accounting artefact, not operating leverage.** Revenue grew 14.7% and cost of revenues grew **1.5%**, lifting gross margin from 48.7% to 54.6%. The mechanism: electricity (+33,173) and depreciation (+41,658) both grew hard, but were more than offset by **material instalasi halving (−58,518)** and **licensing and commission going to exactly zero (−17,675)** because the Equinix agreement was terminated in September 2020. Strip both out and the run-rate cost base grew **+32.2%**, faster than revenue `[calculated]`. This is worth remembering whenever DCII's margin moves.

**(e) The FY2025 margin compression is real, and it is the rent-in line.** "Jasa sewa colocation" went from Rp35,465m to **Rp232,583m, +555.8%** — and Rp264,787m of total cost of revenues came from related parties, 22.69% of COGS. Management's own explanation for the margin fall is different and softer: *"strategic pricing optimisation to secure larger-scale and longer-term contracts… a deliberate volume-driven approach"* [AR2025 p.183]. Both can be true. But the arithmetic says the biggest single new cost in FY2025 was renting a chairman-affiliated building, not discounting to customers.

## 3.2 Who the customers actually are, by tier

**Tier 1 — hyperscalers. These are the megawatts.**
AWS *Asia Pacific (Jakarta)* since Dec 2021 (US$5bn/15yr commitment); Microsoft **Azure Indonesia Central launched 28 May 2025** inside a US$1.7bn 2024–28 commitment, with BCA, Pertamina, Adaro, Telkom, Siloam and the Ministry of Finance among 100+ launch customers; Google Cloud Jakarta since Jun 2020; **Oracle Indonesia North launched Jul 2025 — in Batam, not Jakarta**; Alibaba Cloud (2 AZs since 2018/19), Tencent Cloud (2021), Huawei Cloud; ByteDance/TikTok, whose Tokopedia merger in Jan 2024 put a very large Indonesian transactional dataset squarely inside OJK and PP 71 scope.

Critically, **Indonesian hyperscalers have historically leased rather than self-built** — unlike in Johor. That is the structural reason DCII, PDG, BDx and Digital Edge exist as third-party landlords with hyperscaler anchor tenants. A shift to self-build is the single biggest threat to the model.

**Tier 2 — domestic digital.** GoTo, Grab, Traveloka, Blibli, Bukalapak, Shopee Indonesia, the digital banks. Mostly heavy *public-cloud* consumers rather than direct colocation tenants — they drive hyperscaler MW, not their own racks. (No DCII–GoTo relationship is publicly established; GoTo's announced 2025 cloud agreements are with Tencent and Alibaba.)

**Tier 3 — banks and financial institutions. This is the legally captive segment.** BCA, Mandiri, BRI, BNI, the insurers, the exchange, payment processors. Small in MW, high in price per kW, and it is exactly what DCII's E1 CBD asset and its TVRA/PCI DSS/SOC/OJK-11/2022 certification stack are built for. DCII disclosed **124 BFSI clients in 2020** and ~100 financial institutions in 2025.

**Tier 4 — government.** Pusat Data Nasional, ministries, the sovereign-AI push. Politically important, commercially patchy, and volatile — the **June 2024 PDN ransomware attack paralysed 282 government agencies** and became a national scandal.

**Tier 5 — telcos**, who are simultaneously customers, landlords and competitors. Telkom's NeutraDC and Indosat's BDx/Zankore are the two domestic vehicles trying to move up from carrier to AI-infrastructure provider.

## 3.3 The regulatory floor — what is actually captive

This is the most misunderstood part of the Indonesian data-centre case, and it has been **loosened once already**.

**PP 71/2019 (in force Oct 2019) replaced PP 82/2012 and *relaxed* localisation.** PP 82/2012 had required *all* electronic system operators to site data centres and DR centres in Indonesia. PP 71/2019 split them: **Public ESOs** (state bodies and their appointees) must stay onshore unless the technology is unavailable domestically; **Private ESOs may host offshore**, subject to registration with Komdigi, granting authorities access for supervision and law enforcement, and consent rules for cross-border transfer. Administrative fines run to 2% of annual revenue, enforceable from October 2024. **This is why AWS, Google and Microsoft could serve Indonesia from Singapore for years.**

**The real onshore mandate is sectoral, and it lives at OJK, not Komdigi:**

| Rule | Effect |
|---|---|
| **OJK Reg 11/2022** | Banks: **primary data centre AND disaster-recovery centre must be in Indonesia.** The hard localisation. |
| OJK Reg 3/2024 | Fintech (ITSK) localisation |
| OJK Reg 27/2024 | Crypto-asset services must use Indonesian data centres |
| OJK Reg 4/2021 | Non-bank financial institutions |
| Bank Indonesia PBI 23/6/2021 | Payment-system processing authorisation |

DCII is independently audited for compliance with **OJK Regulation 11/2022** and with the **IDX IT Infrastructure and Data Center Design Guidelines v2.0** (auditor: Veda Praxis) [AR2025 pp.37–41]. Those two audits are the licence to serve the captive segment.

**The PDP Law (UU 27/2022) is fully enforceable since 17 October 2024 — and has no regulator.** As at August 2026 the **Lembaga PDP** required by Article 58 has never been established; the presidential regulation creating it was submitted 20 May 2026 and remains unsigned, and the implementing regulation setting fine procedures cleared review in December 2025 and is also unsigned. **The 2%-of-revenue administrative fine is therefore structurally unavailable today.** Enforcement runs on older instruments and on access-blocking.

**The practical read.** Demand that converts reliably into Indonesian colocation today is: OJK-regulated financial workloads, government workloads, and latency-sensitive inference. Everything else — general consumer internet, e-commerce, most SaaS — is legally free to sit in Singapore, and largely does. **If the Lembaga PDP is finally stood up and the 2% fine becomes live, that is a step-change upward in Indonesian colocation demand. It is the single biggest free option in this case, and it has slipped repeatedly since 2022.**

## 3.4 AI demand — real, but announcement-heavy

This is the live swing factor, and the reason Jakarta's development pipeline jumped 56% in six months.

- **Zankore by Indosat** (Indosat Ooredoo Hutchison + Ooredoo + NVIDIA + Nokia), announced **7 Aug 2026**: a target of **1 GW of NVIDIA "DSX AI Factory" infrastructure across SE Asia, with an initial ~200 MW in H1 2027** on GB300 NVL72, based in Indonesia. Bundled with Sahabat-AI (an Indonesian LLM), an AI Centre of Excellence and an AI-RAN programme.
- **BDx CGK4 at Jatiluhur** — launched Dec 2024 as an AI campus, NVIDIA DGX-Ready certified, live with H100s and specced for GB200 NVL72 liquid-cooled density.
- Indonesia's national AI strategy targets AI contributing **9.7% of GDP by 2030**; the Indonesian AI market is projected from ~US$2.4bn (2025) to ~US$10.9bn (2030), ~28.7% p.a. [cited in AR2025].
- McKinsey, cited by DCII itself: **AI workloads could more than triple between 2025 and 2030.**

**The single most important supply fact: Jakarta's development pipeline more than doubled in twelve months (709 MW → 1,700 MW) while operational capacity barely moved.** Indonesia is not a built-out market; it is a market where a very large wave of capacity is scheduled to land in 2026–2029, gated by power energisation and construction lead time rather than by capital.

Set that against demand: **329 MW under construction is ~96% of the existing live base, and roughly six years of demonstrated net absorption at 55 MW a year.** Jakarta's 20.5% vacancy is already the second-worst in APAC after Bangkok, against Johor at 0.7% and Singapore at 4.9%.

Two things could reconcile the gap, and the DCII case rests on which one you believe:
1. **Absorption accelerates violently** because AI and hyperscaler demand arrives as 20–50 MW single leases rather than 1–2 MW enterprise deals. The tell is pre-leasing — NeutraDC's Batam campus was reported fully secured before launch, and DCII's own contracted-to-shell ratio is above 90% per Fitch.
2. **Much of the pipeline never gets energised** — PLN connection, financing and GPU allocation fail, and the 1.7 GW stays on paper. This is broadly what happened in Johor, where 1.2 GW was contracted and only ~600 MW drawn.

**Be very careful with "Indonesia has X GW" headlines.** One source puts national capacity at 600–650 MW in 1H2026 "rising to 1.7 GW by year-end", which cannot be reconciled with Knight Frank's 344 MW for Jakarta unless the former counts *contracted grid MVA* rather than *live IT load*. Always check which.

## 4.4 Power — the binding constraint, and Indonesia's real edge

**PLN tariffs effective 1 April 2026:**

| Group | Description | Rp/kWh | US$/kWh @ 17,692 |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| **I-4 / TT** | Large industry >30,000 kVA, transmission voltage | **996.74** | **0.0563** |
| I-3 / TM | Industry >200 kVA, medium voltage | 1,114.74 | 0.0630 |
| B-3 / TM | Business >200 kVA, medium voltage | 1,114.74 | 0.0630 |

**Indonesian industrial power is roughly half Malaysia's and a third of Singapore's.** For an AI campus where electricity is 40–60% of operating cost, that is decisive, and it is the strongest single argument for Indonesian capacity taking regional share. Combined with build costs of US$8–11.6m/MW — among APAC's cheapest — this is the durable structural advantage. Note it is an *administered* price, frozen for non-subsidy groups through 2025 and into 2026, not a market one.

**Java has generation surplus but not connection capacity.** PLN reports a ~6.8 GW surplus concentrated on Java; the constraints are transmission, substations, permitting and land. **RUPTL 2025–2034** (issued 28 May 2025) adds 69.5 GW nationally, of which **33.5 GW to Java-Madura-Bali**, plus 47,758 circuit-km of transmission and 107,950 MVA of substations, and **explicitly names data centres a priority load**. The next RUPTL, expected late 2026, is worth watching for whether MW are ring-fenced for the West Java clusters.

**The PLN monopoly is the sector's structural sore point.** PLN holds a legal monopoly over generation, transmission and distribution, so an operator cannot build its own utility-scale renewable plant and wheel the power to its campus. That collides with the fact that ~60% of data centre operators face net-zero-by-2030 commitments while Indonesian REC supply is short. The fix is **power wheeling under the RUU EBET**, stalled since 2019. Whether it passes with a wheeling provision is a genuine, checkable catalyst.

**DCII's specific position on power is unusual and under-appreciated.** Its Cibitung campus does not buy from PLN at all — it buys from **PT Cikarang Listrindo Tbk**, a listed independent power producer serving the Cikarang industrial estates, under a 15 February 2016 business-cooperation agreement that renews automatically for as long as Cikarang Listrindo holds its generation licence. Two independent substations feed the campus, which is a Tier IV requirement. DCII also holds its **own generation licence** (Izin Operasi Usaha Penyediaan Tenaga Listrik Untuk Kepentingan Sendiri, valid to 12 July 2029) and ~25 approved diesel gensets. **No PLN agreement for any DCII site is publicly documented.** That is simultaneously a strength (a private, industrial-estate-grade supply outside PLN's queue) and an undisclosed dependency (one supplier, no take-or-pay, no tariff, no term).

## 4.5 Policy and tax

- **Foreign ownership: unrestricted.** Under Perpres 10/2021 as amended by Perpres 49/2021, data centres carry **no foreign ownership cap**. This is why the pipeline is foreign-funded.
- **Tax holidays** under PMK 130/2020 as amended by PMK 69/2024 run from 50%/5 years at Rp100–500bn of investment up to **100%/20 years at ≥Rp30tn**. DCII has two (see §8.4). Whether a data centre qualifies as a "pioneer industry" is not clean, and the **OECD Pillar Two** global minimum tax blunts holidays for in-scope multinationals — though DCII itself is explicitly **out of scope of PMK 136/2024** [AR2025 Note 30j].
- **KEK / SEZ Batam and Bintan** carry CIT reductions, VAT exemption, import-duty waivers and tax holidays of up to 20 years. This is the fiscal logic of the Bintan project.

# 05 — KEY VARIABLES

What actually moves this model, ranked, with weights.

**1. Contracted MW, and the pace at which contracted becomes billed.** Revenue is capacity × price × utilisation, and price barely moves (see 2). Every Rp of revenue growth since 2019 has come from more MW. At ~Rp20–25bn of revenue per operating MW, **each incremental 10 MW commissioned and filled is worth roughly Rp200–250bn of annual revenue** `[calculated]` — about 8–10% of FY2025 revenue. The 36 MW of JK6 alone, fully ramped, is worth roughly Rp720–900bn.

**2. Price per kW — flat, and the evidence says it stays flat.** Management stated at IPO that price changes had no material effect on revenue over three years, and has never disclosed an ASP since. Derived revenue per operating MW (§8.2) has moved within a narrow band. **Do not underwrite rate increases.** In a market at 20.5% vacancy with 329 MW under construction, the risk is negative renewal spreads, and DCII's own FY2024 report names "**perang harga**" — a price war — as a risk [AR2024 p.71].

**3. Electricity — 18.0% of revenue, 39.2% of cost of revenues, one supplier.** A 10% move in the Cikarang Listrindo tariff is **Rp39.7bn on FY2025 numbers — 3.4% of pre-tax profit** `[calculated]` (Cikarang Listrindo supplied Rp396,786m of the Rp457,760m total; a 10% move across the whole electricity line would be Rp45.8bn, or 3.9%), before any repricing to customers. Because DCII absorbs power rather than passing it through, this is a shareholder risk, not a tenant risk.

**4. PUE — undisclosed, and it is a direct margin lever.** At a tropical-climate PUE of 1.5 versus 1.35, a 100 MW IT load consumes 15% more purchased power for the same billable output. On FY2025's Rp457.8bn electricity bill, **each 0.1 of PUE is worth roughly Rp30bn of gross profit** `[calculated]`. DCII publishes no number.

**5. The rent-in spread.** Rp232.6bn of "jasa sewa colocation" cost in FY2025 against related-party revenue of Rp56.8bn. If the resale margin on rented capacity is materially thinner than on owned capacity — and the FY2025 margin compression says it is — then **mix shift toward rented capacity is a structural margin headwind independent of pricing**.

**6. Utilisation ramp and the J-curve.** JK6 (36 MW) was RFS in Q1 2025 and is still in Phase 3 fit-out at 15.25% complete. E2 (9 MW) commences commercially in 2026. Both carry full depreciation and full fixed opex from commissioning. **A fast-growing operator shows falling margins even when every asset is improving** — this is the single most important thing to hold in mind when reading DCII's declining margin series.

**7. Interest rates.** 83.5% of bank debt is floating at 31 Dec 2025. Disclosed sensitivity: **±100 bp changes pre-tax profit by ±Rp12,738m** — 1.08% of FY2025 pre-tax profit. Small today; on Fitch's projected Rp10–15tn a year of capex it becomes large fast.

**8. The tax holiday roll-off.** Sheltered taxable income fell from Rp423,624m to Rp360,999m and the effective tax rate rose from 11.82% to 15.14%. The JK3 holiday's 100% band appears to have run 2020–2024 and JK5's 2021–2025 `[calculated inference — the FS do not state expiry]`. **A move from a 15.1% to a 22% effective rate is roughly Rp81bn of net profit at FY2025 scale** `[calculated]`, unless new facilities win new holidays.

**9. Customer concentration.** Two customers were 47.95% of FY2025 revenue. A single non-renewal by a hyperscale tenant vacates capacity in 20 MW blocks, not 200 kW blocks. This is the largest single-event risk in the business.

**10. Currency.** Revenue contracts are billed predominantly in Rupiah and capex is "predominantly Rupiah-denominated" per AR2025's new capital-commitment section — but **USD trade receivables collapsed from US$12.25m to US$3.12m in one year** while Rupiah receivables rose, evidence of a deliberate shift of the customer book to Rupiah billing. Equipment prices still reference USD. The May 2026 Rp17tn facility is **IDR and USD**, which reintroduces FX exposure on the liability side for the first time. The IDR is at 17,692/US$ against ~16,300 a year earlier.

# 06 — VICISSITUDES

Data centres are not a cyclical commodity business, so the shocks that matter are different in kind. What the record shows:

**Covid-19 (2020–21) was a tailwind, not a shock.** Revenue grew 55.0% in 2020 and 14.7% in 2021 while the Indonesian economy contracted 2.07%. The prospectus is explicit that the pandemic accelerated the digital economy. There is no down-year anywhere in DCII's history: revenue has risen every year since 2017, from Rp127bn to Rp2,540bn — a **CAGR of 45.3% over eight years** `[calculated]`.

**The single-site era (2013–2021) was the real fragility, and it has been reduced.** The prospectus ranked "**bencana alam**" — natural disaster — as a material risk explicitly because "**sebagian besar sumber pendapatan Perseroan berasal dari SATU LOKASI**". Everything DCII earned came out of one 8.5-hectare campus in Kabupaten Bekasi. That is now four locations across two provinces, with E2 Surabaya explicitly designed as Jakarta's redundancy site. A repeat of the 2020 Jakarta floods, or a seismic event, would now impair rather than halt the business.

**The SLA is the asymmetric exposure.** Under a 99.999% SLA with credits of 25–200% of monthly base rent, a **26-second outage can cost ~6.7% of a facility's annual NOI, and an hour over 40%**. DCII has never had one: zero critical incidents since 2013, 100% power uptime, and — the strongest evidence — an Uptime Institute **Tier IV Gold TCOS**, which audits operations rather than design. The correct way to hold this is that the downside is not gradual. Thirteen years of flawless operation is worth a premium precisely because one bad afternoon is worth a large discount.

**Regulatory reversal has already happened once.** PP 82/2012 required all operators to host onshore; **PP 71/2019 removed that for private operators.** DCII's own MD&A called the change unhelpful to local providers. A future government could tighten again (the PDP Law regulator, the One Data Bill) or loosen further. **Demand that rests on regulation rests on politics.**

**The 2021 and 2025 share-price episodes.** DCII rose from Rp420 to Rp59,000 within six months of listing and was **suspended by the IDX on 17 June 2021 pending an examination into possible transaction manipulation**, remaining halted for fourteen trading days. In February 2025 it was halted twice in three days and placed on the **Papan Pemantauan Khusus with a Full Call Auction mechanism**; in July 2025 it was suspended again for nine trading days, by which point it was up 723.6% year to date, reopening on the FCA board. (The +81.45%-in-a-week figure belongs to the February 2025 episode, not July.). It reached Rp398,000 in August 2025 and is **down ~49% from that peak**. None of this touched the operating business — but it is the record of how this security behaves, and §8.5 explains why.

**What a genuine down-cycle would look like, since there has never been one.** The sequence would be: Jakarta vacancy stops falling as the 329 MW under construction energises → renewal spreads go negative → DCII's already-flat revenue per MW starts falling → the J-curve on JK6 Phase 3 and E2 lengthens → depreciation and interest on Rp10–15tn a year of new capex arrive on schedule while revenue does not → covenant headroom (DSCR 4.27×, DER 0.47×) compresses fast from a very comfortable base. The company has never operated with meaningful leverage and has never operated in an oversupplied market. Both conditions arrive simultaneously in 2026–2028.

The four float numbers measure different things and no filing reconciles them. What is not in dispute is the consequence. On **16 July 2026** the order book showed **one to two lots (100–200 shares)** at successive price levels; DCII fell 7.4% and **took roughly 15 points off the IHSG**, making it the single largest negative contributor to the Indonesian market index that day.

A top-three index weight with a two-lot order book means the market capitalisation is a mathematical artefact of the last price, and the last price is set by trivial volume. This is the mechanism behind every episode in §06: the IDX suspension of June 2021, the twin halts of February 2025, the Full Call Auction board, the July 2025 suspension, the Rp398,000 peak and the 49% drawdown since.

Management has been asked about it. In February 2025 it confirmed it was "exploring" a stock split to improve liquidity; in July 2025 it denied split rumours and stated there was no corporate action. **No split, no rights issue, no bonus issue, no buyback and no bond has ever occurred.** The share count is the same 2,383,745,900 it has been since 6 January 2021.

One month before the IPO, DCI bought **20,606 m² in Kabupaten Bekasi from Marina Budiman, Otto Toto Sugiri, Bing Moniaga and Han Arming Hanafia** for **Rp125,971,054,253**, under four deeds (AJB Nos. 22–25/2020, all 14 Dec 2020), paid in full on 7 October 2020 at a uniform **Rp6.11m per m²**.

**The founders were paid a ~30% higher price per square metre than an unrelated seller in the same week, in the same district, for a larger parcel.** `[calculated]` No valuation, no fairness opinion and no explanation appears in the prospectus, the legal opinion or any annual report. Title was still being certified into the company's name at the date of the legal opinion, which flagged it as an exception to its own clean opinion on asset title.

## 9.2 The IPO, January 2021 — and what it did not restructure

**357,561,900 new shares at Rp420**, 15% of enlarged capital, gross **Rp150,176m**, sole underwriter **PT Buana Capital Sekuritas**, effective 29 December 2020, listed 6 January 2021. Use of proceeds: **80% capex** (51 low-voltage panels for JK5, Rp67,281m; 6 gensets, Rp58,356m) and the rest working capital. Realisation: Rp119,763m of the Rp125,637m capex plan and Rp20,312m of working capital, fully utilised by 2023.

Three features are worth stating plainly.

**The Singapore holding company was not unwound before listing.** At the prospectus date **DCI International Holding Pte. Ltd. held 99.996%**, and post-offer it would hold **84.996%**. The only "restructuring" resolved in Akta No. 30 was the change of status from **PMA to non-PMA** effective at or after listing. The distribution of shares from the Singapore vehicle to the individuals happened **after** listing — the register at 30 April 2021 shows the holdco gone and named individuals in its place, with no filing explaining the mechanism.

**There was no lock-up on any pre-IPO share.** The prospectus states it explicitly and the legal opinion confirms it: "**Tidak terdapat larangan bagi DCI International Holding Pte. Ltd. dan Gunawan Tenggarahardja untuk mengalihkan sebagian atau seluruh kepemilikan sahamnya**". Every pre-IPO share was free to move from day one.

**The offer was priced at roughly 8x FY2019 earnings and 6.3x annualised 8M2020 earnings.** Rp420 against post-split FY2019 EPS of ~Rp52.6 `[calculated]`; annualising the 8M2020 net profit of Rp105,218m gives Rp157,827m against an implied post-money market capitalisation of Rp1,001bn = **6.3x**.

## 9.3 Anthoni Salim's entry, May 2021

On **31 May 2021**, four months after listing, **265,033,461 shares (11.12%) changed hands at Rp5,277**. The block was **carved out of the three founders' own post-IPO holdings** — Otto Toto Sugiri went from 815.1m to 712.8m shares, Marina Budiman from 650.0m to 536.5m, Han Arming Hanafia from 407.5m to 336.4m, a combined reduction of **286,939,503 shares** `[calculated from the published registers]` against the **265,033,461** Salim received. **The ~21.9m-share balance went somewhere else and is unaccounted for.** It was a negotiated crossing, not a market purchase. Reported consideration is inconsistent across sources — Rp1.01tn for 192.7m shares in one, ~Rp1.4tn for 265.0m in another, both at Rp5,277 — and the discrepancy is unresolved.

The stake has been **completely static at 11.12% ever since**; Salim has never added and never sold, and holds directly rather than through a vehicle. On 15 June 2021 DCII was notified that he intended to **pledge (gadai) DCII shares**, described by the corporate secretary as "part of Salim Group's investment plans in Indonesia"; whether the pledge was executed or released has never been disclosed.

The relationship is operational as well as financial: **H2 Karawang was co-developed with the Salim entity DISM**, **E1 was built "in collaboration with Salim Group"**, and **Bintan is a JV with Salim's SGX-listed Gallant Venture Ltd**. Salim is not labelled a controller (the three founders are), holds no board seat, and appears nowhere in the related-party note — his exposure runs entirely through DISM, DISP and Gallant, none of which DCII consolidates.

## 9.4 The Equinix termination, September 2020

DCI's relationship with Equinix began in 2012 with two deeds signed 24 September 2012 — an **Akta Biaya Komisi** (commission fee) and an **Akta Biaya Layanan Teknis** (technical services fee), both renewed on 1 January 2019. DCI gave notice of termination on **14 September 2020**, effective 30 days later; Equinix acknowledged on 21 September 2020. The fees ran through the **"Lisensi dan komisi"** line: Rp16,739m in FY2019 and **Rp17,675m in FY2020, going to exactly zero in FY2021**.

Structurally, DCII stopped paying a foreign operator a fee and started charging a related party one. **Equinix subsequently entered Indonesia on its own**, investing US$74m and opening its own Jakarta facility in May 2025. It is now a competitor.

## 9.5 The refinancing, May 2023 — OCBC NISP to Bank Mandiri

Credit Facility Agreements Nos. 34–38 dated **11 May 2023** replaced seven years of OCBC NISP facilities in a single transaction: **Rp1,251,921m committed at 7.50% fixed for three years**, 89 months to 23 October 2030, fully drawn, explicitly for "**pengambilalihan fasilitas hutang dari PT Bank OCBC NISP Tbk**". **Rp1,383,606m was repaid to OCBC in 2023.** The AGM had mandated pledging more than 50% of net assets.

This was a straight cost-of-funds trade — from JIBOR + 200 bp floating to 7.50% fixed for three years, right before Indonesian rates rose — and it shows in the numbers: finance costs fell from Rp99,207m (FY2023) to **Rp79,791m (FY2024)** while the business grew 38.8%. Note the fixed period **expires 11 May 2026**, after which the loan reprices to 3-month Compounded IndONIA plus an undisclosed margin.

## 9.6 The Sarana Megahtama acquisition, July 2023

**50.005% of PT Sarana Megahtama for Rp110,000m**, via 5,001 newly issued SMT shares, deed of 7 July 2023. The minority is **PT Inti Indotek Informatika (49.995%)**, a party described nowhere. What came in: **Rp189,624m of land and Rp3,129m of building**.

It contributed **Rp1,226m of profit in FY2025** on Rp217,956m of assets — a return on assets of **0.56%** `[calculated]`; the Rp613m that appears on the face of the P&L is the 49.995% minority's share, not SMT's whole result. No summarised financial information for it is disclosed beyond total assets. ## 9.7 The Danantara purchase, October 2025

On **21 October 2025** DCII paid **Rp500,000m** for two long-term debt instruments issued by **PT Danantara Investment Management (Persero)** through **private placement**, maturing October 2030 and October 2032, bearing a **fixed coupon of 2% per annum**. The instruments are **pledged to Bank Mandiri**. They are carried at **Rp501,238m using unobservable valuation inputs**, with a **day-one deferred difference of Rp89,068m**, of which Rp2,533m was released to profit in FY2025. See §10.2.

## 9.8 The Rp17 trillion facility, May 2026

Disclosed 6–7 May 2026: a credit facility of **Rp17 trillion (≈US$980m)**, denominated in **both IDR and USD**, reported as from **Bank Central Asia** with Fitch also naming Bank Mandiri. **Rp17 trillion is 2.3× DCII's entire March-2026 balance sheet and 3.9× its equity.** Tenor, pricing, covenants and amounts drawn have not been disclosed. It also introduces **USD debt into a company whose revenue is now billed predominantly in Rupiah** — the FX position reverses from a natural long-dollar asset position to a liability exposure.

# 10 — RED FLAG

## 10.1 The platform perimeter — related-party capacity, both bought and operated

**The evidence.** DCII reports 128 MW; ~46 MW of it (H2 Karawang 27 MW, E1 Jakarta 19 MW) is owned by **PT Datacenter Indonesia Sukses Makmur** and **PT Datacenter Indonesia Sukses Perkasa**, both of which have **Otto Toto Sugiri — DCII's President Director and 29.90% shareholder — as President Commissioner** [AR2025 p.121, Note 32]. E1's Uptime Institute certificates are issued in DISP's name, not DCII's [PUBEX2025 p.5]. DCII's own gross fixed assets, net of land and CIP, support roughly the 73 MW H1 campus, not 128 MW (§8.1).

**The money flow, FY2025.** DCII paid DISM **Rp255,290m** "for the use of PT Datacenter Indonesia Sukses Makmur's data center building by the Company" and DISP **Rp9,229m** on the same basis — together **Rp264,519m — 22.66% of cost of revenues and 10.41% of revenue**, against **Rp8,354m, or 1.11% of COGS, a year earlier**. Including every related party, related-party COGS went from 2.39% to 22.69% of cost of revenues. It received **Rp42,511m** back as operating fees and revenue share. **Net Rp222,008m left the listed company** `[calculated]`. The payable to DISM stands at Rp83,220m, non-interest bearing.

**Why it deserves suspicion, specifically:**

1. **The pricing is unbenchmarked.** A 10–15% revenue share and an unstated rent per MW, both set between a company and its own President Director's private vehicles. The arm's-length assertion is identical boilerplate in every year, supported by no comparable, no valuation and no independent opinion.
2. **The direction of value is asymmetric.** DCII bears the tenant relationship, the SLA risk, the brand and the sales cost; the private vehicles bear the capex and collect the rent. The listed shareholders fund the demand generation; the private vehicles own the assets that demand fills.
3. **It is not a lease.** Renting ~46 MW of data hall is expensed as "jasa sewa colocation", not capitalised under PSAK 73. The group's entire recognised right-of-use asset is **Rp1,658m of office space**. That keeps roughly Rp232.6bn of annual obligation off the balance sheet and out of the debt-to-equity covenant, with **no disclosed term, no minimum commitment and no renewal mechanism**.
4. **It scaled 14.6× in one year with no explanation.** Related-party cost of revenues went from Rp18,130m to Rp264,787m between FY2024 and FY2025. The annual report explains the margin decline as pricing strategy and does not mention the rent line at all in the MD&A.
5. **Disclosure came late.** The 10–15% mechanism, the US$500,000 and US$250,000 consulting fees, and the DISM/DISP agreement names all appear for the first time in the FY2025 report, four to five years after the agreements were signed. The 2020 EDG agreement was disclosed in the prospectus with the term "**Tidak diatur**" and no fee at all.
6. **It is not new — it is just newly reversed.** In FY2021, related-party *revenue* was **Rp111,573m, 12.81% of the company's revenue**, of which **DISM alone was Rp92,675m (10.64%)** — one of the four customers above 10% that year, and **88.7% of the year's entire revenue growth** `[calculated]`. Third-party revenue grew 1.69% in FY2021. The platform-fee model was already the growth engine five years before the agreements behind it were described in the accounts; what changed by FY2025 is that the cash now flows the other way.

7. **Two related parties were de-related by a resignation, not by a divestment.** Otto Toto Sugiri resigned from the boards of PT Indointernet (14 Mar 2024) and PT Ekagrata Data Gemilang (2 Apr 2024). Commercial dealings continued at scale — Rp37,674m of purchases from EDG and Rp18,345m of revenue in FY2025 — but now sit in **third-party** lines. The related-party tables therefore understate the true intensity, and any year-on-year comparison of related-party exposure is broken.

**What would settle it:** DISM's and DISP's audited accounts, the rent per MW paid, an independent transfer-pricing benchmark, and a statement of who owns DISM and DISP and in what proportions. None of that is public.

## 10.2 Rp500 billion at a 2% coupon, pledged to the lender

DCII paid **Rp500,000m** in October 2025 for privately placed Danantara paper yielding **2% fixed**, at a time when its own bank debt cost ~5.95% and its time deposits earned 5.0%. On any ordinary reading this is a **negative-carry asset of roughly Rp20bn a year in foregone spread** `[calculated]` — Rp500,000m × (5.95% − 2%) = Rp19,750m, or Rp15,000m against the 5.0% it earns on its own time deposits —, funded in a year when the company drew Rp1,100,000m of new bank debt and ran free cash flow of negative Rp194,950m.

The accounting compounds the question. Because the instruments are not traded, fair value is determined using **unobservable inputs**, and the difference between the Rp500,000m transaction price and the assessed fair value — **Rp89,068m** — was recognised as a **deferred day-one gain**, released to profit over time (Rp2,533m in FY2025, of which Rp1,238m appears on the face of the P&L). In other words, the company booked a valuation gain on a below-market-rate instrument it had just bought at par, using inputs no third party can observe.

The instruments are **pledged as collateral to Bank Mandiri**. That is the only functional explanation the filings support — that the paper is a security-package component rather than an investment. **No business rationale is given anywhere.** PT Danantara Investment Management is a state entity; DCII is a private company with no disclosed state relationship. This is Rp500bn — 12.5% of equity and half a year's capex — deployed without explanation.

## 10.3 "No concentration of credit risk" against two customers at 48% of revenue

Every set of accounts since 2019 contains both of these statements:

> *Note 23 (revenue):* "individual customers with total transactions of **more than 10% of revenues** consist of transactions with **2 customers**… amounting to **Rp1,217,909**" — **47.95% of FY2025 revenue.**
>
> *Note 37b (financial risk):* "The Group has **no concentration of credit risk** as its trade receivables relate to a **large number of ultimate customers**."

The second statement is about receivables and the first about revenue, so they are not literally contradictory — but **the accounts disclose no receivables concentration at all**, which means the assertion of no concentration is unsupported by any disclosed number, while the one concentration figure that does exist points the other way. Set alongside **past-due receivables rising from 11.0% to 36.6% of the book with zero expected-credit-loss provision**, the combination is uncomfortable. The prospectus was more honest: it put customer concentration inside the top-ranked risk factor and argued the mitigant explicitly.

## 10.4 The pre-IPO founder land premium

Covered in §9.1 and repeated here because it belongs on this list: **a ~30% price premium per square metre paid to four insiders in the same week as an arm's-length purchase in the same district, one month before listing, with no valuation and no explanation, funded by a purpose-specific bank loan.** Rp126bn is small against today's balance sheet; it was **19.5% of equity** at the time `[calculated]`. What makes it a flag is not the amount but the absence of any process around it, in a document that had to satisfy OJK.

## 10.5 Disclosure that goes backwards

Individually minor; as a pattern, a flag. Between FY2024 and FY2025 the company **withdrew** the loan interest margins (JIBOR + 0.45% and + 0.35%, replaced by "plus margin"), **dropped** a Rp200,000m facility from the note with no explanation, **stopped disclosing directors' remuneration** (Rp28,779m the year before), **replaced** a component table for other operating expenses with prose, and **dropped Structure Research** — the source of every market-share claim it had made since 2020 — without replacing the claim. Meanwhile a Rp22,165m "Lain-lain" line in other operating income, eight times the prior year, is not broken out at all.

Against that, FY2025 *added* real disclosure: the revenue-share mechanism, a segment profitability table, a capital-commitment section, and — for the first time — the full sustainability data series (electricity 264.0 GWh, water 610,833 m³, Scope 1/2/3 emissions). The direction is not uniformly bad. But the items withdrawn are all items an outside analyst would use to check the related-party economics or the cost of debt, and the items added are all items that make the growth story easier to tell.

## 10.6 What was checked and does *not* qualify

For completeness, the things that look like flags and are not:

- **No litigation, ever.** No court case, arbitration, competition proceeding, tax dispute, labour dispute or somasi against the company or any director or commissioner, in any year from the prospectus to FY2025. No administrative sanction from OJK or the IDX.
- **The audit opinion has been unmodified every year**, with no emphasis of matter, and the only Key Audit Matter is land valuation — which is the right KAM for a company carrying Rp1,079bn of Level 2 land.
- **No restatement.** Every FY2024 figure in AR2024 is identical to the FY2024 comparative in AR2025. The FY2024 cost-of-revenue *disaggregation* changed (Rp96,507m of "Lain-lain" was split into three named lines) but the total did not.
- **The covenant record is clean** — DSCR never below 1.94× against 1.25×, DER never above 1.95× against 3.0×, and no breach in fifteen years.
- **The going-concern position is strong**: Rp1.54tn of operating cash flow, Rp225,000m undrawn, Rp255,781m of cash, and a Rp17tn facility signed.
- **The uptime record is verifiable through third parties** — Uptime Institute Tier IV Design, Constructed Facility, TCDD, TCCF and **Gold TCOS**, plus SOC 1 and 2 Type 2, PCI DSS, TVRA, ISO 9001/14001/27001/27701/45001/50001, B Corp, EcoVadis Silver, and audited compliance with OJK 11/2022 and the IDX data centre guidelines. This is not a company that fakes its operations.

The pattern across §10.1–10.5 is not fraud and it is not incompetence. It is a **group being run as one enterprise across a listed vehicle and several private ones, with the listed vehicle disclosing the minimum the rules require and no more.** That is legal in Indonesia and common among family-controlled listed companies. It is also the reason a shareholder cannot compute this business's economics from its accounts.

# MISSING DATA AND OPEN ITEMS

Ranked by how much each would change the analysis. For the top items, where the data most likely sits and what it would decide.

**1. Who owns DISM and DISP, on what terms DCII rents from them, and what those entities earn.**
*Would decide:* whether the "128 MW / market leader" framing is meaningful, whether the FY2025 margin compression is a mix effect or a value transfer, and what DCII's real return on capital is. *Likely location:* the Indonesian company registry (AHU) filings for both entities; Salim Group / Gallant Venture disclosures; any POJK 42 affiliated-transaction disclosure DCII may have filed with the IDX. **Nothing in the annual reports discloses the shareholders of either company, the rent per MW, the term, or any minimum commitment.**

**2. Installed vs contracted vs billed MW, and utilisation, for every year since 2021.**
*Would decide:* whether DCII is filling its halls or building into vacancy, and the size of the J-curve drag in each year. *Likely location:* investor presentations to institutions, Fitch's full rating report (which quotes contracted-to-shell >90%), or a Structure Research Jakarta report. Disclosed by the company **once**, at 31 August 2020, and once as "91% contracted" at end-2021. Never since.

**3. Price per kW per month, and any escalator or renewal-spread data.**
*Would decide:* whether the flat revenue-per-MW in §8.2 reflects stable pricing or a mix shift from retail to wholesale, and whether Jakarta's 20.5% vacancy is already forcing rates down. *Likely location:* Structure Research *Jakarta Data Centre Market* or Arizton's *Indonesia Colocation Portfolio Report* — both paywalled, and **Jakarta/Johor/Batam rates are published nowhere free**. This is the one research report worth buying if the thesis turns on pricing.

**4. PUE.** DCII has never published a number, in six annual reports, while claiming efficiency "better than internal design targets and national industry standards" and winning an award for a "Low-PUE" building. *Would decide:* roughly Rp30bn of gross profit per 0.1 of PUE `[calculated]`, and whether the Cibitung estate is competitive with new liquid-cooled builds. *Likely location:* the sustainability report of a customer, or an Uptime Institute filing.

**5. Rack density (kW/rack) and whether any DCII facility is actually liquid-cooled.** AR2025 names liquid cooling only as an industry requirement; JK6 is described as having "advanced cooling technologies and flexible power configurations"; the only cooling technology named for DCII's own estate is a **chilled water system**. External reporting describes JK6 as "AI-ready with liquid cooling". *Would decide:* whether DCII can serve GB200-class AI workloads at all, or only inference and enterprise at conventional density.

**6. The identity of the tenant that took all 36 MW of JK6.** *Would decide:* the concentration question — JK6 alone is 28% of reported capacity, and two customers are already 48% of revenue. The cloud on-ramp list (Google, AWS, Azure, Alibaba, Huawei, BytePlus) narrows it but names nobody.

**7. The Rp17 trillion facility's tenor, pricing, covenants and drawn amount**, and whether BCA and Bank Mandiri are co-lenders or two separate facilities. *Would decide:* the leverage path through the Rp10–15tn-a-year capex programme, and the covenant headroom at the point where DER stops being 0.47×. *Likely location:* the FY2026 annual report, or an IDX material-transaction disclosure.

**8. Why Rp500bn was placed in 2%-coupon Danantara paper.** *Would decide:* whether this is a collateral arrangement, a state-relationship cost, or something else entirely. *Likely location:* the FY2026 notes, if the position moves.

**9. Receivables concentration by customer, and why Rp127bn is more than 60 days overdue with no provision.** *Would decide:* whether the "no concentration of credit risk" assertion has any support.

**10. Capex per MW on the new build programme.** DCII discloses total capex but never per-MW, and the split between H1 fit-out, E2 and land is not given. Without it, the yield on the Rp10–15tn cannot be estimated at all.

**11. Interconnection / cross-connect revenue and count.** Bundled inside "Lain-lain" (Rp181,809m in FY2025) with flexspace, smart hands and the platform fees. Since interconnection density is the primary switching-cost moat in colocation, its absence removes the ability to assess the moat quantitatively.

**12. Contract structure:** weighted-average remaining term, escalators, take-or-pay minimums, churn, renewal rate, backlog. Disclosed once at IPO as "6 months to 5 years" across 97 agreements. Nothing since.

**13. Equipment purchase commitments and secured vendor slots.** With transformer lead times at 100–210 weeks and DC-spec switchgear at 2–3 years, an operator's forward equipment position is close to being the growth constraint. DCII discloses **no equipment commitment of any kind** — only the PT Sumaraja Indah civil-works POs (Rp36,880m outstanding on JK6).

**14. Power arrangements beyond Cibitung.** Cikarang Listrindo supplies H1 under a 2016 agreement with no tariff, volume or term disclosed. **No PLN agreement for any DCII site appears anywhere** — not in the filings and not in PLN's own publicity, which names Digital Edge, NeutraDC, BDx and Microsoft but not DCII. How E1, E2, H2 and any future Karawang expansion are powered is unknown.

**15. Bintan:** hectares owned versus optioned, capital committed, the JV ownership split with Gallant Venture, and the timeline. Currently a Forbes interview and a website page, with **zero presence in the audited accounts**.

**16. FY2025 directors' remuneration**, and the number of LTI phantom units granted and their carrying value. AR2024 disclosed Rp28,779m; AR2025 discloses neither.

**17. Han Arming Hanafia.** A named controller holding 14.11% with no board seat, no biography and no role disclosed in any of six annual reports or the prospectus.

**18. The composition of Q1 2025.** A 67.2% gross margin on Rp773,552m of revenue, unrepeated in any other quarter, driving a +193% net profit print that has distorted every subsequent year-on-year comparison. No disclosure explains it.

**19. Reconciliation of the four float numbers** — 22.36% register public, 18.55% IDX free float, ~3.81% circulating, 99.96% KSEI concentration. No filing reconciles them.

**20. Sources not supplied for this analysis:** no interim (quarterly or half-year) financial statements were provided, so FY2026 figures in this report come from public reporting rather than from filings; no public expose deck for 2022; no IDX monthly ownership filings; no Fitch rating report in full; and no Structure Research, Cushman & Wakefield or Knight Frank primary report — all market-sizing here is from their press summaries.

- **The Salim block does not fully reconcile:** the three founders' holdings fell by 286,939,503 shares while Salim received 265,033,461. The ~21.9m difference is unexplained.
