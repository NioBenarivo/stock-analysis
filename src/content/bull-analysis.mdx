**What it does:** owns oil, gas and product tankers and hires them out to whoever
needs a cargo moved. Also runs two floating oil-storage units and a small
ship-agency business. Head office Jakarta; ships trade Indonesian waters, the
Mediterranean, the Black Sea, the Arabian Gulf and East Asia.

**Listed:** IDX, ticker BULL, since 23 May 2011 (then named PT Buana Listya Tama Tbk).

**Market capitalisation:** roughly Rp 6.7–6.9 triliun at 4–7 September 2026,
share price around Rp 430–460, 15.49 billion shares out. The stock is up about
200% over the past year and still about 60% below its all-time high.

**A note on currency.** BULL keeps its books in **US dollars**, not rupiah, and
in **full dollars** rather than thousands or millions. Every figure in this
report is US dollars unless it carries a `Rp`. This is not a translation
choice — it is how the audited accounts are drawn up, and converting them would
introduce an exchange-rate error that is not in the source.

**Period covered:** FY2020 through FY2025, plus the 2011 flotation and the 2019
rights issue for history. FY2020 exists only as the comparative column inside the
FY2021 accounts.

**Sources in hand:** five annual reports (FY2021–FY2025), six public-expose
decks (2021–2025), the 2011 IPO prospectus and the 2019 rights-issue prospectus,
plus web research done 7 September 2026. Full list with page counts at the end.

**One caution before anything else:** the FY2025 financial statements reproduced
in the FY2025 annual report are marked **"Draft Report — 28 April 2026"** on
every page. The audit report inside them carries a blank reference number
(`______/____/AU.1/__/1642-_/1/__/2026`) and no signature — only typed names.
FY2025 figures in this report are therefore drawn from a document the company
itself labels a draft.

## THE ONE THING TO UNDERSTAND FIRST

Between the end of 2020 and the end of 2022, BULL's balance sheet shrank from
US$828 million to US$378 million. Slightly over half the company disappeared in
twenty-four months.

It did not disappear through a sale. The FY2022 accounts contain one line that
explains most of it: **US$187 million of debt was settled by "redelivery of the
vessels"**, and another US$26 million by handing over the shares of two
subsidiaries. In plain terms, BULL had bought ships with money borrowed from
finance companies, could not keep up the payments, and gave the ships back. The
lenders that had financed two of the vessel-owning subsidiaries — Fleetscape
Zantoro LLC and Fleetscape Sri Asih LLC — simply took the subsidiaries. Eight
ships went back to eight different creditors. About US$213 million of debt was
extinguished without a single dollar of cash changing hands.

The accounts present this as a series of asset disposals, with a loss on
disposal booked each year. They never present it as what it was, and they never
state a gain or loss on getting out of the debt. There is no note reconciling
which creditor got which vessel for what value.

That event set everything else in this report. The fleet that left was the
contracted fleet — ships on time charter, where a customer pays a fixed daily
hire for months or years at a stretch and covers fuel and port costs itself.
Revenue from that kind of work fell from US$130.5 million in 2020 to US$2.7
million in 2025. What replaced it is voyage work: BULL takes a cargo from A to
B, is paid for the cargo, and pays for the fuel and the port charges out of its
own pocket. That went from 19% of revenue to 95%.

So the company you are looking at in 2026 is not a smaller version of the
company of 2020. It is a different business. The old one was a fleet of
Indonesian-flag ships on multi-year contracts to Pertamina, protected by a law
that reserves domestic sea trade for domestic owners. The new one is a handful
of ships trading spot cargoes in the Mediterranean, the Black Sea and the Gulf,
whose largest customers have been Dubai trading houses that leave almost no
public record — and one of which, Voliton DMCC, matches the name and profile of
a company designated by the US Treasury in December 2023 for carrying Russian
oil (§10.2 sets out exactly how far that identification can and cannot be taken).
Pertamina, which was 38% of revenue in 2020, was 4% in 2025.

Three things follow from that, and they run through the whole report:

1. **The auditor has qualified his opinion five years running** and attached a
   going-concern warning to every one of them. Two grounds recur: debt shown as
   long-term when the company admits it has broken the terms that make it
   immediately repayable, and an inability — since the FY2022 audit — to get any
   confirmation from five finance companies about a balance BULL says they owe it.
2. **The largest single item on the 2025 balance sheet after the ships is
   US$81 million of money paid away in advance** for two businesses in the
   Seychelles and the UAE that have no traceable existence. That is 22% of
   everything the company owns, against equity of US$203 million.
3. **The company has stopped saying how many ships it has.** No vessel count, no
   average age and no breakdown by type has appeared in any filing for 2022, 2023,
   2024 or 2025, and tonnage only as the loose phrases ">1.3m DWT" and
   ">700,000 DWT" in the decks. The last annual report to carry a
   fleet list was FY2021. Its own website lists twelve ship names; trade press in
   mid-2026 says eleven ships, against thirty-seven at the end of 2020.

Meanwhile the shares have roughly tripled in a year, because tanker freight
rates in 2026 are extraordinary — the class of ship BULL runs earned about
US$29,400 a day on average in 2025 and about US$92,400 a day in the second
quarter of 2026. Both things are true at once: the market for what BULL does has
never been better, and the disclosure about what BULL actually owns has never
been thinner.

Evidence for each of these sits in §07 (the numbers), §08 (the audit opinion,
the ship valuations, the advances) and §10 (the red flags).

Two features of that picture matter more than the percentages.

**The controlling family has never held a majority.** PT Delta Royal Sejahtera
has topped out at just under 20% and has been diluted to 17.4% by successive
share issues. It reaches control through the board rather than through votes:
Halim Jusuf has been president commissioner every year since 2016, his son Henry
Jusuf has been president director since April 2021, and the annual reports state
from FY2022 onward that both have "an affiliated relationship with the
Controlling and Major Shareholders". Counting shares held indirectly by everyone
on both boards, the FY2025 report puts board-connected ownership at 23.8%.

**Nearly four in five subsidiaries do nothing.** The FY2025 accounts list 41
group companies. **Thirty-one of them are described as dormant.** That is not a
recent tidy-up gone wrong; the dormant count has climbed steadily as the ships
were handed back — 14 dormant in 2020, roughly 26 by 2022, 31 by 2025. Each
vessel was held in its own single-ship company, a normal arrangement in shipping
because it ring-fences one ship's liabilities from the rest. When the ships left,
the companies stayed on the register as empty boxes.

Some of those "dormant" boxes are not empty. At 31 December 2025 the accounts
show BULL International Shipping Pte Ltd holding US$51.5 million of assets,
Swordfish Maritime Ltd US$57.3 million, Thundercat Maritime Ltd US$37.1 million
and PT Anjasmoro Maritime US$36.8 million — the last classified simply as
"Investment". Swordfish and Anjasmoro are the two entities through which the
US$81 million of advances discussed in §2.3 and §10.1 were paid. A company
described as dormant that between them hold nearly half of everything the group
owns are doing something; the accounts do not say what.

There is one subsidiary BULL does not fully own. **PT Banyu Laju Shipping** is
40% held and consolidated anyway, on the argument that BULL controls it in
practice. The other 60% belongs to two parties who **have never been named in
any filing in any year**. That company paid US$3.3 million and then US$4.3
million of dividends to those unnamed holders in 2020 and 2021 — the only
dividends anyone connected to BULL has received in the whole period — in a year
in which it lost US$4.5 million and disposed of its entire fleet. It has reported
zero revenue every year since.

## 1.3 History — recap

Read as a whole, the twenty-one years show one pattern repeating.

**The company has never generated its own growth capital.** Every expansion has
been paid for by issuing shares or by borrowing, and it has issued shares in
2011, 2015, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2021, 2022 and 2025 — eight times in fourteen
years, four of them private placements to a single buyer with no rights offered
to existing holders. It has never paid a dividend. Total capital raised from
shareholders since flotation runs to well over Rp 2 triliun. Accumulated losses
at the end of 2025 stand at US$218 million.

**It was built to serve its parent, and the flotation was the parent's exit.**
Seven of every ten rupiah the public paid in 2011 went straight back to BLTA to
retire an interest-free loan, which BLTA had 120 days to apply against its own
bank debt. BULL's ships were pledged for BLTA's borrowings while this happened.
BLTA defaulted the following year, and by 2013 BULL was selling ships into its
former parent's restructuring.

**The 2019–2020 expansion was the mistake that everything since has been paying
for.** Twenty-one ships were bought in two years — eight in 2019 and thirteen in
2020 — with US$249 million of cash capex in 2020 alone, funded largely by
non-bank finance companies at rates the accounts never disclose beyond "a certain
percentage per annum". Freight rates then fell. The ships that were bought at the
top were handed back at the bottom.

**Each shrinkage has been presented as strategy.** The FY2022 annual report
describes the year as diversifying revenue "by maximising international spot
income while balancing domestic contract revenue". What happened is that the
contracted fleet was repossessed and only the spot ships were left.

## 1.4 Owner's character

The filings do not describe the owners. But the record of what they have decided
over five years does, and four patterns are visible in it.

**They keep the company funded by selling shares to new buyers rather than by
distributing to existing ones.** Three placements without pre-emptive rights —
2021, 2022 and 2025 — put 2.59 billion new shares into single hands at Rp 280 (or
Rp 220; two notes in the same accounts disagree), Rp 131 and Rp 180. Existing
shareholders were not offered the chance to keep their proportion. In each case
the new holder is either not identified beyond a name or, in the 2025 case,
identified only as **Fortune Street Limited**, of which nothing further is
disclosed and nothing can be found. Meanwhile Delta Royal has never put more
money in: its shareholding has been diluted from 19.8% to 17.4% by these issues.

**They will pledge their own assets rather than inject cash.** To keep the bank
lines open, PT Delta Royal Sejahtera has pledged 394,790,094 of its BULL shares
to Bank Sinarmas. Land owned by "entities related to the majority shareholders",
never named, secures the same facility; more land, and from 2025 land owned by an
unnamed individual, secures the Bank Oke facility; a corporate guarantee from PT
Prima Mustika Chandra and "a personal guarantee" from someone the accounts never
identify were added in the 2022 restructuring. **No fee, interest or
consideration of any kind for any of this has ever been disclosed.**

Every going-concern note from FY2022 onward lists, among the reasons the company
will survive, that it has "obtained financial support from the ultimate
shareholder." That support is never quantified, never dated, and appears nowhere
in the cash flow statement or the balance sheet.

**They shed independent oversight during the worst years.** Between April 2022
and August 2023 the board of commissioners lost Achmad Widjaja, Andreas Kastono
Ahadi, Mohamad Prapanca and Dwi Wahyu Daryoto — including three of the four
people who held the independent-commissioner seats in that window. It shrank from
four members to three. From 21 August 2023 a **single independent commissioner**,
M. Harry Santoso, has been the only independent voice on the board and has
simultaneously chaired the audit committee — the committee whose job is to face
down the auditor who has qualified the accounts five years running. **No reason
has ever been given for any departure, in any year.**

In July 2025 Achmad Widjaja returned to the board — but as an ordinary
commissioner, not an independent one. He is also president director of PT Fajar
Asia Selaras, the intermediary named in the December 2022 agreement through which
the first US$24.3 million advance was paid, and was until 2025 president director
of PT Prima Mustika Chandra, the entity that guaranteed the Sinarmas
restructuring. A man with a foot in two of the transactions this report flags now
sits on the board and on its nomination and remuneration committee.

**They pay themselves modestly and disclose nothing about who gets what.** Total
board pay fell from US$1.36 million in 2021 to about US$0.60 million in 2024 and
2025 — under half a per cent of revenue, and low by the standards of a listed
Indonesian company of this size. It is disclosed as two aggregate lines,
commissioners and directors, with **no individual figure in any year**.

The fair reading is of owners who are conserving cash and personal capital while
using their own collateral to hold the banks off, and who have thinned the
governance around themselves at the same time. Whether that is prudence or
something else is not something the filings settle.

## 1.5 RPT — dealings with the owners and their other companies

Related-party transactions are dealings between the company and people or
businesses connected to its owners or managers. They matter because the price in
such a deal is not set by a market.

BULL's related-party note is remarkable for what it says: **almost nothing
happens.** In every year from 2020 to 2025 the note states that all revenue is
from third parties, there are no purchases from related parties, and there are no
related-party receivables, payables or loans. The only recurring item is board
pay.

| Related-party revenue | nil | nil | nil | nil | nil | nil |

On the face of it, a clean sheet. Two things complicate it.

**The collateral is the real related-party transaction, and it is not priced.**
The owners' shares and the owners' associates' land stand behind the company's
bank debt. In a market deal, a guarantor charges for that. Here nothing is
charged, nothing is disclosed, and nothing appears in the accounts. It is
free support flowing from the owners to the company — which is generous, but it
also means the company's cost of borrowing is understated relative to a
standalone business, and the owners hold a claim over the company that is
nowhere on the balance sheet. If the bank ever calls that collateral, the
owners' recourse against the company is undefined.

**The unnamed counterparties may or may not be third parties — and no reader can
check.** The note's claim that every counterparty is unrelated is only as good as
the company's own identification of them. In 2024, 55% of revenue came from
Blackhill DMCC, a company with no traceable existence anywhere. In 2025, half the
receivable book sat with Solisar Freight and Transport FZE and Galleon Tankers
FZE, neither of which appears in any revenue note or has any public record. The
US$81 million of advances went to Pacrim Maritime Investments Ltd of the
Seychelles, likewise untraceable, through an intermediary whose president
director now sits on BULL's board. Each of these is stated to be a third party.
None can be verified as one. That does not make them related parties — it makes
the disclosure unfalsifiable, which for this purpose is nearly as unhelpful.

# 02 — MODEL

BULL runs one business that matters and two that barely register. The accounts
call the big one "Oil, FPSO and FSO"; it was 96% of revenue in 2025. The gas
segment is now a rounding error, and ship agency is a small service business.
Each is set out below in the same order: what is sold, the physical chain behind
it, what the company owns, what it costs to operate, and how it gets paid.

## 2.1 Oil tankers and floating storage — 96% of revenue

### Product — what is actually being sold

A tanker owner sells the use of a hull for a period of time or for a journey.
Which of the two it sells changes almost everything about the economics, so it is
worth being precise about the difference.

Under a **time charter**, the customer hires the ship for a stretch of months or
years and pays a fixed amount per day. The customer decides where it goes and
pays for the fuel and the port fees; the owner provides the ship, the crew, the
insurance and the maintenance. The owner's revenue is steady and its costs are
largely fixed, so a time charter is close to renting out a building.

Under a **voyage charter** — the accounts call the revenue from this "freight" —
the customer pays for one cargo to be moved from one port to another. The owner
decides nothing about the cargo but bears the fuel and the port charges out of
the freight it collects. Revenue swings with the market and with the oil price,
because fuel is the owner's cost.

The third product is a **floating storage unit**: a tanker permanently moored at
an offshore oil field, taking the field's output and holding it until a shuttle
tanker collects it. Some also process the crude on board, in which case they are
called production units. These sit on very long contracts with the field
operator, and they earn a daily rate for years without moving.

BULL's mix has inverted completely:

from single cargoes at whatever the market pays that week.

**A gap worth naming.** "Oil, FPSO and FSO" is a reportable segment in every
single year, and the company sold FPSO Brotojoyo in 2024. Yet **no revenue line
for floating storage appears in any revenue note in any of the six years.** How
much the storage units earn, and therefore how much of BULL's revenue is
long-contracted rather than spot, cannot be determined from the accounts.

That last branch is not hypothetical for BULL. It is the mechanism by which
half the company disappeared, and it is why the single-ship-subsidiary structure
matters here more than it usually does: handing over the shares of Zantoro
Maritime Ltd and Sri Asih Maritime Ltd to Fleetscape in January 2022 transferred
the ships without any ship ever being sold.

# 03 — DEMAND

Demand for a tanker is not demand for oil. It is demand for **oil moved a long
way by sea**. The industry measure is the tonne-mile: one tonne of cargo carried
one nautical mile. A world that consumes exactly the same amount of oil but sources
it from further away needs more ships, and that is precisely what happened after
2022.

**The global picture.** Two forces have driven tanker demand since 2022, and both
are political rather than economic.

The first is the redrawing of the crude map after the invasion of Ukraine. Russian
crude that used to travel a few hundred miles from Baltic ports to refineries in
north-west Europe now travels to India and China — a journey ten to fifteen times
longer. The oil is the same; the shipping required to move it is several times
greater. Western sanctions and the price cap pushed that trade out of the
mainstream fleet and into a separate pool of older, often anonymously owned
vessels that trade outside the western insurance and finance system. That pool is
usually called the shadow fleet, and it absorbed several hundred hulls, tightening
the supply available to everybody else.

The second is the Red Sea. Attacks on shipping from late 2023 pushed traffic away
from the Suez Canal and around the Cape of Good Hope, adding roughly ten days to a
Gulf-to-Europe voyage. Again: same cargo, more ship-days consumed.

By 2026 a third has been added — disruption around the Strait of Hormuz, where war
risk premiums on Gulf transits have gone from a fraction of a per cent of a ship's
value to several per cent. That is what has taken 2026 rates from good to
extraordinary.

**Indonesia.** BULL's home market is protected but not growing in the way that
helps it. Domestic sea freight volumes have risen steadily — 320.7 million tonnes
in 2022, 351.0 million in 2023, 379.2 million in 2024 and 508.4 million tonnes in
2025 on the company's own reporting of official data. But Indonesian crude
production is falling, not rising: crude lifting was 605,500 barrels a day in 2023,
down 1.1%, against a national target that has been missed repeatedly. The country
imports more refined product each year and produces less of its own crude, which
shifts the domestic tanker task from moving crude between Indonesian fields and
refineries toward moving imported product from Singapore and the Gulf.

The demand that matters to BULL is therefore split in two, and the two halves are
moving in opposite directions:

| | Domestic (cabotage-protected) | International (spot) |
|---|---|---|
| Who buys | Pertamina and its shipping arm PIS, almost exclusively | Oil traders, refiners, anyone with a cargo |
| Contract type | Time charter, 1–5 years, tendered | Voyage by voyage |
| Pricing | Tender-determined, stable | Market-determined, volatile |
| BULL's share of revenue, 2020 | 38% (Pertamina alone) | — |
| BULL's share of revenue, 2025 | **4%** | **96%** |
| Direction of travel | Pertamina is building its own fleet | Rates at multi-decade highs |

**A structural point about the domestic half.** Cabotage guarantees that Indonesian
coastal trade goes to Indonesian-flag ships, which sounds like a moat. But the
buyer behind almost all of that trade is one entity — Pertamina, through PT
Pertamina International Shipping — and that entity is simultaneously building an
owned fleet of its own. A protected market with a single buyer who is insourcing
is not a comfortable place to sit. It is worth adding that in July 2025 the
Indonesian Attorney General named suspects in a large corruption case covering
Pertamina's fuel trade, and one of the allegations is the **inflating of shipping
costs on Pertamina cargoes**. BULL is not named in any account of it. But the
pricing mechanism in the market BULL used to depend on is under criminal
investigation, which is context for why domestic contract work may have become
harder to win on acceptable terms.

# 04 — SUPPLY

Supply in tanker shipping means hulls. It moves slowly, because a ship ordered
today arrives in two to three years, and it is the variable that ends every boom.

**The orderbook has gone from empty to full.** This is the single most important
supply fact for anyone looking at BULL today.

| | mid-2024 | July 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Total tanker orderbook | VLCC 7%, Suezmax 14%, Aframax/LR2 15% of fleet | **1,267 ships / 173.3m dwt = 20.1% of the fleet by count, 25.0% by tonnage** |
| Scrapping | minimal | 21 ships / 2.07m dwt in the first half of 2026 — very low |
| Fleet growth, 1H 2026 | — | +167 ships / 17.1m dwt |

Two years ago the industry's defence was that nobody had ordered ships during the
downturn, so a decade of under-investment would keep the fleet tight however good
demand was. That defence is gone. A quarter of the existing fleet by tonnage is on
order, and 2026 has seen record crude-tanker contracting. Those ships arrive from
2027 through 2029.

**Old ships are worth extraordinary money, which is the second half of the same
story.** A twenty-year-old VLCC is currently worth about US$71 million against a
scrap value of about US$21 million — up 90% in a year. Old tonnage is being bid up
because the sanctioned and grey trades need hulls that nobody minds losing. That
is directly relevant to BULL, whose ships were 17.2 years old on average the last
time it said, and are older now.

**The shadow fleet is starting to unwind, and that adds supply.** Kpler's reporting
is that roughly 350 vessels which had been serving Iranian and Venezuelan trade
lost that work as Venezuela came out of the sanctioned network, and those hulls are
now competing for mainstream cargoes again. Old ships coming back into the
mainstream market is more competition for exactly the ships BULL owns.

# 05 — KEY VARIABLES

Five things move this business. They are listed with what each is worth, so far
as the disclosure allows the sum to be done.

**1. The Aframax / mid-size spot rate.** With 95% of revenue now earned voyage by
voyage, this is the whole revenue line. The published market average for BULL's
class went from US$11,800 a day in 2021 to US$46,800 in 2023 to US$29,400 in 2025
and US$71,700 in the first half of 2026. BULL does not disclose its own realised
rate, its ship count or its days at sea, so the sensitivity cannot be computed
exactly from the accounts. Working backwards from the FY2025 numbers on an
assumed eleven-ship fleet at 340 revenue days each, every US$1,000 a day of
achieved rate is worth roughly **US$3.7 million of revenue and, since the marginal
cost of a rate change is nil, essentially US$3.7 million of profit** — about 15%
of FY2025's reported profit. The fleet-count assumption is the weak link; see
§8.5 for the working.

**2. The bunker fuel price.** Fuel is 29.6% of direct costs and is entirely BULL's
own cost now that time charters have gone. It ran US$31.1 million in 2025 on
US$145.4 million of revenue. A 10% move in fuel prices is about **US$3.1 million**
of operating profit, roughly 13% of the FY2025 result — and the exposure is
uncovered, because the accounts state in every year that the group does no hedging
of any kind, on fuel, currency or interest rates.

**3. Port charges.** At 24.7% of direct costs and US$26.0 million a year, these
have grown faster than fuel — up 69% since 2023 while revenue slightly fell. Port costs
scale with the number of port calls, so a rise of this size on flat revenue points
to shorter voyages with more calls, or higher tariffs in the ports BULL now uses.
The accounts do not break the line down or explain the growth.

**4. Interest rates and the debt balance.** Borrowings have fallen from US$435
million to US$115 million, and the interest bill with them: US$46.8 million in
2021 against US$10.0 million in 2025. The company's own disclosed sensitivity is
that a half-point move in rates is worth **US$619,745** of pre-tax profit. That is
small now; it was US$1.84 million in 2020. The deleveraging is the single largest
positive contributor to reported profit over the period — a US$36.8 million
reduction in the annual interest bill, larger than any year's profit.

**5. The value put on the ships.** Not an operating variable, but it moves the
accounts more than several of the above. The ships are carried at a valuer's
estimate rather than at cost, and the swings have been enormous: minus US$102.2
million in 2021, plus US$59.9 million in 2022, nothing at all in 2023 and 2024,
plus US$2.8 million in 2025. In 2022 US$14.1 million of that uplift went straight
through the profit and loss account, reducing the reported loss. And the loan
covenants are written against those same valuations, so the valuer's number
determines both the balance sheet and whether the company is in breach. §8.2 sets
out how stale those valuations have been.

**What is not on this list, and should be.** Fleet size and utilisation are the
two variables every shipowner is judged on, and neither can be tracked because
neither is disclosed after 2021. Any model of this company is a model of a fleet
whose size is inferred from trade press.

# 06 — VICISSITUDES

Tanker shipping is among the most cyclical businesses there is, because supply
takes three years to arrive and demand can change in a week. BULL has been through
three distinct shocks in six years, and how it handled each says more about the
company than any ratio.

**Shock 1 — the 2021 rate collapse, and the response was to write down and sell.**
Rates for BULL's class fell to US$11,800 a day, a level at which a leveraged
owner cannot service its debt. The company took a US$102.8 million write-down of
its ships, sold ten of them at a US$55.0 million loss, wrote off US$25.6 million
of goodwill attached to those ships, and redeemed a fund investment at a US$42.8
million loss. Net loss for the year: US$230.9 million, against equity that started
the year at US$350 million. Equity ended at US$111 million.

**Shock 2 — the 2022 default, and the response was to hand over the fleet.** With
US$217 million owed to non-bank finance companies and rates still weak, BULL
settled by giving the ships back. Eight lessors took eight vessels; two took
whole subsidiaries. The accounts book it as disposals; the effect is a
restructuring. Assets fell 37% in a year. Notably, the company also revalued its
remaining ships **upward** by US$59.9 million in the same year, of which US$14.1
million went through the profit and loss account — so equity actually rose,
despite a US$43.4 million loss.

**Shock 3 — the disclosure withdrawal, from 2022 onward.** This is not a market
shock but it is the company's most consistent response to stress. Fleet counts,
tonnage, ages, ship names, utilisation — all present in 2020 and 2021, all gone
from 2022. Governance thinned at the same time: three independent commissioners
resigned in eighteen months and the board shrank.

**What a repeat would do.** The arithmetic is different now, and better in one
respect and worse in another.

Better: debt is US$115 million against US$435 million in 2020, and net debt to
equity is 54% against 363% at the 2021 low. A rate collapse would not put the
company straight back into the hands of its lenders.

Worse: the business is now almost entirely spot. In 2020, two-thirds of revenue
was contracted and would have kept coming for months after rates turned. Today
95% of revenue reprices immediately. Applying 2021's Aframax rate of US$11,800 a
day to a business whose 2025 revenue was earned at roughly US$29,400 a day
suggests revenue would fall by roughly half to two-thirds within a couple of
quarters — the range reflects the gap between the market average and the roughly
US$23,600 a ship-day BULL's own numbers imply (§8.5) — while fuel,
port, crew and depreciation costs of roughly US$105 million would fall only with
voyages actually stopped. The company would be loss-making within two quarters and
back into a working-capital hole almost as fast.

And the working-capital hole never closed. Current liabilities have exceeded
current assets in every single year since 2021 — by US$139.8 million that year, and
still by US$55.1 million at the end of 2025, on the company's own classification.
On the auditor's classification, which moves the debt whose covenants are breached
into the current column, the 2025 deficit is the same US$55.1 million but the 2024
deficit was US$112.2 million. This is a company that has not had a comfortable
balance sheet at any point in the window.

## 8.1 The audit opinion, year by year

An auditor's report normally says the accounts give a true and fair view, full
stop. That is a clean opinion. When the auditor says "except for" something, the
opinion is **qualified** — he is telling you there is a specific part of the
accounts he does not stand behind. When he adds a **going-concern** paragraph, he
is saying there is real doubt the company can keep operating for another year.

BULL has had both, every year, for five years.

**One further gap: nobody knows who audited FY2020.** The FY2021 report contains

## 8.2 How the ships are valued, and how stale the valuations have been

BULL does not carry its ships at what it paid minus depreciation. It carries them
at a professional valuer's estimate of market value, updated from time to time, a
method the standards permit. It matters here for three reasons: the estimate has
swung by more than US$100 million in a single year; part of the swing goes
straight into the profit line; and the loan covenants are written against the
same values.

| Year end | Valuation date actually used | Valuer | Report signed | How stale |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2020 | 31 Dec 2020 (older hulls: 31 Dec 2019) | Herman Meirizki & Rekan | 2 Jun 2021 | 5 months |
| 2021 | 31 Dec 2020 / 2019; **30 Jun 2021** for later purchases | Herman Meirizki & Rekan | 1 Oct 2021 | **no valuation at the balance-sheet date** |

Two years' balance sheets — 2023 and 2024 — carried the fleet at a value struck

assets, which is the conservative direction; but it also means the covenant test
of collateral value against loan outstanding was being run on numbers two years
old, and the accounts never state which direction that cut.

The valuer has changed twice in four years. Three different firms in six years,
each arriving in a year with a large valuation movement, is a pattern worth
noting even though each is licensed and each used standard methods.

The accounting rule is that increases go to a reserve unless they reverse an

never subjected to a value-in-use test in any filing, in any year, including 2021
when US$102.8 million came off them. The revaluation model substitutes for it.

## 8.4 Goodwill: US$33 million that has never moved

Goodwill is what a buyer pays above the value of the identifiable assets it
acquires. It sits on the balance sheet until an annual test says it is no longer
supported, at which point it must be written off.

BULL carries **US$33,017,000** of it, unchanged since 2021, arising on two
Indonesian vessel-owning subsidiaries — PT Nusa Bhakti Jayaraya and PT Mahameru
Nusa Mentari. It has survived a US$231 million loss year, the surrender of the
fleet, and the disappearance of the customers.

The test is a forecast: project the subsidiary's future cash, discount it, and
see whether it covers the goodwill. The assumptions BULL has fed into that
forecast have moved a great deal:

| Assumption | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| NBJ assumed operating margin | 48.85% | 26.40% | 46.39% | 55.71% | 43.78% | 47.68% |
| MNM assumed long-run growth rate | 1.00% | 1.00% | 3.80% | **46.91%** | 6.46% | 1.89% |

A terminal growth rate is the pace at which a business is assumed to grow for
ever after the forecast period. Economies grow at 2–5%. **A 46.91% perpetual
growth assumption, used in FY2023, is not a forecast of anything.** It is the
number required for the test to pass. It reverted to 6.46% the following year and
1.89% the year after, and no impairment was recognised in any of them.

The only goodwill ever written off was the US$25.6 million in 2021 that attached
to ships that were sold — a mechanical derecognition, not a judgement that the
goodwill was impaired.

## 8.5 Unit economics — what one ship earns, and how uncertain that is

BULL discloses no fleet count, no deadweight, no operating days and no achieved
rate. Everything below is therefore an estimate built on an assumed fleet, and it
should be read as an order of magnitude rather than a measurement. The assumption
is stated at each step.

**Step 1 — how many ships.** The audited accounts say nothing. BULL's website
lists twelve names. Indonesian trade press in July 2026 says eleven, with a target
of twenty-one. The FY2025 accounts describe eight subsidiaries as vessel owners.
**Assume eleven revenue-earning vessels through 2025.**

**Step 2 — revenue per ship.** US$145,379,945 ÷ 11 = **US$13.2 million per ship
per year** `[calculated]`.

**Step 3 — implied day rate.** A tanker is available roughly 340 days a year after
dry-docking and repairs. US$13.2 million ÷ 340 = **about US$38,900 per ship per
day of gross freight** `[calculated]`. But gross freight on a voyage charter
includes the fuel and port costs the owner pays. Stripping those out — US$31.1
million of fuel and US$26.0 million of port charges, US$57.1 million between them
— leaves US$88.3 million, or **about US$23,600 per ship-day** on the industry's
comparable measure `[calculated]`.

**Step 4 — sanity check against the market.** The published average for
Aframax/LR2 in 2025 was US$29,400 a day. BULL's implied US$23,600 is roughly 20%
below that. Three explanations are equally consistent with the disclosure: the
fleet is more than eleven ships, so the per-ship figure is overstated; the ships
are smaller than Aframax, which would put the comparison against the MR average of
US$26,374 and make BULL's number close to market; or BULL genuinely earns a
discount. The accounts do not allow a choice between them.

**Step 5 — what falls out per ship.**

|---|---:|
| Revenue | 13,216,359 |
| Less fuel | (2,827,974) |
| Less port charges | (2,361,068) |
| Less crew | (719,749) |
| Less operating expenses, insurance, lubricants, transport, other | (1,996,810) |

The operating-margin line is the check that the rest is right: US$3,664,392 times

**Step 6 — does that earn its cost of capital?** Net vessel carrying value at the
end of 2025 was US$197.3 million, or about **US$17.9 million a ship**. The

Those are respectable numbers, and they are the answer to the obvious question
about a company carrying this many warning signs: **the ships themselves work.**
At 2025 rates they earn about a fifth of their carrying value before financing,
and interest and head-office costs take roughly 44% of that before it reaches a
shareholder. The problem in this file is not that the trade is unprofitable.

quarter of 2026 against US$29,400 for the whole of 2025. If BULL achieves even
half of that improvement across its fleet for a full year, the same eleven ships
would earn roughly US$120 million more of gross freight than in 2025, against
essentially unchanged crew, insurance and depreciation, and higher fuel only to
the extent it sails more. That is what the market is pricing, and it is why
management's 2026 projection of US$229 million of revenue and US$85.2 million of
profit — 57% and 250% above 2025 — is not on its face absurd, even though the
same management has missed its own profit target in every year it has published
one (§9.5).

# 09 — IMPORTANT TRANSACTIONS

## 9.1 The 2011 flotation — a parent's exit dressed as a growth story

BULL raised about Rp 1.03 triliun from the public in May 2011 on a prospectus
built around Indonesia's cabotage law, offshore expansion and gas. **Sixty-nine
and a half per cent of the money went straight back to PT Berlian Laju Tanker** to
repay an interest-free, undated loan of Rp 677 billion, which BLTA had 120 days to
apply against its own bank debt, after which it would release the mortgages it
held over BULL's ships. Only 21.3% was earmarked for the fleet growth the
prospectus spent its pages describing.

BLTA defaulted the following year. By 2013 BULL was selling ships into its former
parent's court restructuring, and by 2014 BLTA had gone from the register
entirely.

The mechanism by which the business had been assembled is worth recording because
it is not visible in any later filing. Four BLTA subsidiaries were transferred in
under common control and accounted for by pooling of interests — meaning the
difference between what was paid and book value went straight to equity, showing
as Rp (15,335) million, with **no consideration for the four companies stated
anywhere**. Eleven named vessels were bought with money BLTA borrowed from six
Indonesian banks between 2006 and 2010 and on-lent at 0%. Six more ships were
bareboat-chartered in from BLTA at US$634,000 a month. BULL, in other words, was
never an independent business that floated; it was a container for BLTA's domestic
fleet and its debt, and the IPO was how the debt was settled.

## 9.6 The 2026 refinancing and the LNG pivot

Since the FY2025 year-end the company has:

- taken a **US$24 million** five-year facility from Bank Sinarmas (13 March 2026)
  secured on land owned by entities related to the majority shareholder **and on
  shares of the company itself**;
- taken a **Rp 336 billion** five-year facility from Bank Mandiri (13 March 2026)
  secured on one vessel and a corporate guarantee — Mandiri is a new lender and a
  state bank;
- repaid the old Sinarmas facility in full (31 March 2026) and the Bank Oke fixed
  loan (1 April 2026);
- **completed the US$58.8 million purchase of Offshore Maritime Holdings from
  Pacrim** (27 March 2026), while stating it is "evaluating the necessary action
  to comply with the applicable terms and regulation" for it;
- taken delivery of its first LNG carrier and declared four strategic pillars —
  crude and product tankers, LNG, floating production and storage, and floating
  regasification — with the last two not expected to contribute revenue until
  **2029**.

A state bank lending against a single vessel, on a five-year term, to a company
with five consecutive qualified opinions is a meaningful vote of confidence in
the current rate environment. Whether it survives a downturn is a different
question.

# 10 — RED FLAG

Nine items, ranked by how much each would change the analysis if it turned out
badly. For each: what the evidence is, and where the money goes.

## 10.1 US$81 million paid to counterparties that do not exist in any public record

**The evidence.** Set out in full in §8.3. US$81,012,000 — 22.0% of total assets,
40% of equity — sits as advances to Pacrim Maritime Investments Ltd (Seychelles),
for two targets described identically as "engaged in investing in shipping
companies". The same money has been relabelled three times in three years, each
time in the final days of December, each time after the previous agreement was
cancelled. Pacrim is simultaneously seller in one deal and escrow agent in the
other. The auditor gave it a key audit matter in FY2024 and an emphasis of matter
in FY2025.

**Where the money goes.** Out of the group, to a Seychelles company, via an
intermediary whose president director now sits on BULL's board. US$58.8 million of
it converted into an acquisition in March 2026 that the company itself says may
not yet comply with applicable regulation.

**What would settle it.** Singapore's company registry would name the directors
and shareholders of Offshore Maritime Holdings Pte Ltd for a small fee. That is
the single highest-value piece of research an investor in this name could do.

## 10.2 A sanctioned entity's name among the customers

**The evidence.** BULL's largest customer in FY2023 was **Voliton DMCC**, which
paid US$60,620,318 — 40.9% of that year's revenue. On **20 December 2023**, eleven
days before that year closed, the US Treasury designated a company of exactly that
name:

> "VOLITON DMCC (a.k.a. PETROKIM TRADING MIDDLE EAST AND ASIA DMCC), Unit No:
> R29-33, Reef Tower, Plot No: JLT-PH2-O1A, Jumeirah Lakes Towers, Dubai, United
> Arab Emirates; Organization Established Date 22 May 2018; License DMCC-476388
> (United Arab Emirates); Registration Number 124420 (United Arab Emirates)
> [RUSSIA-EO14024]."

The designation is under Executive Order 14024, for operating in the marine sector
of the Russian economy; Treasury's release says the company "has sharply increased
its share of the trade of Russian oil since the price cap policy was implemented."
It is also listed by the UK, EU, Canada, Switzerland, Australia, New Zealand and
Japan. In FY2024, BULL's revenue from Voliton fell to US$89,618.

**How far this can be taken, and no further.** There is only one Voliton DMCC in
any public registry, the name is coined rather than generic, and DMCC is a single
Dubai free zone — so a second unrelated company of the same name in the same free
zone is improbable. That is the whole of the argument. **No BULL filing gives the
counterparty's registration number, licence or address**, so nothing ties BULL's
customer to registration 124420. The identification is probable, not established.
BULL has never published a word about sanctions screening, OFAC, or the identity
of its Dubai counterparties, in any filing or announcement, in any language.

**Where the money goes.** Not out — in. This is a revenue counterparty, and the
question is not whether BULL lost money but whether the revenue was lawfully
earned and whether the banking relationships that carry it are exposed.

**What would settle it.** BULL disclosing the counterparty's UAE licence number,
or the board confirming the identity. Failing that, a DMCC registry extract.

## 10.3 Two more customers, and two more debtors, with no traceable existence

**The evidence.** Blackhill DMCC was **55.3% of FY2024 revenue — US$77,560,995 —
and 24.3% of the receivable book**, and has no corporate record, no sanctions
listing and no trace in any open source. Eastway Expedition FZE was 10% of FY2024
revenue and 26.8% of receivables: same, nothing. At the end of 2025, **Solisar
Freight and Transport FZE (24.6%) and Galleon Tankers FZE (24.5%) together owe
BULL half its receivable book** and appear in no revenue note in any year: same,
nothing.

**The pattern.** Five UAE free-zone counterparties in three years, each dominating
either revenue or receivables for a year or two and then vanishing, none of which
can be verified as an operating business. In 2025, 87.7% of revenue came from
parties BULL does not name at all.

**Where the money goes.** Nowhere yet — these are debtors. But a receivable book
half-owed by two unverifiable free-zone companies is a collection risk that no
loss allowance in these accounts reflects.

## 10.4 Debt shown as long-term for three years when the company knew it was not

**The evidence.** §8.1. Understatement of near-term obligations of US$102.3
million, US$120.4 million, US$88.1 million and US$70.7 million in the FY2021 to
FY2024 accounts respectively, on the auditor's own figures. Corrected only in the
FY2025 accounts, with the auditor stating that his opinion on the prior year has
changed.

**Why it is a red flag rather than an accounting quibble.** A reader of the FY2023
report saw a US$36.6 million working-capital shortfall; the true figure on the
auditor's view was US$124.7 million. That is not a presentational preference. It
is the difference between a company that is tight and one that is dependent on its
lenders' forbearance, and it was the company's own choice for four consecutive
years against a stated audit qualification.

## 10.5 A receivable from five creditors that nobody will confirm

**The evidence.** US$7,968,000 at the end of 2025 (US$9,929,376 for the two years
before), claimed against Uranus, Lavies, Beta, Alpha and Eris, arising from the
2022 vessel handovers. **No confirmation has been obtained from any of the five
since the FY2022 audit** — four consecutive years — and there is an acknowledged
unreconciled difference of US$4,140,325.

**Where the money goes.** It does not go anywhere. It sits, rated "low risk of
default" with no provision, four years after the counterparties stopped
responding. Three of the nine 2022 settlement agreements were struck on balances
that were never reconciled, which means the amounts owed either way rest on BULL's
own arithmetic alone.

## 10.6 A company that will not say how many ships it owns

**The evidence.** §2.1. No vessel count, tonnage, average age or type breakdown
for four consecutive years. No utilisation or operating-day figure since the 2011
prospectus. The last ship named in an annual report was in FY2021. The website
lists twelve names that appear in no filing; trade press says eleven.

**Why it matters more than it sounds.** Everything an outsider would use to check
this business — revenue per ship, day rates against the market, whether the
valuation is plausible, whether the depreciation charge is right — requires a
fleet count. Without it, §8.5's unit economics are a guess and the accounts cannot
be tested against the market at all. A shipowner that stops disclosing its fleet
has removed the one check on everything else it says.

## 10.7 The FY2025 accounts are a draft

**The evidence.** Every page of the financial statements in the FY2025 annual
report carries "Draft Report — 28 April 2026". The audit report number is a blank
template. There are no signatures, only typed names. The board's own statements in
the same annual report are dated "Jakarta, Mei 2026".

**Why it matters.** A listed company's published annual accounts should be the
signed, final version. Numbers taken from a draft may change, and the FY2025
column throughout this report carries that caveat.

## 10.8 Two audited filings that disagree about whether the controlling shareholder sold

**The evidence.** For 31 December 2023, the FY2023 accounts show PT Delta Royal
Sejahtera holding 2,585,027,981 shares (18.31%) with a public float of 79.58% —
implying it sold 110,639,017 shares during the year. The FY2024 accounts, for the
identical date, show 2,695,666,998 shares (19.09%) and a float of 78.79% —
implying no sale. The FY2023 annual report's own profile chapter sides with the
FY2024 version.

**Why it matters.** Whether the controlling shareholder was selling in the year
the company returned to profit is a material fact for every other shareholder, and
two audited documents give opposite answers to it. There is also a smaller
version at 31 December 2022, where two filings differ by 450,600 shares.

## 10.9 Governance thinned exactly when it was needed

**The evidence.** §1.4. Three of four sequential independent commissioners resigned
between April 2022 and August 2023, with no reason given for any departure in any
year. The board of commissioners fell from four to three. Since 21 August 2023 a
single independent commissioner has been the only independent voice on the board
and has also chaired the audit committee that faces the auditor issuing the
qualifications. In July 2025 the board gained a commissioner who is president
director of the intermediary through which the first US$24.3 million advance was
paid, and former president director of the guarantor in the 2022 Sinarmas
restructuring, and placed him on the nomination and remuneration committee.

## What was checked and did not qualify as a red flag

- **Related-party dealings in the ordinary course.** Genuinely nil, every year.
  There is no evidence of the group buying from or selling to owner-connected
  businesses.
- **Board pay.** Under half a per cent of revenue and falling. Not a leakage.
- **Legal proceedings and sanctions from regulators.** The FY2025 report discloses
  none, in those words. Nothing was found to contradict it.
- **The auditor.** The same firm throughout, with the qualifications getting more
  specific rather than quieter, and a partner rotation in 2022 and again in 2025.
  The auditor is the most reliable narrator in this file.
- **The cash flow.** Operating cash generation has been positive in five of six
  years and reconciles to the profit line without an unexplained gap.
