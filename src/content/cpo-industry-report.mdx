## 00. Executive summary

**The thirteen together are 12.1% of Indonesia.** They produced 6.25 million tonnes of crude palm oil in FY2025 against a national crop of 51.66 Mt. No single name is above 2.4%. This is a fragmented industry in which the listed sector is a minority participant — roughly 41% of national planted area belongs to smallholders, and the largest private groups (Wilmar, Musim Mas, Apical/RGE, and the unlisted arms of Sinar Mas and Salim) are mostly not on the exchange at all.

**They all sell at the same price, so the price is not the variable.** FY2025 realised CPO selling prices across the eleven names that disclose one span Rp13,154–15,750/kg, with a standard deviation of 4.1% around a mean of Rp14,282/kg. Everyone sells into the same domestic tender (KPBN) or the same export benchmark net of the same levy. **What differs is what it costs them to have a tonne to sell** — and that is set by two things: how much of their fruit they grow themselves, and how good their trees are.

**The single most important structural fact in the sector: most of these companies no longer grow most of what they mill.** Across eleven of the thirteen, the largest line in cost of goods sold is *purchased raw material* — fruit or crude oil bought from someone else — not farming. The two exceptions are NSSS, at 96.4% self-supplied, and SGRO, whose own-crop production cost (47.4% of COGS) just edges out its purchased fruit (44.9%). At the other extreme, SMAR buys 90% of its COGS as raw material and AALI 76%. This converts a farming business, whose cost base moves slowly, into a milling spread, whose cost base moves with the output price. It is why the sector's gross margins cluster in the 25–35% band instead of the 45–55% a pure planter earns.

**The estate is being consumed.** On a uniform basis — nucleus hectares divided by a 25-year cycle — only three of thirteen replant at or above the required rate: SMAR (227%), CSRA (131%) and SGRO (105%). And of those, **only SMAR has demonstrably brought its average tree age down** (18 years in FY2021 to 16 in FY2025); SGRO's rose from 13 to 15 over the same period and CSRA discloses no average age at all. Nine names run at 0–94% of requirement and one (STAA) has never disclosed a replanting figure in any filing, in any year. Three — SSMS (6%), SIMP (17%) and BWPT (0%) — are effectively not replanting. Average tree ages now run 9.8 years (NSSS) to ~21 (SIMP). The volume consequence of this decade's under-investment lands in the 2029–2035 window.

**B50 is live and it is a demand event, not a profit event, for twelve of the thirteen.** The 50% biodiesel blend took effect 1 July 2026. Exactly one company in this peer group — **SMAR** — owns a FAME plant (1.05 Mt/yr) and holds a government allocation, and so is paid directly by the programme. Every other name benefits only through the price the mandate holds up. Meanwhile at least five of them (SSMS, STAA, SMAR, AALI, SIMP) *pay* the 12.5% export levy that funds it. SMAR sits on both sides at once: it pays roughly Rp2.68 trillion a year in export tax and levy, and receives BPDP subsidy on 1,076 kt of FAME.

**El Niño is a 2027–28 problem, not a 2026 one.** NOAA declared an El Niño Advisory on 13 August 2026 with a >90% probability of a very strong event through the northern winter. Palm responds to drought on a 6–24 month lag, so the yield damage lands from roughly H2 2027. The most exposed estates are the Central and East Kalimantan monocultures — NSSS, SSMS, PSGO, TAPG, DSNG — and the least exposed are the North Sumatra-weighted books, CSRA and STAA. But there is a second-order effect that cuts the other way: in a drought, mills bid against each other for scarce fruit, so **the high bought-in names get squeezed on input cost exactly when the low bought-in names are enjoying the higher price.**

**Where the quality is.** On the only agronomically comparable metric — tonnes of CPO produced from a hectare of the company's own mature land — the spread is 2.2× from best to worst: TAPG 5.66 t/ha, STAA 5.27, DSNG 5.15 at the top; SIMP 2.55, BWPT 2.91, AALI 3.01 at the bottom. The national average is 3.6 t/ha. Four of the thirteen are below it.

### Three consolidation traps you must know before comparing anything

The reason a naïve screen of these thirteen produces nonsense is that three of them report a perimeter that is not the business.

**TAPG — roughly 31% of the physical operation is outside the P&L.** Every operational figure TAPG publishes — 159,707 ha, 953kt of CPO, 18 mills — includes its 50/50 joint venture, PT Union Sampoerna Triputra Persada. The financial statements do not. USTP appears as a single line, "share of profit", worth Rp1,067bn or 27.7% of net profit. So TAPG's consolidated planted area is 82,682 ha, not 159,707. If you divide reported revenue by reported hectares you get a number that means nothing.

**SIMP — you are buying a minority of a minority.** SIMP consolidates 59.51%-owned LSIP, which is separately listed and holds around Rp7.8 trillion of its own cash. That cash cannot be moved up to SIMP shareholders without leaking 40.49% to LSIP's minorities. So SIMP screens as almost unlevered — Rp1.2tn of net debt against Rp8.3tn of cash, just 0.2× EBITDA — while the parent company on its own, once LSIP is stripped out, carries roughly Rp8.5tn of net debt. The negative carry on that structure is Rp150–250bn a year: the pyramid tax. Above SIMP sit three more layers — IndoAgri → Indofood → First Pacific → Anthoni Salim — each taking a holding-company discount.

**SSMS — the accounts have been reset twice and are not comparable across the breaks.** SSMS bought two businesses from its own controlling shareholder using pooling-of-interests accounting (the CBUT refinery in December 2023, the SML plantation in November 2025). Under Indonesian accounting standard PSAK 38, a purchase from your own parent is not treated as an acquisition — prior years are restated as though you always owned it, and the premium paid is pushed into paid-in capital rather than through the P&L. The result: FY2022 revenue was restated from Rp7,261bn to Rp11,240bn, and additional paid-in capital is now **negative Rp3,937bn**, which is 134% of total equity. Every return ratio computed off that equity base is inflated. Reported ROE of 40.9% is really about 15.6%.

## 02. Value chain — upstream, midstream, downstream

You asked for the chain split, so first the vocabulary, then the map.

**Upstream** is growing fruit. The company holds land, plants oil palms, and harvests fresh fruit bunches (FFB, or *tandan buah segar*). FFB has to be milled within about 24 hours before free fatty acid builds and quality degrades, which is why estates and mills are always co-located and why fruit is never traded far.

**Midstream** is turning fruit into commodity oil. A palm oil mill (PKS, *pabrik kelapa sawit*) sterilises and presses the bunch, giving crude palm oil (CPO) from the orange flesh and palm kernels from the nut. A kernel crushing plant (KCP) then presses the kernels into palm kernel oil (PKO) and palm kernel expeller (PKE, an animal feed). A refinery removes gums, free fatty acids, colour and odour — refining, bleaching, deodorising, hence RBD — and fractionation then splits the refined oil into a liquid fraction (olein, the cooking oil) and a solid one (stearin, for margarine and shortening). PFAD is the fatty-acid by-product.

**Downstream** is anything with a customer that isn't a commodity trader: branded consumer goods, oleochemicals (fatty acids, glycerine, soap and cosmetics feedstock), and biodiesel — fatty acid methyl ester, FAME, made by reacting CPO with methanol.

### Grouped

**Group A — Full chain (1).** SMAR alone runs fruit to fuel: estates, mills, kernel crushing, four refineries, an oleochemical plant, two consumer brands, and 1.05 Mt/yr of FAME capacity. The consequence is that it barely looks like a plantation company. It grows 524.7 kt of CPO but sells 5.33 Mt of product — **roughly ten tonnes move through its refineries for every tonne its own land grows.** Downstream is 86% of net sales. Its own estate feedstock is about 10% of throughput.

**Group B — Integrated, no biodiesel (4).** SIMP, SSMS, STAA, AALI. All four grow, mill and refine, and none makes FAME. SIMP is the only one that also owns consumer brands.

**Group C — Upstream + partial midstream (3).** DSNG, TAPG, SGRO. They mill and (except SGRO) crush kernels, but stop before refining. DSNG has a genuine second business in wood panels and flooring; SGRO has a genuine second business in oil-palm seed genetics ("DxP Sriwijaya"), which earns ~75% gross margins and is the only differentiated non-commodity asset anywhere in the peer group.

**Group D — Pure upstream (5).** BWPT, ANJT, PSGO, NSSS, CSRA. Grow fruit, mill it, sell CPO and kernels at the mill gate. Nothing else.

### The finding that should change how you read the downstream names

**Going downstream has destroyed margin for every IDX planter that has tried it.** Look at the segment margins these companies publish themselves:

| **SSMS** (FY2024) | 26.6% | **1.2%** (Edible Oil) | 67.1% |
| **STAA** (FY2025) | 24.6% | **1.4%** (refinery) | 50.8% |
| **SIMP** (FY2025) | 23.2% | **4.7%** (EOF) | 71.1% |

| **AALI** (FY2025) | Kalimantan 18.5% | Sulawesi 4.9% (refining hub) | — |

Four for four, and AALI's regional segments show the same shape. STAA's refinery, which cost Rp972bn and consumed the entire IPO raise, produced 50.8% of FY2025 revenue and 3.1% of profit. SSMS's edible-oil arm earns 1.2%. SIMP's cooking-oil business is 71% of revenue at 4.7% and falling (6.2 → 5.9 → 5.5 → 4.7% across 2022–25).

The mechanism is straightforward. Refining is a conversion business with more capacity than feedstock in Indonesia, so the refining margin is competed to near zero. The refiner's input is CPO at a market price and its output is olein at a market price; the spread is thin and it does not scale with the CPO price. What the refinery *does* do is inflate revenue — which flatters headline growth, dilutes every group margin ratio, and makes the company look larger than the profit engine underneath.

**Read the segment note, not the group margin.** STAA's group gross margin fell from 33.9% to 32.7% in a record price year purely because the refinery arrived. It looked like operational deterioration. It was mix.

**Corollary — a caution on SMAR.** SMAR's group margins look terrible (11.7% gross, 3.0% net) because 86% of its revenue is a thin-margin processing flow. But its half-million-tonne upstream core earned Rp2,559bn of segment result in FY2025 — **more than the Rp2,151bn earned by the entire Rp79tn downstream machine.** The company is best understood as a small plantation with a very large toll-processing operation bolted to it.

## 03. Assets — land, plasma, age, extraction

### 3.1 Landbank — nucleus and plasma

Two words you need first. **Nucleus** (*inti*) is the estate the company owns and farms itself. **Plasma** is the smallholder scheme it is legally obliged to develop alongside it — under Indonesian regulation, a company granted a plantation permit must develop a proportion of the area (historically 20%, under Permentan 98/2013) for surrounding smallholders, finance the planting through a bank facility, and buy the fruit back. Plasma land is *not* owned by the company. The company carries the development loan as a receivable, buys the fruit at a regulated price, and typically earns nothing on the hectares themselves.

This matters for comparison in three ways. Plasma hectares inflate a "landbank" headline without contributing estate profit. Plasma fruit is bought, not grown, so it belongs on the purchased side of the fruit ledger. And plasma receivables are a real credit exposure that several of these companies have had to write off.

**Reading the plasma column.** SGRO carries the heaviest plasma load at 38.3% — 48,644 ha across 35,239 smallholder families. That is a third of the reported landbank producing fruit the company must buy. SIMP's 91,352 plasma hectares come with a receivable that had a 62% allowance against it before Rp1,084bn was written off in 2024. BWPT's plasma covers 7,791 smallholders with Rp414bn of receivables (4.1% of assets) and a Rp710bn guarantee facility. At the other end, CSRA (4.2%) and NSSS (6.3%) barely have a plasma programme, which is one reason their gross margins are the highest in the group.

**The immature column is the tell.** Immature palms — under about three to four years, not yet bearing — are the company's future volume. A healthy estate on a 25-year cycle should carry roughly 12–16% of its area immature at any time. Look at what's actually there: **SSMS 0.6%, BWPT 0.36%, PSGO 1.0%.** These estates have no replacement pipeline. BWPT's immature area has been frozen at 271 hectares since FY2019 — seven consecutive years in which essentially nothing was planted. SSMS's immature plantation capex in FY2025 was Rp13.4 billion against a steady-state requirement of roughly Rp215 billion.

**Land title is a shared, under-disclosed vulnerability.** Indonesian plantation land is held under *Hak Guna Usaha* (HGU), a cultivation right of 25–35 years, renewable. It is not freehold, which is why land carries so little book value in these accounts (AALI: Rp592bn of land against Rp28.7tn of revenue; TAPG: Rp494bn, 3.8% of gross fixed assets). Recurring problems across the thirteen:

- **Planting on land the company does not yet have title to.** DSNG has 25,559 ha on location permits only, HGU "in process". STAA has 7,187 planted hectares with no certificate at all and 19,171 ha of HGU pending. TAPG capitalises bearer plants on location-permit land. In each case the trees are on the balance sheet on the belief the title will arrive.
- **Expiries inside the investment horizon.** DSNG's earliest HGU expires 2030. TAPG's run 2036–2053. STAA has a 2029 tranche of undisclosed size. Only SSMS and SIMP publish no expiry schedule at all.
- **A live state enforcement campaign.** Since 2025, the Satgas PKH task force (under Perpres 5/2025 and PP 45/2025) has been reclaiming plantation land inside designated forest areas. The national totals are genuinely unsettled — four of the thirteen teardowns cite four different figures, and I record the range rather than picking one: **land recovered** 3.3m ha (TAPG's source) / 5.89m ha (SGRO's) / 5.9m ha (DSNG's and SMAR's) / 688k–890k ha "returned" (SIMP's, on a narrower definition); **transferred to state operator PT Agrinas** 1.5m ha (TAPG) / 2.37m ha (SGRO) / 4.1m ha (DSNG, SMAR); **fines collected** Rp10.2–10.3tn (NSSS, DSNG) / Rp11.4tn (SIMP), against a Satgas billing range reported at Rp2.34tn across 20 companies up to Rp38.6tn across 71. The company list has never been published. Exposure among the thirteen (detail in §9): **AALI Rp572bn** of fines buried in the G&A "Legal" line; **SGRO Rp700.5bn** plus 2,986 ha surrendered; **SIMP Rp2,337bn** paid into escrow and booked as an *asset* rather than an expense; **NSSS Rp108bn** booked against an auditor-assessed Rp554bn, which is what triggered its qualified opinion; **SSMS Rp146bn** of forestry payments hidden in G&A "insurance and permits"; **BWPT** 4,803 live hectares rejected under SK 36/2025 with no disclosure at all. Only CSRA has affirmatively told the exchange it has no land in unauthorised forest area.

### 3.2 Age profile and the replanting arithmetic

An oil palm yields nothing for three to four years, rises to a peak around years seven to twelve, plateaus through about year eighteen, and then declines — not mainly because the bunches get smaller, but because the tree gets tall enough that harvesting cost rises and harvest quality falls. Past roughly 25 years it must be felled and replanted, which costs about Rp70 million per hectare and gives up four years of production. So a plantation is a portfolio of vintages, and the honest question about any planter is: *are you replacing your trees at the rate you are consuming them?*

**A note on method, because it changes the answer.** The individual teardowns each computed a replanting requirement on a different basis — DSNG's used a 25-year cycle on total planted area (~4,500 ha/yr), SGRO's a 20-year cycle (~3,900 ha/yr), STAA's 4% of headline area (~1,970 ha/yr), AALI's a 25-year agronomic need (8,323 ha/yr). Those are not comparable. The table below puts all thirteen on one basis — **nucleus hectares ÷ 25** — and shows the source's own figure alongside where it differs. ### 3.3 Yield, OER and kernel extraction

**Oil extraction rate (OER)** is the percentage of a tonne of fresh fruit that comes out as crude palm oil. It is the single most-watched operating number in a mill because it is nearly pure margin — the fruit is already bought and the mill is already running, so an extra percentage point of OER is an extra percentage point of oil at zero incremental cost. BWPT's teardown quantifies it: one percentage point of OER is worth about Rp173 billion of revenue to that company alone.

OER is determined by three things the company controls: the genetics of the planting material, the ripeness of fruit at harvest, and how quickly fruit reaches the mill. It is also *reduced* by buying third-party fruit, which arrives at unknown ripeness after unknown transit — which is why the highest-OER names in this group are the ones that mill mostly their own fruit.

**Kernel extraction rate (KER)** is the same idea for the nut: kernels as a percentage of fruit, normally 4–5%.

**2. OER is where the Kalimantan names win.** TAPG 23.3%, DSNG 23.3%, BWPT 23.3%, SSMS 22.1%, NSSS 22.2% — versus AALI at 19.2% and the Sumatra-weighted books in the 20–21% band. Part of this is planting material; part is that the Kalimantan estates are more contiguous, so fruit reaches the mill faster. Industry average is roughly 20–21%.

**3. AALI's 19.22% is the worst in the group and it is a bought-fruit problem.** When 38.7% of your throughput arrives from third parties, you cannot control ripeness or transit time. AALI's OER did improve to 19.5% in 1H2026 — its best since 2019 — but it starts from the bottom of the table.

**4. BWPT is the sharpest illustration that OER cannot save a bad estate.** It has a top-three OER of 23.3% and the *second-worst* CPO per hectare, because its fruit yield is 12.5 t/ha against TAPG's 24.3. Excellent milling of a starved estate. (Note the derivation is unflattering to BWPT: its 12.5 t/ha is stated on the 87,000-ha company-narrative base, so multiplying by OER gives 2.91 t/ha, below the 3.86 t/ha the company itself prints on the audited 74,339-ha base. The company has never reconciled the two area figures, which is exactly why this row is uncertain.)

**5. PSGO has never published an OER, in any filing, since founding.** This is not a gap; it is the central disclosure problem in that company. The 2019–20 throughput data it did disclose implies an OER of 24.5–24.9%, which is above the physical ceiling for the crop. Post-2021 the mass balance implies 18–19%. The teardown's verdict: "every branch of the tree has at least one implausible leaf."

**Trend matters as much as level.** Two names are visibly deteriorating: **SSMS**, whose nucleus FFB yield has fallen from 27.0 t/ha in FY2022 to 21.2 in FY2025 — a 21.5% decline management has never acknowledged, and which coincides with a Rp559bn cut to fertiliser and maintenance spend between FY2022 and FY2024. And **SIMP**, down 23% since 2018 (15.9 → 12.3 t/ha) on an estate averaging ~21 years. Running the other way: **STAA** has raised its FFB yield six consecutive years, and **NSSS** has gone 15.86 → 20.65 t/ha in four, though from a young-estate base that flatters it.

### 4.2 The kernel windfall — a sector-wide earnings distortion in FY2025

Palm kernel normally sells at 45–50% of the CPO price. In FY2025 it went to 71–88% across every single name in this peer group.

The cause is outside palm: a global supply shock in lauric oils — coconut and palm kernel oil, the short-chain oils used in soaps, detergents, surfactants and specialty fats. Coconut supply failed, and palm kernel oil is the only substitute at scale. Kernel prices roughly doubled.

Every company in the table got the same windfall, and none of them earned it. The teardowns each sized the reversion — what happens to earnings if the PK/CPO ratio returns to its historical band:

**The mass-balance problem at PSGO.** Two mills totalling 150 t/hr process far more fruit than 28,026 hectares can grow. But PSGO stopped disclosing purchased FFB tonnage after FY2021 — precisely the year the purchase line tripled to Rp866bn — has never published an OER, and discontinued mill utilisation disclosure the same year. Three deletions at exactly the three points where an outside analyst would test the model. Roughly a third of the business is unauditable from outside.

**Why bought fruit compresses margin, mechanically.** Own fruit costs what it costs to farm — CSRA's is Rp1,154/kg cash, PSGO's Rp1,132/kg, BWPT's Rp1,696/kg. Bought fruit costs a market price that is indexed to the CPO price, typically Rp3,000–3,800/kg. **Every tonne of bought fruit costs roughly 2.4× a tonne of own fruit** (CSRA's own arithmetic), and the miller keeps only the conversion spread. So the more you buy, the more your business becomes a fixed, thin, price-indexed spread rather than a farming margin that expands when prices rise. That is why NSSS earns a 54.3% gross margin and AALI earns 16.2%.

**Eleven of thirteen have a purchased-input line as their largest cost. Only NSSS and SGRO have farming as their biggest.** For NSSS that is what 96.4% self-supply looks like on a P&L, and it is why its gross margin is 20 points above the peer median. For SGRO it is a much narrower margin — own crop beats purchased fruit by 2.5 percentage points of COGS — and the company is better described as roughly half planter, half fruit buyer.

**The disclosure problem is severe and it is systematic.** The largest cost line in this industry is almost never broken down:

- **TAPG** puts 66% of production cost into one undifferentiated line — "raw and supporting materials plus processing costs" — that bundles fertiliser, purchased fruit, chemicals and mill costs together. The teardown calls it the weakest disclosure in the financial statements.
- **AALI's** Rp18.3 trillion line is never broken down at all. Its raw-material cost per tonne of FFB processed has risen 63% since 2022 while its CPO selling price rose 11%. The cost base is now a market price, not a farming cost — and there is no way to see inside it.
- **Fertiliser is disclosed as a discrete line by exactly two of thirteen.** PSGO ("Fertilising", Rp70,265m, 4.0% of COGS) and NSSS (FY2024 Rp275bn, 30.4% of that year's COGS — disclosed once, then dropped). Two more give it bundled with maintenance, which is not the same thing: CSRA ("fertiliser & estate maintenance" Rp252.5bn, 20.5% of COGS) and SSMS ("fertiliser and maintenance", never split). The remaining nine bury it entirely. SGRO's teardown puts it bluntly: "never separately quantified in any filing in hand — a disclosure gap peers do fill."
- **The purchase price of third-party fruit — the single biggest cost in the sector — is disclosed by nobody, and no supplier is ever named.** CSRA states one and implies another 19% higher. STAA has never disclosed it in six filings.

**Two fertiliser stories worth extracting, because they are the clearest examples of an income statement flattered by an agronomic decision.** AALI's fertiliser *inventory* has been drawn down 74% from its 2022 peak — Rp687bn to Rp176bn — a three-year stock rundown that reduces reported cost without reducing reported yield yet. And SSMS cut fertiliser-and-maintenance spending from Rp1,273bn (FY2022) to Rp714bn (FY2024), a Rp559bn reduction, over exactly the period its nucleus yield fell 21.5%.

**And one government cost that is bigger than it looks.** SMAR's single largest operating expense line is not selling or administration — it is **export tax and levy at Rp2,680bn, 52.7% of all selling expense and 3.1% of revenue.** STAA's export tax and levy line went from Rp39.7bn to Rp461.4bn in one year (+1,063%) when its refinery started exporting. The state's take is a first-order cost item for anyone who exports.

## 06. B50 — who benefits, and how

### What B50 is, and where it stands

Indonesia mandates a minimum blend of palm-derived biodiesel (FAME) into diesel fuel. The blend has stepped up: B30 in 2020, B35 in February 2023, **B40 from 1 January 2025**, and **B50 from 1 July 2026**.

The timeline was genuinely turbulent, which is why the thirteen teardowns disagree about it. B50 was flagged for H2 2026 in December 2025, postponed in January 2026, then reversed and signed as **Kepmen ESDM 257.K/2026 on 17 June 2026**, taking effect 1 July with a three-month transition to run down B40 stock in the distribution chain — full compliance from 1 October 2026, with a programme review in December. Minister Bahlil Lahadalia's stated goal is to eliminate diesel imports entirely in 2026.

**Results so far.** B40 delivered 14.2 million kL in 2025, 105.2% of target. Diesel imports fell from 8.3 Mt to 5.0 Mt, saving Rp130.21 trillion of foreign exchange. The 2026 allocation was set at 15.65 million kL in December 2025, then raised to **~17.60 million kL** when B50 was confirmed for the second half.

**Incremental CPO demand.** Sources disagree and I record both: Kementan estimates B50 needs about **5.3 million tonnes/year more CPO** than B40 (against 12.70 Mt actually consumed by biodiesel in 2025, implying ~18 Mt); CNBC Indonesia's research desk frames full-year B50 run-rate offtake at **~23.3 Mt**, roughly 30% of the national crop. STAA's own estimate is 17.5–18.0 Mt. The conservative number is 5.3 Mt incremental.

### Who is a direct beneficiary: exactly one

**SMAR is the only company in this peer group with a FAME plant.** 1.05 Mt/yr of capacity at PT Sinarmas Bio Energy; 1,076 kt of FAME sold in FY2025, worth about Rp12.2 trillion or 14% of revenue. It sells at the administered HIP price with the market-versus-diesel gap paid by BPDP out of levy receipts.

Note one unresolved conflict in the allocation figures. The SMAR teardown records a **FY2025 allocation of 1.33 million kL (8.4% of the national quota)**. The published 2026 allocation list shows **981,971 kL**. That is a 26% reduction and I cannot reconcile it — the 2026 list is a secondary reproduction and may not be complete or final. Flagged in §9.

**The circularity worth understanding.** SMAR pays about Rp2.68 trillion a year in export tax and levy — its single largest operating expense line — into the fund that then pays it a subsidy on its FAME. The state taxes SMAR's exports to subsidise SMAR's biodiesel. Whether that nets positive depends on the crude oil price, which is not a palm variable at all.

### Who benefits indirectly, and how much

Everyone else gets the same thing: a mandate that removes 12.7 Mt (soon ~18 Mt) of oil from the exportable pool each year and holds the domestic clearing price up. For a **100% domestic seller who never pays the export levy, this is close to pure tailwind.** TAPG, DSNG, NSSS, PSGO, CSRA, BWPT, ANJT and SGRO are in that position. DSNG's teardown puts it plainly: the levy is borne by exporters while DSNG enjoys the demand pull — net strongly positive.

**But the tailwind is policy-made, and policy can be withdrawn.** DSNG's own stress case: B50 throttled back to an effective B45 through quota management plus a further levy increase, KPBN price to Rp13,500/kg, pre-tax profit back to FY2024 levels — roughly a 35% cut to fair value. As that teardown says, the FY2025–26 price windfall is "substantially policy-made."

**And the funding is not secure.** In early July 2026 the biodiesel index price was Rp14,562/litre against diesel at Rp13,442 — a **Rp1,117/litre gap** the state must fund. PASPI estimates ~Rp10 trillion of incentives needed for H2 2026 alone. BPDP's own scenario analysis is the number to watch: at US$85/barrel crude it pays **Rp41.3 trillion**; at US$100 it pays Rp30tn; at US$115, Rp25tn. BPDP has stated that a **23.8% export levy — nearly double today's 12.5%** — would be required for the programme to be self-funding. It has simultaneously said publicly that funds are safe through end-2026. Both positions are on the record.

**The levy is the transmission channel of that risk to the upstream names, and it is already at 27% of the price.** Under PMK 9/2026 effective 1 March 2026 the CPO export levy is 12.5% of the Kemendag reference price, on top of a US$148/t export duty. At the August 2026 reference price of US$996.52/t that is US$124.56 + US$148 = **US$272.56, about 27% of the reference price.** Every increase toward the 23.8% BPDP says it needs comes out of the domestic price that the eight domestic-only sellers realise. The smallholder association SPKS has already publicly rejected the 12.5% rate for exactly this reason.

### Two company-specific findings

**STAA deliberately reduced its B50 exposure immediately before B50 arrived.** Its revenue to customers holding biodiesel allocations was 42–56% through FY2021–23, peaking at 55.7%. By FY2025 it was about 11%, because the new Dumai refinery redirected sales to Olam Global Agri (now its largest customer at 15.69%) and to exports — export share of revenue went from 4.6% to 34.7% in one year. The teardown's phrase: *"STAA reduced its B50 exposure by roughly four-fifths, by choice, immediately before B50 arrived."* There is a second-order problem too — B50 competes for the same feedstock STAA's own refinery needs, roughly 600,000 t/yr. It has hedged itself onto the wrong side of its own tailwind.

**TAPG is the most B50-exposed name in the group without owning any biodiesel capacity.** It sells 100% domestically, direct to refiners, and its top three customers are Apical/Kutai Refinery (21.93%), Wilmar/Sinar Alam Permai (21.09%) and SMART (9.30%) — **52.32% of revenue to three groups that between them hold a large share of the national FAME allocation.** Its exposure runs through its customer book rather than its asset base.

### The counter-signal

**The demand pull has not yet shown up in the price.** B50 started 1 July 2026, but national CPO stocks *rose* to 3.042 Mt by end-May from 2.068 Mt at the start of the year, monthly output fell three months running, the Kemendag reference price slipped below US$1,000/t in August 2026, and Malaysian stocks hit a five-month high. Jan–May production ran +10.68% — well ahead of GAPKI's "thin growth" guidance. Whatever B50 does to the price, it had not done it by mid-August 2026.

## 07. El Niño — who is exposed

### The mechanism, and why the timing matters more than the event

Drought does not reduce palm oil production immediately. Water stress acts on the tree's reproductive cycle in three stages:

- **0–6 months:** minimal. Bunches already set continue to fill; some weight loss and slower ripening.
- **6–12 months:** bunch abortion feeds through. Already-initiated bunches fail. FFB volumes begin to fall.
- **12–24 months:** the deepest effect. Water deficit at the point of flower initiation shifts the sex ratio toward male inflorescences. Fewer female flowers means fewer bunches one to two years later.

**The 2023–24 event is the empirical fit.** El Niño peaked November 2023 – January 2024. Indonesian CPO output fell in **2024** to 48.16 Mt, −3.8% — a roughly 9–15 month lag — then rebounded 7.26% in 2025 once rainfall normalised. GAPKI's own explanation for the 2025 recovery was better weather plus high 2024 prices funding fertiliser.

### Where we are now

**NOAA CPC issued an El Niño Advisory on 13 August 2026** — conditions are already present, not merely watched. The Niño-3.4 anomaly stood at **+1.4°C** in July, with eastern equatorial Pacific anomalies above +2.0°C and strengthening. The forecast is a **>90% probability of a very strong El Niño** through the northern autumn and winter of 2026–27, and a **69% probability of a historically strong event exceeding +2.5°C during October–December 2026.**

**BMKG** (10 June 2026) forecast a 2026 dry season "drier and longer than the normal average", peaking in **August across 369 zones — 48.84% of Indonesia's land area** — with El Niño persisting into early 2027 at 98% probability of at least moderate intensity and 62% probability of strong.

**Therefore: the production hit lands from roughly H2 2027 through 2028, not in 2026.** TAPG's own factsheet says the impact "will occur in the 2027 production." STAA's teardown says the same. DSNG's calls the 2016 and 2021 production holes "12–24-month El Niño echoes" and places the current one in 2027–28. **2026's numbers reflect the benign 2025 weather.** The near-term operational hazard is fire, not yield — BMKG explicitly flags *karhutla* (land and forest fire) and water-availability risk for Sumatra and Kalimantan peatlands this dry season.

### Regional exposure

El Niño rainfall deficits in Indonesia are strongest in the south and east of the archipelago and weakest in the northwest. North Sumatra and Aceh sit closest to the equator and are the least ENSO-coupled. South Sumatra, Lampung and Jambi, with a sharper dry season, take materially larger deficits. Central and South Kalimantan take large deficits — the peat-fire years of 1997, 2015, 2019 and 2023 cluster there. West Kalimantan less so. Sulawesi and Papua see among the biggest deficits in strong events but hold little planted area.

**One caveat, stated plainly:** that ranking is general Indonesian ENSO teleconnection climatology, not a figure from a dated BMKG or GAPKI regional publication. BMKG's June 2026 release names Sumatra, Kalimantan, Sulawesi, Java, Nusa Tenggara, Maluku and Papua as all facing significant deficits without ranking them. ### The second-order effect that inverts the ranking

Drought cuts volume but lifts price — and for most of these companies the price gain more than offsets the volume loss, because CPO is priced on a global balance while any one company's volume is idiosyncratic. So a pure grower with no bought fruit **wins** in a drought year on a net revenue basis.

**But a company that buys a large share of its fruit gets squeezed from both ends.** In a regional drought, mills compete for a shrinking pool of third-party fruit, so FFB purchase prices rise *faster* than CPO prices — the miller's spread compresses precisely when the planter's margin expands. Ranked by how much fruit they must buy in a drought:

| Most squeezed by bought-fruit inflation | Bought % | Least squeezed | Bought % |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| **STAA** | 46.3% | **NSSS** | 3.6% |
| **AALI** | 38.7% | **TAPG** | 16.1% |
| **ANJT** | 38.2% | **DSNG** | 19.1% |
| **CSRA** | 37.7% | **BWPT** | ~24% |
| **SSMS** | 31.7% | **SIMP** | 27% |

**The two names that come out worst on a combined view are SSMS and AALI.** SSMS has the highest-exposure geography (100% Central Kalimantan), a yield already declining 21.5% from peak, effectively zero replanting, and 31.7% bought fruit. AALI has the best geographic diversification but the oldest estate, the lowest OER, and the second-highest bought-fruit share — so it loses on cost even where it does not lose on rainfall.

**The two that come out best are CSRA and NSSS, for opposite reasons.** CSRA sits 79% in North Sumatra, the least ENSO-coupled palm region in the country, and is one of only four names replanting at above the required rate. NSSS is in the most exposed province in the country but is 96.4% self-supplied on the youngest estate in the group — it takes the full volume hit and the full price gain, with no purchased-fruit cost inflation in between. Its own sensitivity disclosure: a 10% CPO price move is worth 19–20% of net profit.

### What the teardowns do *not* say

**Two of thirteen contain no weather discussion whatsoever, and three more barely qualify.** The genuine blanks are **AALI**, silent on El Niño, drought, rainfall and peat across five annual reports — it attributes every yield decline to tree age — and **SSMS**, whose seven annual reports, roughly 3,300 pages, contain no El Niño, drought or rainfall discussion at all; the only tangential item is an erratic fire-insurance line on immature plantations "in Central Kalimantan" that went from Rp540bn (2018) to nil (2021) to Rp32.8bn (2022), never explained, with the risk register silent throughout. The three thin ones do at least name the mechanism: **SIMP** only through a GAPKI quote (\<2% national growth guided for 2026 "on El Niño, aging national tree stock and slow replanting") with nothing company-specific; **SMAR** attributes its FFB decline since FY2022 to "El Niño lag plus replanting withdrawals" and stops there; and **BWPT** discusses two quantified drought episodes — FY2020 FFB −25% on an 18-month lag, FY2019 yield down to 10.2 t/ha with a 0.4% gross margin — without ever using the words "El Niño". BWPT's is the most useful of the three precisely because it is quantified.

**Peatland exposure is disclosed by exactly one company, and not by the company.** Only BWPT has a figure, and it comes from the external SPOTT assessment, not from any BWPT filing: 12,468 ha of peatland of which 9,740 planted, plus burn scars at PT STP (1,105 ha, 2015) and PT Arrtu (318 ha, 2019). Peat is the material that burns in an El Niño dry season, and twelve of thirteen companies do not tell you how much of it they farm.

## 08. Synthesis — five kinds of company

The thirteen are not a single asset class. Sorted by what actually drives the equity:

### Group 1 — Operating quality, priced as a commodity (3)
**TAPG, DSNG, STAA.** The best agronomy in the group: 5.66, 5.15 and 5.27 tonnes of CPO per mature hectare, OER of 23.3%, 23.3% and 21.3%, net cash or near it, clean-to-clean-ish related-party files. All three are running an under-replanted estate (45%, 54% and undisclosed against requirement), and all three carry a specific hair: TAPG's 31% off-balance-sheet joint venture and 99% cash payout; DSNG's 25,559 untitled hectares and a habit of withdrawing disclosures; STAA's 1.4%-margin refinery and its own guidance of a 15.6% profit decline in FY2026.

### Group 2 — Structurally impaired, whatever the price does (3)
**AALI, SIMP, BWPT.** The three worst estates in the group by CPO per hectare (3.01, 2.55, 2.91), all with the oldest trees (16.7, ~21, ~17–18 years), all replanting at 61%, 17% and 0% of requirement, all buying 24–39% of their fruit to fill mills their own land can no longer feed. AALI has a Rp572bn forestry fine buried in G&A and a governance chapter in the same document saying there were no material cases. SIMP has a Rp2,337bn forestry charge booked as an *asset*, three stacked holding-company discounts above it, and an age table that was deleted from the filings. BWPT is not a plantation investment at all — it is a claim on a US$678m arbitration between two 37% shareholders, with a plantation attached.

### Group 3 — Governance is the investment case (4)
**SSMS, CSRA, PSGO, SGRO.** In each of these the controlling family's behaviour, not the palm cycle, is what determines the outcome. SSMS: two related-party poolings that moved ~Rp5.05tn to the parent and left equity negative Rp3.94tn in paid-in capital; free float drained from 35.8% to 24.0% with no disclosed transactions; a customer taking 61% of revenue that went to zero five weeks before the state named SSMS in a transfer-pricing finding. CSRA: float collapsed to 6.71%, board pay at 80% of all dividends ever paid, a decade-old interest-free director loan. PSGO: float 11.48% and shrinking through the controller's personal purchases; the president director stood down over an undisclosed criminal matter in February 2026; a one-day restructuring on 5 March 2026 that repaid Rp848bn of debt, injected capital into two subsidiaries and reshuffled eight of nine subsidiary boards around an unknown new director. SGRO: a nineteen-month double sale ending in POSCO ownership at 98.695% and a **1.3% float**, with an OJK deadline to restore 20% by March 2028.

### Group 4 — Captive subsidiaries with a listed rump (2)
**ANJT, SMAR.** Both are majority-owned operating units of a larger group, and both route the majority of revenue to affiliates. ANJT: 95.92% First Resources, free float 4.08% (below IDX's 7.5% minimum), related-party revenue 0% → 57.5% in a single year with no disclosed pricing basis, every year from FY2020 to FY2024 restated, and the Papua estates — 91,210 ha — sold for **US$24,316**. SMAR: 92.40% Purimas/Golden Agri, float 7.60% one notch above the floor, Rp43.3tn of sales and Rp14.0tn of purchases with the family, and no achieved-price disclosure that would let you test any of it. The difference is that SMAR is a well-run, conservatively geared, metronomically distributing cash conduit — and ANJT is a special situation on someone else's corporate-action calendar.

### Group 5 — The clean single-asset play (1)
**NSSS.** The purest upstream business here: one province, five subsidiaries, 96.4% self-supplied, sells CPO and kernel at the mill gate, 54.3% gross margin precisely because it owns nothing downstream, deleveraged from 351% to 35% net gearing. And it carries the single sharpest disclosed risk in the peer group: a **qualified FY2025 audit opinion** over forestry provisioning, where the auditor's own arithmetic would cut reported net profit roughly in half (Rp648bn → ~Rp316bn), in the same document whose governance chapters state there were no legal cases or administrative sanctions.

### The three questions that separate them all

If you strip the thirteen teardowns down to what actually differentiates these companies, it is three numbers:

1. **What share of your fruit do you grow?** Range: 96.4% (NSSS) to ~10% (SMAR). This sets your gross margin and your drought sensitivity.
2. **Are you replacing your trees?** Range: 227% of requirement (SMAR) to 0% (BWPT). This sets whether the business exists in 2035.
3. **Whose price is it?** Related-party revenue ranges from 0.0% (CSRA, PSGO, SGRO, BWPT) to 57.5% (ANJT) and 49.8% (SMAR). This sets whether the reported margin is a market outcome or a family policy variable.

Price, mandate and weather affect all thirteen roughly equally. Those three numbers do not.

## 09. Missing data and open items

Ranked by what it would change.

**1. SMAR's and SIMP's achieved selling prices — the two companies with the most related-party revenue are the two that do not disclose ASP.** SMAR sells Rp43.3tn (49.79% of revenue) to affiliates, Golden Agri International alone taking 33.28%, and publishes no realised-price-versus-benchmark comparison. SIMP transfers Rp8.4tn internally and does the same. *Where it likely sits:* nowhere — this is a disclosure choice, not an extraction failure. The only external test would be comparing segment revenue per tonne against the KPBN print, which requires a tonnage split neither company gives. *What it decides:* whether roughly half of SMAR's revenue moves at market, and therefore whether its reported margin means anything.

**2. SMAR's FAME allocation — a 26% discrepancy between two sources.** The SMAR teardown records a FY2025 allocation of 1.33 million kL (8.4% of national quota); the reproduced 2026 ESDM allocation list shows 981,971 kL. *Where it likely sits:* the primary ESDM allocation decree for 2026, and the BPDP quarterly realisation reports. *What it decides:* the size of the only direct B50 benefit anywhere in this peer group.

**3. SSMS's 1H2026 results — not filed as of 20 August 2026.** SSMS is a habitually late interim filer; 1H2025 appeared only in mid-September 2025. *Where it likely sits:* IDX filing, expected ~September 2026. *What it decides:* whether the Q1 collapse in related-party sales (Borneo Agri-Resources from 46% of Q1 2025 revenue to zero) was a one-quarter event or a structural break, and whether the 25 May 2026 transfer-pricing finding has bitten.

**4. Two 1H2026 source conflicts I could not resolve — STAA and TAPG.** In both cases the company teardown carries an interim P&L that press coverage does not, and the two disagree. **STAA:** the teardown gives revenue Rp6,070bn (+69.1%) and net profit Rp854bn (+12.3%) from the 2Q2026 investor bulletin; press coverage carries operational data only. **TAPG:** the teardown gives revenue Rp5,857bn (+6%) and net profit Rp2,120bn (+21%) from Investor Newsletter Vol. 23; Kontan reports Rp2.04tn (+20.43%) — an Rp80bn gap most plausibly explained as total versus attributable-to-parent, but no source states it. *Where it likely sits:* the IDX interim filings for both, which would settle the profit line and give STAA's operating profit. *What it decides:* for STAA, whether its own FY2026 guidance of PATMI −15.6% is tracking — with FFB −7.2%, CPO −16.8% and EBITDA margin down 10 points, this is the peer group's clearest test of whether refinery throughput can offset upstream decline. For TAPG, whether the +21% is operations or the equity-accounted joint-venture line.

**5. The purchase price of third-party FFB — undisclosed by all thirteen, and it is the sector's single largest cost.** CSRA states Rp3,207/kg and implies Rp3,807/kg from its own cash-flow note, a 19% gap on 58.5% of COGS that determines whether its bought book earns 4% or loses money. STAA has never disclosed it across six filings and has never named a supplier for its largest cost line. *Where it likely sits:* nowhere public. Regional FFB tender prices from provincial *Disbun* (plantation offices) — which publish periodic FFB reference prices by province and tree age — would give a testable benchmark. *What it decides:* the true margin of roughly a third of this industry's activity.

**6. Peatland hectares — disclosed by nobody.** Only BWPT has a number, and it comes from the external SPOTT assessment (12,468 ha peat, 9,740 planted), not from any BWPT filing. *Where it likely sits:* Ministry of Environment and Forestry peat-hydrological-unit (KHG) maps overlaid on HGU boundaries; Auriga and SPOTT do this externally. *What it decides:* fire exposure in the 2026–27 dry season, which BMKG has explicitly flagged, and EUDR compliance risk.

**7. BWPT's 1H2026 underlying result, and whether the disposal gain is the whole story.** Three of four press sources report Rp215bn / +16%; one reports Rp211.19bn / +22.9%; the teardown says the half includes a Rp19,680m one-off gain on the PT SMS disposal and puts underlying growth at roughly +5%. No press source mentions the disposal at all. *Where it likely sits:* the IDX interim filing's other-income note and the segment disclosure. *What it decides:* whether BWPT's operating recovery is real or whether a 74,000-hectare estate with zero replanting is running flat — which is the difference between the equity being a recovery story and being purely a claim on the Felda arbitration.

**8. Satgas PKH exposure at the six companies that have disclosed nothing.** The task force has published no company list. Confirmed: AALI Rp572bn, SGRO Rp700.5bn + 2,986 ha, SIMP Rp2,337bn, NSSS Rp108bn booked / Rp554bn assessed, SSMS Rp146bn hidden in G&A. Undisclosed but externally evidenced: **BWPT** — SK 36/2025 rejected 11,191 ha across seven group entities with 4,803 hectares live, none of it in any filing, against roughly 80,000 ha of unplanted concession. Silent entirely: TAPG, DSNG, STAA, PSGO (named on IDX's 13 October 2025 letter to 19 palm issuers; denies it), SMAR. *Where it likely sits:* the unpublished Satgas PKH company list (variously reported at 20, 71 and 83 companies); Kemenhut SK 36/2025 annexes. *What it decides:* for SSMS, potential fines of Rp844bn–2,781bn against equity of Rp2,941bn — a solvency question, not an earnings question.

**9. Per-province hectare splits.** Six companies map subsidiaries to provinces without giving hectares: STAA, BWPT, DSNG (beyond the 61.7k Muara Wahau block), SMAR (Sumatra 46% / Kalimantan 54% is the entire disclosure), SGRO (approximate only), TAPG (regional table exists but excludes the JV split). *Where it likely sits:* HGU certificates at BPN; ISPO/RSPO certification annexes for the certified entities. *What it decides:* the precision of §07 — the El Niño exposure ranking is currently qualitative because the underlying hectares are not published.

**10. Replanting hectares at STAA — never disclosed in any filing, any year.** No hectares, no budget, no programme, no target. AR2025 discusses replanting as a national "productivity trap" and says 2026 is the strategic window, then discloses nothing about its own estate. *Where it likely sits:* the immature-plantation additions line in the audited bearer-plant note would proxy it. *What it decides:* whether the best estate in the peer group has a plan for the 2030s.

**11. GAPKI's numeric 2026 production forecast.** Only qualitative guidance exists ("thin growth, stable prices", November 2025) against Jan–May actuals of +10.68%. June and July 2026 monthly data were not published as of 20 August. *What it decides:* whether the stock build to 3.042 Mt is a demand problem or a supply surprise.

**12. Full-year 2025 domestic split into food versus oleochemical.** GAPKI discloses only the biodiesel component (12.70 Mt) of 24.77 Mt domestic. Monthly 2026 run-rates imply food ≈10–11 Mt/yr and oleochemical ≈2 Mt/yr, but that is inference. *What it decides:* how much genuine headroom B50 has before it starts displacing cooking oil — which is where the political constraint on the mandate ultimately binds.
