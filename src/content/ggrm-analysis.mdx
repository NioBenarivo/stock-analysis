Second-largest legal cigarette maker in the country by volume, behind HM Sampoerna. ## THE ONE THING TO UNDERSTAND FIRST

When Gudang Garam sells a pack of cigarettes, most of the money is not its own. Roughly two-thirds of the shelf price of a machine-made pack is tax: a fixed excise duty per stick set by the Finance Ministry each year, a regional "cigarette tax" that is one-tenth of the excise, and VAT charged on the government's minimum retail price. The company pays all three up front — it buys excise stamps (*pita cukai*) from Customs, sticks one on every pack, and only then may the pack leave the factory. The stamp cost sits in inventory until the pack is sold and then goes through cost of goods sold.

This shapes everything in the accounts. Gudang Garam books the full pack price, tax included, as revenue, and books the tax as its biggest cost. So its reported revenue of Rp89 triliun in 2025 is really about Rp25 triliun of its own money plus Rp65 triliun collected for the state. Its reported gross margin of 10% looks like a commodity business; on the money it actually keeps, the margin is nearer 38%. The reported net margin of under 2% hides a business that converts about 6–7% of its own revenue into profit, and did over 20% before 2021. Section 08 rebuilds the whole P&L on both bases, per stick, for every year from 2017.

The second thing follows from the first. Because the excise is a fixed rupiah amount per stick that the government raises by decree, and because the company's customers are largely lower-income smokers who cannot absorb price rises, the tariff decision each autumn is the single most important number in Gudang Garam's year — more than clove prices, more than volumes, more than anything management controls. From 2020 to 2024 the machine-made tariff rose 66% in four steps; the company's own take per stick fell by a fifth in the first two of those years and had barely recovered by the fourth. Volume halved, from 96 billion sticks in 2019 to 47 billion in 2025, as smokers moved down to hand-rolled products taxed at a third of the rate, and to illegal untaxed cigarettes. Then the government froze the tariff for 2025 and 2026 and has said it intends to hold 2027 flat too, and in the first half of 2026 the company's profit went from almost nothing to Rp3 triliun on revenue that was still falling. Nothing about the business changed; the tax stopped rising.

The third thing is what the owners did with the money while this was happening. Between 2020 and 2024 the family-controlled board pushed almost Rp12 triliun of cigarette cash into an airport for their home town, then in 2024 committed to a Rp10 triliun toll road. Both are 50-year concessions that hand the asset back to the state for nothing at the end, both lose money, and neither has been tested for impairment. In the years when the airport was being built the company skipped its dividend twice. In 2026 it paid out 99% of profit.

Evidence: the tax mechanics in §2.1 (Distribute) and §08.1–08.2; the volume collapse in §03; the airport and toll road in §08.4 and §09; the dividend record in §1.4.

## 1.3 History — recap

For sixty years this was one thing done well: a kretek maker that grew with the market, moved from hand to machine when the market did, and kept its factories, its packaging and its distribution inside the family. The founder's children ran it in sequence — Rachman Halim for 25 years, then Susilo since 2009 — and the third generation is now being installed. The company has not issued a share since 1996, never bought back a share, never taken long-term debt of any size, and never diversified out of tobacco until 2020.

Then two things happened at once. The government began raising excise faster than the company could pass it on, and the family began spending the company's cash on infrastructure for Kediri. The first halved the business; the second absorbed the cash the business still threw off. The record from 2020 to 2025 is of a company that named the excise risk correctly every year, was hit harder than it said each time, and kept building the airport regardless.

## 1.4 Owner's character

What the record shows, not what anyone says:

**They pay themselves through dividends, not salary, and only when they choose to.** The stated policy is 20–40% of earnings. Actual payouts have run between 64% and 99% in the years a dividend was paid, and zero in two of the last seven fiscal years. With 76% of the shares, the family took roughly Rp9.6 triliun of the Rp12.6 triliun paid out between 2021 and 2025. Board pay is modest by comparison — Rp100–190 billion a year for thirteen people, with no per-person disclosure and no share scheme — but in 2024, the year of no dividend and Rp981 billion of profit, the boards' pay equalled 19% of that profit.

**The dividend is a residual after the family's projects, not a commitment to minorities.** The two skipped years were 2020 (from FY2019's record profit of Rp10.9tn, "because of COVID") and 2024 (from FY2023, "to strengthen the financial condition"). In both years the company was pushing Rp2–4tn into the airport. In 2026, with the airport built and the toll road still early, it paid out 99% of a small profit.

**They keep the family in every seat that matters.** Susilo Wonowidjojo (70) has been a director since 1976 and President Director since 2009. His sister Juni Setiawati (71) has been a commissioner since 1983 and President Commissioner since 2009. His son Indra Gunawan (39) was made Vice President Director in 2022 after ten years in the company. A third-generation member, Adhi Wibhawa Wonowidjojo, joined the Board of Commissioners in June 2026. The non-family directors are long-serving employees: the corporate secretary since 2000, the finance director since 2007 (joined 1983), two plant directors since 2019. Of the three independent commissioners, two have served 24 and 12 years; the "independent director" label quietly disappeared from the 2025 accounts. The audit committee — chair and both members — was replaced wholesale in 2025: the new independent commissioner, Hanlim Suprianto, took the chair from Gotama Hengdratsonata (who stays on the board), and two former KPMG partners replaced the members of ten years' standing.

**They respond to downturns by cutting people, not by changing the model.** The 2023 volume collapse was met with price rises; the 2024 profit collapse with a 27% headcount cut in 2025 (30,308 to 21,964 people) executed as an "early retirement programme" that the filings describe only through a Rp305 billion pension credit. No factory closure, no brand withdrawal, no exit from any segment has ever been disclosed. Two plants named in the 2021 accounts (Karanganyar, Sumenep) simply stop being mentioned.

**They do not explain.** Nine documents, and there is no statement of why an airport, no budget for it before it was built, no traffic figure since, no impairment test, no explanation for the 2025 toll-vehicle reshuffle, no reason for the headcount cut, and no numeric guidance in any year. The annual report is among the shortest of any IDX large-cap (about 160 pages, 60 of them the financial statements).

Dealings with companies owned by the same family — related-party transactions — are small, one-directional and stable. The listed group buys cigarette paper and packaging from PT Surya Zig Zag and something unspecified from PT Taman Sriwedari, together about 1.3–3.0% of what it buys in a year. # 02 — MODEL

Gudang Garam has one business and two side activities. Cigarettes are 98% of revenue and all of the profit. Paperboard (PT Surya Pamenang) is a mill that mostly feeds the cigarette business and has lost money since 2023. Infrastructure (the airport and the toll road) is a place cigarette cash goes. The model below is the cigarette business; the other two are in §08.3–08.4.

## 2.1 Cigarettes

### Product

A kretek is a cigarette whose filler is roughly two-thirds tobacco and one-third chopped clove buds, with a flavouring "sauce" added. Gudang Garam makes three kinds:

The physical chain has not changed since 1990: buy leaf and cloves at harvest, hold them for a year or two so that the blend is consistent across seasons, process in Kediri and Gempol, stamp, ship. What has changed is the distribution layer (from three family-owned distributors to a wholly-owned one) and the scale of the stock holding — see Purchase below.

### Purchase — raw materials and inventory

The company buys two crops and holds them for a long time. Tobacco is bought at harvest and aged; the 1990 prospectus said up to 26 months, and the modern balance sheet says the same thing in numbers. At end-2025 raw material stock was about two and a half years of the year's raw-material usage. This is deliberate: a kretek brand is a blend, and a blend that tastes the same every year needs leaf from several harvests in the warehouse at once. It also means the company's largest asset is a pile of dried leaves and cloves.

What the company pays for its crops is never disclosed — not a tonnage, not a price, not a sourcing region in any modern filing. What can be seen is the raw-material cost per stick, which rose from Rp157 to Rp196 between 2020 and 2025 (§08.2). Cloves are the volatile input: farm-gate prices fell to around Rp60,000/kg in 2020 and have since traded at Rp100,000–140,000, so a rising clove bill is likely to be part of that per-stick rise. The two-year buffer stock is what stops a bad clove harvest from showing up in any single year's margin, and is why the company can say, every year, that supply risk is "managed via inventory buffer".

### Process — how a stick is made and what it costs

The making is simple and the cost of making is small. Leaf and cloves are conditioned, cut and blended with the sauce; for SKM the blend is fed into making machines that form the rod and attach a filter, then packing machines; for SKT the blend goes to hand-rolling halls where a worker rolls, cuts and packs by hand. Both then receive a stamped pack. The manufacturing cost of a stick — leaf, cloves, paper, filter, labour and factory overhead — was about Rp280 in 2025. The tax on the same stick was about Rp1,390.

### Distribute — the tax, the price, and who gets what

This is the part of the model that decides everything, so it is worth being exact about the mechanics.

**The stamp.** Indonesian tobacco excise is a *specific* duty: a fixed rupiah amount per stick, set by tier, paid by buying excise stamps from the Directorate General of Customs and Excise. The producer orders stamps (a "CK-1" order), may take up to about two months' credit on them, sticks them on packs, and the excise is deemed paid when the pack leaves the factory. Two more taxes ride on the stamp: a regional cigarette tax (*pajak rokok*) of 10% of the excise, and VAT at an effective 9.9% (9.1% before April 2022) of the government-set minimum retail price (*harga jual eceran*, HJE). All three are collected at the factory gate, all three sit in the stamp cost.

**The tier.** The excise rate depends on the type of cigarette and on the factory's annual volume. Any factory making more than 3 billion machine-made sticks a year is "Golongan I" for SKM and pays the top rate; Gudang Garam makes 40 billion, so there is no tier relief available to it — the tiers exist to protect small makers, not to grade large ones. For hand-rolled, the top tier starts at 2 billion sticks and is split into two price bands; Gudang Garam's SKT products sit in the lower band. The full tier tables for 2016–2026 are in §08.1.

**The price.** Because the tax is a fixed amount per stick, a rise in the tariff does not scale with the price — it lands as a fixed rupiah amount that the producer must either pass to the smoker or absorb. Gudang Garam's customers are largely lower-income. Between 2020 and 2022 the company chose to absorb: its own take per stick fell about 20% while the tax per stick rose 35%. From mid-2022 it began "more frequent price increments" and its take recovered, but each rise cost volume.

**Who gets what from a pack.** Take a 12-stick machine-made pack at the government's 2025 minimum price for a top-tier maker, Rp28,500. Rp19,071 (67%) is excise, cigarette tax and VAT. The remaining Rp9,429 is shared between the retailer, the wholesaler, Gudang Garam's distributor and Gudang Garam's factory. Gudang Garam's own blended take of about Rp483 a stick (Rp5,800 a pack, pulled down by the cheaper hand-rolled packs in the mix) is consistent with the trade keeping something like a third of the after-tax value at the minimum price. The company's actual declared prices for Surya and GG International sit above the minimum and are never published, so the real split cannot be derived from the filings; what can be said is that the state's Rp19,071 is fixed and everyone else's share is the remainder.

**The customer.** Nobody buys more than 10% of the company's output; receivables are under a month of sales; distributors lodge Rp417bn of guarantee deposits with the company. The company effectively sells for cash. The end customer is the reason volume has halved: the government minimum for a top-tier machine-made 12-pack went from about Rp13,000 in 2019 to Rp27,000 in 2024, and the smoker either traded down to hand-rolled (taxed at a third of the rate), to a small maker's tier-II brand, or to an untaxed illegal pack.

Three demand shifts, in order of importance to Gudang Garam:

**Down-trading from machine-made full-flavour to hand-rolled.** SKT's share of the legal market rose from 19% to 31% in four years. This is not nostalgia; it is arithmetic. A hand-rolled pack from a top-tier maker carries Rp6,837 of tax against Rp19,071 for a machine-made one, so the retail gap between the two widened from about Rp6,000 a pack in 2020 to over Rp12,000 by 2024. Gudang Garam is 85% SKM. Sampoerna, whose hand-rolled business is five times Gudang Garam's, has been the beneficiary — its 1H2026 revenue grew while Gudang Garam's fell.

**Illegal cigarettes.** Estimates run from a few percent to almost half the market depending on who is counting; Customs seized 816 million sticks in the first nine months of 2025, up 37%. The government's own explanation for freezing the tariff in 2025 and 2026 was that further rises would push more volume untaxed. Whatever the true share, on the series the company used at the time the legal market lost 22 billion sticks in 2023 and GG lost 21 billion of them; on the restated series the market grew that year and GG still lost 21 billion. Either way the loss sat in GG's segment.

**Purchasing power.** The company's customer is the lower-middle-income smoker whose real income has not recovered since 2020. Every annual report since 2021 names this; none quantifies it.

Demand drivers that would help: a growing adult population (still true), and a government that treats the excise as an industry-protection lever as much as a health lever (true again since 2025). Demand drivers that hurt: PP 28/2024, the health-law regulation that bans sales within 200 metres of schools, bans single-stick sales, tightens advertising, and contemplates plain packaging — the implementing ministerial rules were still being drafted in 2026.

## 3.2 Paperboard

PT Surya Pamenang sells about Rp0.8tn of board outside the group and Rp0.4tn inside it. External sales have halved since 2021 and the mill has lost money for three years. It exists to guarantee packaging supply, not to earn a return.

# 04 — SUPPLY

## 4.1 Producers and the tier system

The legal market is four large makers and several hundred small ones. Sampoerna (Philip Morris) leads with about 27–29%; Gudang Garam 15–21% depending on the series; Djarum (private) a similar order of magnitude; BAT's Bentoel and Nojorono behind; Wismilak the only other listed name, small but growing fast on the down-trading trend (revenue +34% in 2025).

The tier system is the supply-side structure that matters. A factory making under 3 billion machine-made sticks a year pays Rp746 of excise per stick in 2025 against Rp1,231 for the big four — a 40% discount, and the small maker's minimum price for a 12-pack is Rp17,820 where Gudang Garam's is Rp28,500. Since 2022 the government has merged tiers (13 in 2016, 8 since 2022) to narrow that gap, but the 3-billion cliff remains. Below it sits a large, fragmented tier-II industry that has taken volume from the majors every year since 2020.

## 4.2 Raw material supply

Indonesia grows both crops. Clove output is around 150,000 tonnes a year, over 85% from ten provinces, mostly Sulawesi and Maluku; the kretek industry is essentially the only buyer, so prices swing with the harvest (a Rp60,000/kg trough in 2020, Rp140,000 in 2025). Tobacco leaf is grown across Java, Madura and NTB, with imports of about 160,000 tonnes a year filling the gap for Virginia and Oriental grades. Gudang Garam discloses nothing about its own purchases. Its two-year stock policy is the supply strategy.

# 06 — VICISSITUDES

This is not a cyclical business in the commodity sense — demand does not swing with GDP — but it has had two kinds of shock and a slow structural decline.

**The excise shock, 2020–2024.** The machine-made tariff rose by a cumulative 66% while the company's customers lost income. The company absorbed first and raised prices late; a 20% gross margin became 9%, profit went from Rp10.9tn to Rp1.0tn, and volume halved. Profit fell in four of the five years. The 2023–2024 two-year rule produced a tolerable 2023 and a terrible 2024, and the next multi-year rule could do the same.

**The relief, 2025–2026.** Freeze the tariff and the same business earns Rp3tn in a half-year. Nothing operational changed between 2024 and 1H2026 except headcount; the swing in annualised profit was about Rp4tn. That is the measure of how little management controls.

**Structural: the product is being taxed out of its own market.** Every year since 2019 the legal market has shrunk and GG has shrunk faster. Hand-rolled has grown as a share, and GG's hand-rolled business — the one it started with — is a fifth of Sampoerna's. The 2024 launch of hand-rolled versions of the machine-made flagships is the first sign of the company following the smoker down the tax ladder.

**The self-inflicted one: infrastructure.** The airport and toll road are 50-year concessions that return the asset to the state for nothing; they lose Rp0.4–0.5tn a year before any write-down. At the 2024 level of cigarette profit that is about a sixth of it.

For scale, in 1990 the company carried bank debt at 19–20% interest, had 48,000 employees, and earned a 23% gross margin under an excise system charged as a percentage of price. The move to a fixed amount per stick is why a price rise no longer protects the margin: the state's share is fixed in rupiah, and the company's share is whatever is left.

### Working capital

Half of everything the company owns is inventory, and a third of that inventory, at the peak, was excise stamps and stamped packs — tax paid to the state before the smoker has paid the company. This is the company's working-capital cycle: buy stamps on two months' credit from Customs, hold them, sell the pack, collect within a month. When the tariff rises, the same number of stamps costs more and the cycle absorbs more cash; when volume falls, it releases it.

- **2021:** inventories +Rp7.6tn, of which stamps +Rp2.8tn; prepaid VAT +Rp2.8tn. The 2020–21 tariff rises were paid for in stock before they were paid for by smokers. Financed with short-term bank debt (+Rp4tn).
- **2022:** finished goods jump to Rp12.1tn (+38%) as sales stalled — product stamped at 2022 rates sitting unsold.
- **2023–2025:** the cycle unwinds. Stamps in stock fall from Rp5.8tn to Rp1.8tn and finished goods to Rp6.6tn as volume falls; Rp13tn of working capital is released over three years and used to repay the banks.

Receivables are trivial (a month of sales, no concentration, no related-party balances). Prepaid VAT is never explained in any note and swings by trillions.

### Fixed asset

- **2020–2022:** airport construction runs through construction-in-progress inside fixed assets; Rp5.9tn of "others" capex over three years.
- **2023:** Rp12.4tn is reclassified from fixed assets into "airport concession rights" — an intangible, because the company does not own the airport, it owns fifty years of the right to charge for it. The fixed-asset line drops by a quarter despite Rp7.7tn of additions. Amortisation of the right began at Rp248bn a year.
- **2024:** Rp0.9tn of land moved into toll-road concession rights; toll road construction now accrues as an intangible under construction (Rp2.4tn by end-2025).
- **No impairment test is disclosed in any year** for the Rp14.9tn of concession rights, although the airport has lost money since it opened.

### Business payable

The biggest thing the company owes is tax on stamps it has bought but not yet paid for. Customs gives producers roughly two months to pay for excise stamps, so at any year-end there is a large balance owed to the state — Rp16tn at the 2021–22 peak, 56% of all current liabilities, and 73% of a much smaller total in 2025. This is free credit from the government and it is the reason the company could carry Rp47tn of inventory on Rp10tn of bank debt. It also cuts the other way: a return to volume growth would have to rebuild the stamp stock and absorb cash again. Trade payables to actual suppliers are about a month of raw-material purchases.

- **2021:** excise payable +78% to Rp16.1tn — the 2021 tariff rise and a bigger stamp order ahead of the 2022 rise.
- **2023–2025:** falls with volume to Rp7.9tn.

### Business loan

Since listing the company has not borrowed long-term in any meaningful amount. What it borrows is one-to-three-month revolving rupiah facilities from a dozen banks, used to fund stamp purchases and, in 2020–2023, to fund the airport while paying dividends. Debt peaked at Rp13.4tn in 2023 (six lenders, 6.3% average rate, no security given) and was repaid to Rp0.8tn in 2025 out of the working-capital release. Undrawn facilities at end-2025: Rp28tn. Interest was never more than 0.5% of revenue; covenants are described as "determined financial ratios" and never quantified.

## 8.1 The excise tiers, 2016–2026

Indonesian tobacco excise is set once a year by Finance Ministry regulation and consists of a fixed rupiah amount per stick (the *tarif spesifik*) plus a minimum retail price per stick (*HJE minimum*) that the producer must declare on the stamp. Both depend on the product type and on the factory's annual volume, which places it in a *golongan* (tier). Two other charges ride on the excise: the regional cigarette tax at 10% of excise, and VAT at an effective 8.7% of HJE (to 2016), 9.1% (2017–March 2022) and 9.9% (April 2022 onward, held at 9.9% by PMK 11/2025 when the headline VAT rate rose).

What the two tables say, in order:

**The tax rose faster than the company could charge.** From 2019 to 2024 the SKM stack per stick rose from Rp751 to Rp1,578, a rise of Rp827. The company's booked price per stick rose by Rp655. It gave up the difference, plus more: its own take fell from about Rp450 to Rp421, and in the worst year, 2022, to Rp309. Every rupiah of tariff it did not pass on came straight out of gross profit, because the tariff is a fixed cost per stick and nothing else in the stick got cheaper.

**The reported margins are a ratio of two numbers the company does not control.** Reported gross margin fell from 21% to 9% between 2017 and 2022. On the company's own revenue it fell from 53% to 41% — a real deterioration, but a business still keeping two-fifths of its own sales as gross profit. The reported figures are dragged down mechanically because the denominator (revenue) swells with every tariff rise while the numerator does not. Any comparison of Gudang Garam's "gross margin" with a non-tobacco company, or with a company in a country where excise is netted out of revenue, is meaningless.

**Overheads did not shrink with volume.** Selling and administrative cost per stick rose from about Rp80 to Rp140 between 2019 and 2024 as 96 billion sticks became 53 billion and the sales fleet, offices and head office stayed. That is why operating margin on own revenue fell from 35% to 8% — a far bigger fall than gross margin — and why the 2025 headcount and selling-cost cuts mattered more to profit than anything the government did that year.

**When the tariff stopped, the margin came back.** In 1H2026 the stack per stick was flat for the second year, price rose, volume fell less, and the net margin on own revenue went back to 23%, where it was in 2017. The reported net margin of 7% is the same business seen through the tax-inclusive lens. If the government holds 2027 flat as the minister has said, the arithmetic continues: every rupiah of price increase falls through to profit until volume gives way.

**And the ceiling.** Even in the good years the company kept about a quarter or less of its own revenue as profit and under a tenth of the pack price. There is no lever inside the company that changes that ratio by more than a few points; the lever is in the Ministry of Finance.

## 8.4 Dhoho airport and the Kediri–Tulungagung toll road

**What was built.** An international-standard airport at Grogol, Kediri, built by the company's subsidiary PT Surya Dhoho Investama under a public-private partnership signed with the Ministry of Transport on 7 September 2022 (though construction and land buying began in 2020, before the agreement). Angkasa Pura I operates it under a joint-operation agreement for a management fee. First commercial flight 5 April 2024.

**What it cost and on what terms.** Rp13.0tn of concession rights on the balance sheet, funded by Rp11.7tn of share capital injected into the subsidiary between 2020 and 2024, all of it from cigarette cash and short-term bank debt. The concession runs 50 years from commercial operation, is not renewable, and at the end the subsidiary hands the airport to the state for nothing. The subsidiary also carries a Rp590bn obligation to upgrade the airport later. Amortisation is Rp248bn a year, which implies a life of about 52 years against a 50-year term (the filing does not reconcile the two), and the segment lost Rp443bn in 2025 after that charge. No passenger numbers, no revenue split, no fee to Angkasa Pura, no impairment test, no forecast has ever been published.

**What was then decided.** In February 2024, with the airport just finished and the cigarette business about to have its worst year, the company signed a second 50-year concession, for a toll road from Kediri (and the airport) to Tulungagung, through PT Surya Sapta Agung Tol. Reported cost Rp9.9–10tn. Work order issued 1 October 2024; Rp2.4tn of construction booked by end-2025; not yet open. Same terms: hand back for nothing at year 50, no renewal.

**The reshuffle.** Two older vehicles, PT Surya Kerta Agung and its subsidiary PT Surya Kertaagung Toll, had been capitalised with about Rp1.7tn in 2020–2023 for the same road. In 2025 Rp0.99tn was withdrawn from the first and Rp0.93tn from the second, leaving the second with Rp6bn of assets and a 93% ownership (down from 99.99%) — someone else now owns 7% of an empty company — while Rp1.8tn went into the new vehicle. No explanation is given anywhere.

Why a cigarette company builds an airport is not stated in any document. What can be observed: Kediri is the family's home town and the site of its founding factory; the airport was built on land partly bought from a family-owned company (PT Bukit Dhoho Indah); and the company's own business had Rp12tn of surplus cash a year in 2019–2020 with nowhere inside tobacco to put it.

# 09 — IMPORTANT TRANSACTIONS

## 9.1 The 1990 IPO and the pre-IPO clean-up

Before listing, the family capitalised a Rp124bn shareholder loan and a revaluation surplus into share capital, collapsed a two-class share structure (704 voting "Serial A" shares held by insiders against 1,047 non-voting) into one class, and sold 12% to the public at Rp10,250. Eighty-five percent of the proceeds repaid bank debt then costing 19–20%. The prospectus's legal opinion admitted that SKM output already exceeded the factory's licensed 9.4bn sticks a year; the directors called it immaterial. The three family-owned distributors that held 98% of receivables were later replaced by a wholly-owned distributor (2004). Effect: the family kept 88%, and has diluted since only by never buying back — 76% today.

## 9.2 Succession, 2008–2009 and 2022–2026

Rachman Halim's death in 2008 passed control to his brother Susilo (President Director) and sister Juni Setiawati (President Commissioner), who have now held those seats for seventeen years. Susilo's son Indra Gunawan was made Vice President Director in June 2022 at 36, and a further family member, Adhi Wibhawa Wonowidjojo, became a commissioner in June 2026. The 2025 AGMS reappointed both boards to 2030. Effect on the numbers: none visible — the boards' composition has not changed the dividend, capex or product decisions in any traceable way.

## 9.3 Dhoho airport, 2020–2024: Rp11.7tn out of the cigarette business

Injections into PT Surya Dhoho Investama: Rp1.4tn (2020), Rp2.0tn (2021), Rp2.9tn (2022), Rp4.1tn (2023), Rp1.3tn (2024). Concession agreement 7 Sep 2022, commercial-operation criteria met 29 Dec 2023, first flight 5 Apr 2024. Booked as Rp13.0tn of 50-year concession rights, amortised at Rp248bn a year; segment loss Rp377bn (2024) and Rp443bn (2025). Financed by Rp7tn of short-term bank borrowing in 2021–2023 and two skipped dividends. Effect: fixed assets and intangibles went from 35% to 47% of the balance sheet; the group tax rate rose to 39% as the subsidiary's losses earned no relief; Rp0.4–0.5tn a year now comes off operating profit indefinitely.

## 9.4 The toll road, 2024–2025: a second Rp10tn commitment

Concession signed 27 Feb 2024 for the Kediri–Tulungagung toll road, 50 years from the first work order (1 Oct 2024), via PT Surya Sapta Agung Tol; capitalised with Rp0.9tn in 2024 and Rp1.8tn in 2025; Rp2.4tn of construction booked by end-2025. The competitive tender through the Finance Ministry's PPP office was public. Reported total cost about Rp10tn — between one and three years of recent free cash flow. Effect so far: Rp0.8tn of self-assessed construction revenue in 2025 at zero margin; the real effect begins when construction spend ramps, which the company has not scheduled in any document.

## 9.5 The 2025 restructuring that was not called one

Headcount −8,344 (−27%), a Rp305bn pension-curtailment credit, selling expenses −Rp1.4tn, short-term debt repaid from Rp9.4tn to Rp0.8tn, Rp1.9tn withdrawn from the old toll vehicles. The 2025 annual report's "four pillars" of strategy replaced "people development" with "workforce review for productivity and cost efficiency" — the only place the cut is acknowledged. Effect: the cost base entering 2026 is about Rp1.5tn a year lower, which with the tariff freeze is most of the 1H2026 profit.

## 9.6 Dividends: two skips and a 99% payout

FY2019 (skipped, Aug 2020, record profit), FY2023 (skipped, June 2024), FY2025 (Rp800, 99% of a Rp1.56tn profit, June 2026). The pattern tracks the airport's cash needs, not earnings. Effect: Rp12.6tn paid out over 2020–2025 against Rp28tn of free cash flow; the balance built the airport and repaid its debt.

# 10 — RED FLAG

## 10.1 Rp14.9tn of concession rights that have never been tested for loss

The airport concession (Rp13.0tn at cost, Rp12.5tn after amortisation) has lost money in both years since opening; the toll road (Rp2.4tn and rising) will not open for years. Accounting rules require an asset showing signs of impairment to be tested against the cash it will generate. No test, no cash-generating-unit, no discount rate and no forecast is disclosed in any year, and the auditor (Siddharta Widjaja & Rekan, the KPMG member firm) did not make it a key audit matter — the sole KAM every year is revenue recognition on cigarettes. A write-down of the airport to what it can earn would take a large slice of the Rp62.6tn of equity in one line. Money flow: cigarette cash → subsidiary share capital → an asset that reverts to the state at year 50.

## 10.2 Revenue the company pays itself

Rp12.4tn (2023), Rp1.6tn (2024) and Rp0.8tn (2025) of revenue is the company recognising the construction of its own airport and toll road at "fair value as determined by" the subsidiary. It is permitted by the concession accounting standard, but it means a fifth of 2023 revenue was not a sale to anyone, and the group's revenue trend is mis-stated in the headline highlights for three years. It also means the size of the infrastructure assets on the balance sheet was set by the company that built them.

## 10.3 The airport and toll road are intra-group and therefore invisible in the related-party note

The related-party note names two paper suppliers and a land seller. It does not cover the Rp13tn airport, built on land partly bought from family-owned PT Bukit Dhoho Indah, whose contractors, fees and land prices are never disclosed; nor the management fee paid to Angkasa Pura I; nor whether any family company is a contractor, landowner or tenant of either project. Directors sit on the boards of PT Bukit Dhoho Indah and PT Puri Dhoho Kediri (per their biographies) — both outside the listed group, both named after the airport. What checked: FS Notes on related parties 2021/2023/2025 (purchases from two counterparties only); director biographies in AR2021–2025.

## 10.4 The 2025 toll-vehicle reshuffle

Rp1.92tn of capital withdrawn from PT Surya Kerta Agung and PT Surya Kertaagung Toll, ownership of the latter falling from 99.99% to 93.33% — creating a 6.7% outside holder of an entity with Rp6bn of assets — and Rp1.8tn injected into PT Surya Sapta Agung Tol. Who the new 6.7% holder is, what they paid, and why the money went out one door and in another, is not stated. Rp3.1bn of non-controlling interest appeared in equity as a result.

## 10.5 Two BVI aircraft companies and a private airline

Galaxy Prime Ltd and Prime Galaxy Ltd (British Virgin Islands, "non-scheduled air transport") and PT Surya Air hold about Rp1.3tn of assets between them — the only assets the group holds outside Indonesia. Their revenue is inside "others" (Rp90bn a year for the whole segment). Who flies, who pays, and whether the family uses them is not disclosed.

## 10.6 Unexplained items inside the tax and pension lines

A Rp547bn "prior-year tax adjustment" in 2023 (36% of that year's tax charge), a Rp136bn tax assessment in 2022, Rp301bn of subsidiary tax losses written off in 2025, and pension "past service cost" credits of Rp417bn (2021), Rp338bn (2022) and Rp305bn (2025) that together added Rp1.06tn to profit over five years. None has a sentence of explanation. No tax dispute, contingency, litigation or subsequent-events note exists in any year — the commitments note is always the last.

## 10.7 A market-share figure that cannot be reproduced

The company reports 17.4% (2024) and 15.2% (2025) share of a Nielsen industry volume that its own 2025 documents restated upward by 14% for 2023 and 2024 without a footnote. 53.1bn ÷ 244.3bn is 21.7%, not 17.4%. Either the share is computed on a third series or the restatement was partial. The share series is the company's only external performance metric and it is not checkable.

## 10.8 The 99% payout

Rp1.54tn paid in July 2026 from a Rp1.56tn profit, while the toll road needs perhaps Rp7tn more and the airport loses Rp0.4tn a year. The stated policy is 20–40%. A 76% holder receiving Rp1.2tn is the only visible beneficiary of a payout at four times the top of the policy range.

What was checked and found clean: the share register (unchanged, no pledges disclosed), related-party purchases (small, stable, priced "at arm's length" per the note), bank debt (unsecured, repaid, no covenant breach asserted), receivables (no related-party balances, no concentration), auditor (unmodified opinion every year; no change of auditor since at least 2020), and no share issue, buyback or option scheme of any kind.

# MISSING DATA AND OPEN ITEMS

Ranked by how much the answer would change the analysis.

1. **Airport economics.** Passenger numbers, airport revenue, the Angkasa Pura fee, and any impairment analysis for the Rp13.0tn concession right. Lives in PT Surya Dhoho Investama's own accounts (not public) and possibly in Ministry of Transport KPBU disclosures. Would decide whether the equity is overstated by Rp5–10tn.
2. **Toll-road budget, schedule and funding.** Total cost, construction timetable, and whether the company will borrow long-term for it. Lives in the PPJT with BPJT and the Kompas progress reports (blocked). Would decide the dividend capacity for 2027–2030.
3. **Volume by brand and the company's actual declared prices (HJE) for Surya 12/16 and GG International.** Never disclosed. Would allow a real price-elasticity estimate rather than a blended per-stick take.
4. **The 2025 headcount cut.** Who left (rollers vs sales vs office), severance paid, which plant. The filing gives only the pension credit; press gives 200 people. Would decide whether the company is exiting hand-rolling.
5. **The 2027 excise regulation.** The minister's May 2026 statement is not a PMK. Would decide the whole 2027 P&L.
6. **Clove and tobacco purchase volumes and prices.** Never disclosed by the company; national series behind BPS/Databoks paywalls. Would decide whether the Rp157→196 raw-material cost per stick is price, mix or quality.
7. **The related-party question on the infrastructure.** Contractors, landowners, and the roles of PT Bukit Dhoho Indah and PT Puri Dhoho Kediri. Lives in land registry and the subsidiaries' contracts.
8. **The unexplained tax and pension items** (§10.6). Lives in the tax notes the company chooses not to write.
9. **A consistent industry-volume series.** Nielsen's 2023 restatement; HMSP's annual reports quote Nielsen and would give a second source.
10. **PP 28/2024 implementing rules** (plain packaging, 200 m school ban). Draft Permenkes status as of 2026 not found.
11. **FY2016 volumes, FY2016–2017 dividends, FY2017–2019 line-item statements.** Earlier annual reports (AR2016–AR2020) were not supplied; the five-year highlights tables are the only source.
12. **Lease liabilities** — right-of-use assets appear on the balance sheet every year with no matching liability or note.
13. Smaller items: the 6.7% outside holder of PT Surya Kertaagung Toll created in 2025; the status of the Karanganyar and Sumenep plants (named in 2021, absent since) and of PT Surya Inti Tembakau ("ceased operating July 2024" per AR2024, still consolidated with Rp507bn of assets); the distributor margin and outlet count behind Rp417bn of guarantee deposits; and date conflicts between reports (Gempol plant 1993/2002/2008, Surya Pamenang 1990/1993, Rachman Halim's appointment 1979/1980/1983, Dhoho's first flight 2024 vs 2025).
