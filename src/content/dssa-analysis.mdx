**What it does.** A holding company inside Indonesia's Sinar Mas group. Nearly nine tenths of its
sales are thermal coal, dug in South Sumatra and South Kalimantan and shipped mostly to China, and
almost all of that coal sits inside a separately listed subsidiary, Golden Energy Mines (IDX: GEMS),
which DSSA owns 51% of. The rest is a fast-growing home-internet and data-centre business, a small
fertiliser and chemicals trading arm, and a geothermal exploration effort that has not yet produced
anything.

**Where.** Head office Jakarta; mines in South Sumatra, South and Central Kalimantan, Jambi and West
Sumatra; fibre network across 162 Indonesian cities; data centres in 24 cities.

**Reporting currency.** United States dollars. Every figure below is US$ unless it carries "Rp".

**Size.** Revenue US$2.79bn and profit attributable to DSSA's own shareholders US$230.5m in FY2025.
Market capitalisation Rp625.1 trillion (US$37.2bn) at 31 December 2025 on the company's own
statement; roughly Rp176 trillion (about US$10.5bn) on the shares actually outstanding at
Rp1,140 in early September 2026, after a 1-for-25 stock split in April 2026 and a fall of about 79%
from the September 2025 peak.

**Period covered.** FY2009 (the IPO prospectus) and FY2017–FY2025 continuously, plus events to
March 2026 from the FY2025 report's subsequent-events note and press reporting to September 2026.

**Sources in hand.** Five annual reports (FY2021, FY2022, FY2023, FY2024, FY2025), seven public
expose decks (FY2020 through FY2025), and the December 2009 IPO prospectus. Full list and coverage
in *Sources* at the end. There is no interim or first-half 2026 filing in this set.

## THE ONE THING TO UNDERSTAND FIRST

When you buy a share in DSSA you are not buying the coal. You are buying a claim that has been
thinned at three separate points before it reaches you, and the market has been pricing it at many
times what the same coal costs on the same exchange.

The first thinning is that the coal is not DSSA's. Coal is 89% of what the group sells, and almost
all of it belongs to Golden Energy Mines, a company listed on the Indonesian exchange in its own
right, of which DSSA owns 51%. The other 49% belongs to outside shareholders. When the mines earn
a dollar, roughly half of it is legally theirs. In the group's accounts this appears as profit
"attributable to non-controlling interests" — the share of profit belonging to outsiders who own
part of a subsidiary. In 2025 that outside share was US$131m of the group's US$361m of profit.

The second thinning is that the outsiders' half does not stay in the group. Golden Energy Mines pays
out almost everything it earns — half a billion dollars declared for 2023 and again for 2024 — and
49% of every payment walks out of the door to its own minority shareholders. Counting the minority
holders of every subsidiary, of which Golden Energy Mines is much the largest, just over one billion
dollars of cash left the group that way in 2020–2025. DSSA, over the same six years and
in fact since it listed in 2009, has never paid its own shareholders a single dividend. It did
spend US$484m buying back a fifth of its own shares in 2023, at a price it has never justified in
any filing.

The third thinning is the share register itself. The parent, PT Sinar Mas Tunggal, holds 59.9%. The
company holds another 19.68% in its own treasury from that buyback. What is left for everyone else
is about a fifth of the company, and the amount that actually trades is smaller still. In 2026 the
Indonesia Stock Exchange formally classified the stock as having high shareholding concentration,
which cost it its place in the MSCI and FTSE indices and forced index funds to sell. The share price
fell about 79% from its September 2025 peak — and was down roughly 89% from where it began 2026 at
its low that May — having briefly made DSSA the third most valuable listed company in Indonesia.

What this arithmetic leaves is stark. DSSA's 51% of Golden Energy Mines is worth roughly Rp19
trillion at that subsidiary's own market price. DSSA's own market value in September 2026 was
roughly Rp176 trillion — about nine times the value of the coal stake that generates nearly all its
sales. Golden Energy Mines trades at about ten times earnings with a dividend yield above 20%. DSSA,
which owns it, trades at roughly forty-five times earnings and pays nothing. The gap is the market
paying for what DSSA is building with the coal money: fibre to ten and a half million homes, data
centres, and a geothermal option. Ninety-three percent of the group's 2025 capital spending went
into that build. Whether it earns anything is, as of the FY2025 report, no longer disclosed — the
company allocated profit by business segment in 2022, 2023 and 2024, and stopped doing so in 2025.

Everything in this report is a variation on that structure: the coal is someone else's half, the
cash is going somewhere the accounts no longer describe, and the shareholder at the bottom of the
chain has never been paid.

Where the evidence sits: the ownership chain in §1.1, the dividend record in §1.4 and §7.4, the buyback in
§8.4, the segment disclosure withdrawal in §7.5 and §10.3, the market-price history in §8.1.

Percentages here are on **issued** shares, which is how the company prints them. Because a fifth of
those issued shares sit in the company's own treasury and carry no vote or dividend, the parent's
real economic and voting stake in the shares actually outstanding is closer to **74.6%**. Both the
FY2023 and FY2025 financial statements print 59.90% without that qualification.

Beneath DSSA sit 115 group entities as at FY2025, up from 100 a year earlier. Ten are held
directly; the rest are held through those ten. The four that matter:

| Entity | Held | What it is | Total assets, 31 Dec 2025 |

Two structural points about this tree are worth holding on to.

**The coal is one mine.** Within Golden Energy Mines, PT Borneo Indobara is 55% of group reserves
and the overwhelming majority of production. Its mining licence — a Coal Contract of Work, the older
and more favourable of Indonesia's two coal licence types — runs to 16 February 2036. Reserves at
end-2025 were 954.6 Mt against 57.2 Mt of production, so on a purely arithmetic basis the reserve
base outlasts the licence, but not by a wide margin, and no reserve has been added since 2021 (§2.1).

**A cluster of subsidiaries sits at exactly half.** Six geothermal companies, several data-centre
vehicles and a hosting company all show ownership of precisely 50.000% at end-2025, having been
99%-plus a year earlier, after partnerships with the Philippine geothermal developer First Gen and
with Korean investors. All of them are still fully consolidated — that is, their whole revenue,
costs, assets and debts are added into DSSA's accounts as if wholly owned, with the outside half
shown as a minority interest. A company consolidates a business only when it controls it, and at
exactly 50% control is not obvious. The FY2025 report gives no explanation of why control is
retained. This matters because the growth balance sheet — the data centres and the geothermal
licences the market is paying for — is partly these entities.

## 1.3 History — recap

Three patterns run through that timeline, and they explain most of what the company is today.

**The business is periodically replaced rather than grown.** DSSA has been a captive power utility, a
coal miner, a diversified Australian resources group, and now a coal miner plus a fibre network. Each
transition happened by moving whole businesses in or out — the coal came in through an affiliate
purchase in 2009, the Australian assets left through a related-party transfer in 2023, the founding
power plants left through a related-party sale in 2024. Very little was built from nothing.

**The counterparty is almost always another Sinar Mas company.** The 1997 power contracts, the 2009
coal purchase, the 2023 handover of GEAR, the 2024 sale of the power plants, the 2024 Smartfren sale
and loan — all of them are with parties inside or adjacent to the same family group. Price is
disclosed for the small ones and withheld for the large ones.

**The shape of ownership is managed as actively as the business.** Two stock splits in twenty months,
a buyback of a fifth of the company, a partial reissue of treasury shares the following year, and a
share price that went from Rp12,500 at the start of 2021 to Rp106,200 in September 2025 — Rp4,248 in
post-split terms — and down to Rp1,140 within a year.
Over the same period the company has never distributed a rupiah of profit.

## 1.4 Owner's character

This section reads only from the record — what the owners actually did, in what order.

**They pay themselves through the subsidiary, not the parent.** Golden Energy Mines has declared
half a billion dollars of dividends for each of 2023 and 2024. DSSA collects 51% of that and passes
none of it on. Its stated policy is a payout of "at most 30% of net profit" — a ceiling written into
the 2009 prospectus, not a floor — and the FY2025 report states plainly that no dividend has ever
been paid, citing capital needs at subsidiaries. The parent's 59.9% therefore earns nothing in cash
either; the family's return is the share price and whatever moves inside the group.

**When they do return capital, they choose the mechanism that concentrates ownership.** The
Rp7.4 trillion spent in 2023 bought shares, not dividends. A dividend would have paid 40% of it to
outside holders and left the ownership split untouched. The buyback instead removed a fifth of the
company from circulation and lifted the parent's effective stake from 59.9% to about 75%. Eleven
months later, 24.8 million of those treasury shares (counted after the split) were sold back into the
market for Rp794bn, without needing a shareholder vote — the company said so explicitly.

**Disclosure narrows as the transactions get larger.** The 2009 purchase of a coal holding company
for Rp48.8bn comes with an independent appraiser's valuation range and a fairness opinion. The 2023
transfer of an entire Australian resources group gets one sentence in the annual report's
"Divestment" heading and no price at all. The 2024 sale of the four founding power plants gets two
sentences in two different notes, both without a number. The pattern is consistent and it runs the
wrong way.

**They keep the boards small and shared.** Two of DSSA's own directors sit on the board of Golden
Energy Mines — the 51%-owned subsidiary whose other half belongs to outside shareholders — and DSSA's
president director chaired that subsidiary's board until 2021. The auditors' fee disclosure
specifically excludes that subsidiary and everything under it. The FY2025
board affiliation matrix names only two relationships: the controller himself, and a pair of
directors (Lay Krisnan Cahya, president director, and Daniel Cahya, aged 36) marked as affiliated
without saying how.

**Who "the owner" is has changed on paper.** The FY2021 and FY2022 reports name three brothers —
Franky Oesman Widjaja, Indra Widjaja and Muktar Widjaja — as joint controllers, through a four-step
chain of holding companies. The 2009 prospectus named four brothers. From the FY2024 report the
chain is deleted and one man, Franky Oesman Widjaja, is named alone as ultimate beneficial owner.
Nothing in any filing explains the change. It is almost certainly the same event as the January 2025
related-party break described next, and together they are the most consequential undisclosed fact in
the file.

## 1.5 Related-party transactions

Sinar Mas is a group of hundreds of companies, and DSSA sells coal, chemicals and electricity to
dozens of them. The accounts call these "related-party transactions" — dealings with companies under
the same family ownership, where the price is not set by an open market. The size of that trade, and
the direction it moves, is the thing to watch.

### The January 2025 break

The FY2025 report's related-party note carries this footnote, verbatim: *"Since January 2025, the
above mentioned companies ceased to be related parties due to changes in their ultimate shareholders
and no longer meet the definition of related parties."*

The companies are the Asia Pulp & Paper mills and forestry arms: Indah Kiat, Tjiwi Kimia, Pindo Deli,
Lontar Papyrus, OKI Pulp & Paper, Cakrawala Mega Indah, Arara Abadi and Wirakarya Sakti. Together
they were US$201.5m of related-party revenue in 2024. Remove them and the drop from 12.0% to 5.25%
is fully explained. The coal presumably still goes to the same mills; it simply stopped being
reported as a related-party sale.

No date is given. No new ultimate shareholder is named. No legal mechanism is described. Publicly
available reporting points to a reorganisation putting Asia Pulp & Paper under a different branch of
the family, but nothing in the filings confirms it. This is the same event, seen from the other side,
as the controller changing from three brothers to one (§1.4). Its practical effect is that a
material block of the group's sales moved outside the disclosure perimeter in a single step.

It also recolours the 2024 power-plant sale. DSSA sold its four founding power plants to Indah Kiat
and Pindo Deli on 30 April 2024, at an undisclosed price, and eight months later those two buyers
ceased to be related parties.

## 2.1 Coal

### Product

The group sells thermal coal — the low-grade kind burned in power stations, not the higher-value
coking coal used in steelmaking. Its coal is unusually low in energy content: the main mine's product
runs 4,000–4,200 kilocalories per kilogram as received, against roughly 6,000 for the Australian
benchmark grade. Low-energy coal fetches a lower price per tonne, so the business is a volume
business. Sulphur is low (0.2–1.7%), which is what makes it saleable into China and India where
emissions rules bite.

| Mine | Energy content (kcal/kg, as received) |
|---|---|
| Borneo Indobara (BIB) — the main mine | 4,000–4,200 |
| BSL | 4,500–4,800 |
| KIM | 4,600 |
| TKS | 6,600 |
| PMS / SKS / ASL / EMS | 3,000–3,580 |

Almost none of this is done by DSSA's own people. The whole group employs 2,016 people against
US$2.79bn of revenue. In 2025 it paid US$1.72bn to contractors and suppliers and US$0.10bn to
employees. The mining itself — stripping, digging, hauling — is done by outside contractors, of whom
the two largest are visible only as trade payables at year end: PT Putra Perkasa Abadi (US$104.1m)
and PT Cipta Kridatama (US$77.2m). **No mining services contract is disclosed anywhere in any
filing**, in any year: not the rate, not the term, not the volume commitment. For a business where
contractors are the single largest cost, that is a striking absence.

The FY2025 report gives reserves at the start of 2025 as 1,011.8 Mt and at the end as 954.6 Mt.
The difference is 57.2 Mt — exactly the year's production. Nothing was added in 2025. The same pattern appears in the separately analysed Golden Energy Mines

a large downward revision buried in a subtraction, and the mine in question is 55% of group reserves.

### Process — what it costs to dig

This is where the disclosure stops. **DSSA has never published an average selling price per tonne,
a cash cost per tonne, or a strip ratio — in any annual report, in any financial statement, or in
any of the seven public expose decks, in any year.** The only strip ratio anywhere in the file is a
1:3 to 1:5 range in the 2009 IPO prospectus.

What can be derived is the implied realised price — coal revenue divided by tonnes sold:

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|

| Royalty as % of coal revenue `[calculated]` | n/d | 11.5% | 10.3% | 9.7% | 9.7% | 12.3% | 12.6% |

The cross-check comes from outside this document set. Golden Energy Mines — the subsidiary that
does the mining — discloses both, in its own annual reports, which are **not part of this document
set** and are drawn on here from a separate teardown of that company. For 2025 it reports an average
selling price of **US$44.57** a tonne and a gross margin of **US$14.24** a tonne; after selling costs,
overheads and royalty its all-in cost was US$37.80, leaving under US$7 a tonne before tax and about
US$4.80 after it. DSSA's derived US$43.9 sits within seventy cents of the subsidiary's disclosed
price. That is reassuring for the derivation and damning for the disclosure: the number exists, the
51%-owned subsidiary publishes it, and the listed parent does not.

The royalty line is the one cost that is visible, and it behaves like a tax on revenue at roughly
10–13%. Note that it *rose* as a share of revenue in 2024 and 2025 while prices fell — Indonesia's
2025 royalty reform moved rates onto a scale linked to the government reference price, and the
low-grade bands did not fall as fast as the market.

operating fact in the FY2025 report.

What is not disclosed is what each subscriber pays. **Average revenue per user has never been

or about Rp160,000 a month — but the company has never confirmed it, never split it between internet
and pay-TV, and never disclosed how many customers leave each year.

# 03 — DEMAND

## 3.1 Thermal coal

Demand for the grade DSSA sells is concentrated in a handful of places and is best understood as
demand for cheap heat, not for coal in general.

**China** takes 44% of DSSA's revenue. Chinese power stations blend imported low-grade Indonesian
coal with domestic supply because it is cheap per unit of heat and low in sulphur. That demand is
price-sensitive and policy-sensitive: when domestic Chinese production rises or import quotas
tighten, low-CV Indonesian tonnes are the first thing cut.

**Indonesia itself** takes 44% of group revenue, and 38% of the coal tonnes sold. The state utility
PLN and domestic industry must be supplied
under the domestic market obligation, a rule requiring producers to sell a set share of output at
home at a capped price — currently 25% of output with a US$70 a tonne ceiling for power-station coal.
Because the cap sits above the current market for 4,200-kcal coal, the obligation is not presently
costing the group much; when export prices are high, it costs a great deal.

**India and Korea** have collapsed as destinations, down 86% and 91% respectively since 2022. Japan
and Europe have gone to zero.

The structural question for the next decade is whether coal-burning capacity in Asia keeps growing.
Indonesia's own answer, so far, is yes — new domestic coal plants continue to be built, and the
country's own consumption has been the growth market while exports shrank.

## 3.2 Home internet in Indonesia

Fixed broadband penetration in Indonesia is low — most Indonesians reach the internet through a
mobile phone, not a cable into the house. That is the entire investment case for the fibre business:
a large population, rising incomes, and a household connection rate far below regional peers. DSSA's
network now passes over ten million homes, and roughly one in six of them buys. The incumbent is
Telkom's IndiHome; the challengers are Biznet, First Media and MNC Play. DSSA's own estimate of its
share of the multimedia market has gone from 2–3% to about 10% in four years.

## 3.3 Data centres

The demand here is not Indonesian households but cloud and artificial-intelligence workloads. More
than US$750m of hyperscaler commitments have been announced for Indonesia since 2024, concentrated
in Jakarta and Batam. Indonesian data rules that require certain data to be held in-country add a
floor under domestic demand. The competition is well-capitalised and mostly foreign-backed: DCI
Indonesia, Indonet/EDGE DC, BDx and Princeton Digital. DSSA's 25 small regional facilities are a
different product from those operators' large Jakarta campuses; SMX01 is its first attempt at the
latter.

# 04 — SUPPLY

## 4.1 Indonesian thermal coal

Indonesia is the world's largest exporter of thermal coal, and the supply side is set by government
quota as much as by geology. Each producer's annual output is capped by an approved work plan (RKAB).
For 2026 the national quota was set at about **600 Mt against roughly 790 Mt actually produced in
2025** — a cut of nearly a quarter — with about 580 Mt approved by mid-year and industry lobbying for
more; a presidential directive in March 2026 partly reversed it. DSSA produced 57.2 Mt in 2025,
about 7.2% of the national total.

A volume cut hurts a producer like this more than a price cut, because the cost base does not shrink
in proportion. Contractor mobilisation, port throughput commitments and overhead are largely fixed
within a year.

**Royalties.** Government Regulation 19/2025 moved coal royalties onto a scale linked to the
government reference price. For coal at or below 4,200 kcal — which is nearly all of DSSA's output —
the rate runs 5–9% depending on the price band. The main mine, Borneo Indobara, holds a Coal Contract
of Work rather than a mining business licence, and instead hands the government a contractual 13.5%
share of the coal it produces, taken in value. That
older regime is more expensive but also more legally secure, and it is why the group's royalty line
sits above the statutory scale.

**Export pricing.** From 1 March 2025 the government reference price (HBA) became a mandatory floor
for export contracts, not merely a royalty base. In early September 2026 the 4,100-kcal reference
price was US$65.06 a tonne while spot 4,200-kcal coal out of South Kalimantan was US$72.00.

**A divergence worth watching.** In September 2026 the high-energy Newcastle benchmark reached about
US$150 a tonne, near a two-year high, and was widely reported as a coal rally. In the same
government price announcement, the 4,100 and 3,400-kcal reference prices both *fell*. The rally is in
a grade DSSA does not sell. Its own price has been broadly flat to soft while the headline number
rose.

## 4.2 Geothermal supply

Indonesia holds among the world's largest geothermal resources and develops them slowly. Returns on
geothermal projects have been running at 8–9% against a cost of capital of 10–12%, and in 2025 the
government offered ten geothermal working areas and received **zero bids**. That is the context for
DSSA's 440 MW ambition: the resource is real, the economics have not worked for anyone, and a
partner with operating experience is a necessary but not sufficient condition.

## 4.3 Fibre and data centres

Supply of fibre in Indonesia is expanding fast and competitively; the April 2026 merger with
Moratelindo is a consolidation move in a market where several operators are all passing homes at
once. Data-centre supply is being added by better-capitalised specialists, and DSSA's advantage — if
it has one — is cheap power and the group's own land, not scale.

# 05 — KEY VARIABLES

These are the things that actually move the numbers, in order of weight.

**1. The realised price of 4,200-kcal coal — about 89% of revenue.** Every dollar per tonne on
57 Mt is US$57m of revenue and, because the cost base is largely fixed within a year, close to
US$57m of pre-tax profit. Implied realised price has run from US$32.5 (2020) to US$111.5 (2022) to
US$43.9 (2025). At the subsidiary's own disclosed economics — roughly US$14 of gross margin and under
US$5 of net profit per tonne in 2025 — a ten-dollar move in either direction changes the coal
business from comfortably profitable to marginal or from marginal to very good. **This is the single
most important variable and the company does not disclose its own position on it.**

**2. Coal volume, set by government quota.** 57.2 Mt in 2025 against a national quota cut to about
600 Mt for 2026 from 790 Mt of 2025 output. A quota cut is worse than a price cut because the
contractor and port cost block does not deflate with it.

**3. The share of coal profit that belongs to outsiders — 49% of Golden Energy Mines.** Fixed by
ownership, and it is why group profit of US$361m becomes US$231m for DSSA's own holders. Any change
in that stake changes attributable earnings by more than any plausible operating improvement.

**4. Fibre take-up rate.** One in six homes passed currently buys. Each percentage point of take-up
on 10.5 million homes is about 105,000 subscribers, worth roughly US$12m of annual revenue at the
derived average bill. The build has already been paid for; take-up is what turns it into profit.

**5. The cost of borrowing, and the currency it is borrowed in.** Debt is now US$1.48bn, 78% of it
in rupiah, with US$896m of it on floating rates. The company's own sensitivity disclosure puts a
one-percentage-point rate move at about US$5.0m of pre-tax profit. Separately the group runs a net
liability position in foreign currency of US$1.19bn, so a weaker rupiah against the dollar helps the
reported dollar figures and a stronger one hurts.

**6. Whether the digital segment earns anything.** In 2024, the last year it was disclosed, the
segment made US$22.8m of pre-tax profit on US$144.1m of revenue — a 16% margin, before the far larger
2025 spending. For 2025 the company withdrew segment profitability entirely. With 93% of capital
spending going here, this is the variable the market is trading and nobody can currently measure.

**7. Government royalty and domestic-obligation policy.** Royalty already takes 12.6% of coal
revenue and rose as prices fell. The domestic price cap of US$70 a tonne is above the current market
and therefore harmless today; it becomes expensive in any price recovery.

# 06 — VICISSITUDES

What past shocks did to this company, and what a repeat would do.

**The 2020 trough.** Coal at an implied US$32.5 a tonne, plus the loss of most power revenue when
75% of the independent power vehicle went to Datang, produced a **net loss of US$57.9m** — the group's
only loss year in the period. Operating profit was still positive at US$173.6m; the loss came from
US$179.1m of other expenses, including a US$70.1m loss on disposing of a subsidiary. That is the
template for a bad year: the operating business survives a low price, and one-off items below the
operating line do the damage.

**The 2022 boom.** Coal at US$111.5 a tonne and the Australian assets consolidated took revenue to
US$5.96bn and group profit to US$1.30bn. Less than half of that profit — US$590m — belonged to DSSA's
own shareholders; US$708m belonged to minorities. Even at the top of the biggest coal cycle in
decades, most of the profit was someone else's, and none of it was distributed.

**The 2023 restructuring.** Handing the GEAR group to a related party halved total assets from
US$6.50bn to US$3.06bn in one step and put a US$618m debit into equity. Revenue fell 16% and profit
33%.

**The 2026 de-rating.** The share price fell about 79% from its September 2025 peak, and about 89%
from the start of 2026 to its low that May. The cause was not earnings — 2025 profit was down 33% and
the price fell four fifths. It was the exchange
classifying the stock as too concentrated, which removed it from the MSCI and FTSE indices and forced
passive funds to sell into a market with very little genuine float. This is the specific risk of a
company with 80% of its shares locked up: the index membership that drove the price up in 2025 was
itself contingent on a float the company had spent Rp7.4 trillion reducing.

**What a repeat of each would do now.**

| Shock | 2025 exposure | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Coal back to US$32/t | 89% of revenue, no disclosed cost cushion | Coal gross margin, at roughly US$14/t in 2025, goes to zero or below on the subsidiary's own cost base. Group net profit would not survive it. |
| Quota cut to 45 Mt | volume-driven business, fixed cost block | Roughly US$0.5bn of revenue, most of it margin |
| Rupiah weakens 10% | US$1.19bn net foreign-currency liability | Reported dollar figures flattered; rupiah debt service unchanged in local terms |
| Rates up 1% | US$896m floating | ~US$5.0m of pre-tax profit |
| Digital build disappoints | US$490.6m of construction in progress, 47% of net plant | An impairment of unknown size on assets whose project list has never been published |

what was paid and what the assets were carried at is put straight into equity. The FY2023 note gives
the number and says nothing else: no counterparty, no price, no date, no computation. It is the GEAR
handover to Duchess Avenue, and it is still sitting there unchanged in 2025.

book value goes to equity rather than profit. No filing names the subsidiary or the transactions.

and a further US$49.1m was written off through other comprehensive income. Neither passed through the
income statement. A sale of the founding business at an undisclosed price produced no visible gain or
loss anywhere.

**The general reserve.** Indonesian law requires a company to build a reserve of 20% of paid-up
capital, about US$14.5m here. DSSA's general reserve is US$1.3m and grows by exactly US$100,000 a
year. At that rate it reaches the statutory level in the twenty-second century. Every annual report
states the requirement and the balance side by side with no comment.

# 09 — IMPORTANT TRANSACTIONS

## 9.1 The 2009 listing and the coal that arrived with it

At its IPO, DSSA was a captive power company: four plants inside two Sinar Mas paper mills, 300 MW
and 1,336 tonnes an hour of steam, on 25-year contracts signed in 1997 where the customer supplies
the fuel and the water and DSSA earns a capacity fee plus operating charges. In the five months to
May 2009, **99.30% of its revenue came from related parties**, and 45.67% of it from PT Sinar Mas
Tunggal — the company that was in the process of taking control.

Alongside the offering, DSSA bought a coal holding company from an affiliate for Rp48.8bn, which
converted a 42.63% minority stake into control of Borneo Indobara — the mine that is still 55% of
group reserves seventeen years later. The independent appraiser valued the target at Rp44.8–51.8bn
and the Rp1,000,000-per-share price sat inside that range. **But the price was fixed in a binding
agreement on 23 June 2009 and 10% was paid on 27 April 2009 — four and six months before the
valuation and fairness opinion, both dated 9 November 2009.** The transaction was declared an
affiliated transaction but not a conflict-of-interest one, so no independent shareholder vote was
required.

Also at IPO: DSSA had guaranteed **US$300m** of an affiliate's China Development Bank loan — more
than its own total liabilities and roughly equal to its entire equity. The parent gave an undertaking
to assume it, dated the same 9 November 2009, with the formal release still described as in process.

## 9.2 The BHP Mitsui Coal acquisition, 2021–2022

On 8 November 2021 Stanmore Resources — an Australian company then controlled by DSSA through GEAR
— agreed to buy 100% of Dampier Coal, which owned 80% of BHP Mitsui Coal, from BHP for **up to
US$1.35bn**. Funding came from a rights issue that raised about AU$694m and a **US$625m** senior
secured acquisition facility from credit funds advised by Värde Partners, Canyon Capital and Farallon
Capital Asia, secured over everything Stanmore's acquisition vehicle owned. No interest rate is
disclosed anywhere. GEAR issued US$90m of 8.5% senior secured notes purely to take up its rights.
Shareholders approved on 23 February 2022.

The chain of exposure ran DSSA → GEAR → Golden Investments (Australia) → Stanmore → the acquisition
vehicle → Dampier → 80% of BHP Mitsui Coal. Eighteen months later the whole chain left the group.

Worth noting as an internal contradiction: the FY2021 report describes this transaction as requiring
shareholder approval under the material-transaction rules, and the same report's "material
transactions" summary states that no material transactions occurred in 2021.

## 9.3 The 2023 split of the group

Covered in §8.3. The essential facts: EGM approval on 2 May 2023, execution on 10 August 2023,
Golden Energy Mines pulled up to DSSA in kind, GEAR and everything else handed to Duchess Avenue, a
related party, at an undisclosed price, leaving a US$618m debit in equity. Total assets halved. The
buyback started five days later.

## 9.4 The 2024 sale of the power plants

DSSA sold the four plants it had built inside Indah Kiat's and Pindo Deli's mills back to Indah Kiat
and Pindo Deli on 30 April 2024. The disclosure is two sentences in two notes, in both cases without
a price, a carrying value or a gain. The plants had been revalued upward in the restatement booked
one year earlier. Power segment revenue went from US$40.2m in 2023 to US$9.7m in 2024 to zero.
Parent-company headcount fell from 531 to **37**, with 457 resignations.

## 9.7 The restatement that appeared just before the bond programme

In the FY2023 report the company restated both the opening and closing 2022 balance sheets and the
2021 comparatives. The largest piece was a revaluation of the power plants — an upward revision of

performed in **2017, 2019 and 2021** but only recorded in the accounts in 2023. It added US$83,990,848
to a revaluation reserve inside equity at both dates, and it pushed a small extra depreciation charge
of US$5.4m (2022) and US$4.4m (2021) into cost of sales, cutting 2022 profit attributable to owners
from US$595.3m to US$589.9m. Two smaller items reversed capitalised interest and adjusted the fair
value of convertible bonds.

Two things about the timing are worth noting without asserting a motive. The valuations were up to
six years old when they were booked. And they were booked in the year DSSA prepared its first bond
and sukuk shelf programme, which requires a credit rating — the rating agency's view of a company's
assets being one input to it. Fifteen months later the revalued plants were sold, and the reserve the
restatement created was moved straight to retained earnings without passing through profit (§7.3).

# 10 — RED FLAG

## 10.1 The largest disposals carry no price, and all went to related parties

The GEAR transfer, the power-plant sale and the Smartfren sale together removed the majority of what
DSSA was, and moved it to parties inside or adjacent to the family group. Between them they generated
a US$618m equity debit, a US$111m reserve transfer straight to retained earnings, a US$66m realised
loss routed around the income statement, and a net US$417m cash outflow. **The two largest — the GEAR
group and the power plants — carry no disclosed consideration at all.** The Smartfren sale, much the
smallest, is the only one priced.

The specific concern is not that related-party sales happened — in a group this size they are
unavoidable — but that the disclosure is inversely proportional to size. A Rp48.8bn purchase in 2009
came with an appraiser's range and a fairness opinion. A transfer that halved the balance sheet came
with one sentence. An outside shareholder cannot tell whether value left the company or entered it.

## 10.2 Half a billion dollars lent to two companies nobody can identify

At the end of 2025, "other receivables from third parties" stood at **US$531,326,897** — larger than
the group's entire trade receivable book. Two names make up 98% of it:

| Borrower | Amount | % of balance |
|---|---:|---:|
| PT Pintar Nusantara Sejahtera | US$432.0m | 81.3% |
| PT Media Utama Sejahtera | US$90.4m | 17.0% |

The only description given is that these are loan facilities used to fund satellite projects. The
interest rate is not disclosed. No security is disclosed. Neither borrower is identified anywhere in
any filing — not as a related party, not as a subsidiary, not as an associate. The balance is
classified as **current**, meaning due within a year, yet it grows rather than being collected. The
allowance for possible non-payment is US$157,908 — three hundredths of one percent. The US$407.5m
increase in 2024 was placed in investing cash flow, where a loan belongs, rather than operating.

## 10.3 Segment profitability withdrawn in the year it mattered most

The company disclosed profit by business segment in 2022, 2023 and 2024. In 2025 it stopped. The
withdrawal coincides exactly with the year the digital segment absorbed 93% of capital spending, and
with the year the company began asking the market to value it as an infrastructure business rather
than a coal miner. There is no accounting standard that required the change. The information existed
a year earlier.

Compounding it: the segment column labelled "steam and electricity" in the FY2025 report carries
**US$2.66bn of assets and US$668m of liabilities against zero revenue**. It is functionally the
holding company's own investment book, filed under the name of a business that no longer exists.

## 10.4 A US$525m facility to the buyer of your own assets, on the day of the sale

Covered in §9.5. To restate the shape: DSSA sold an asset for Rp562bn and simultaneously extended
the buyer credit of up to fifteen times that amount, convertible into the buyer's equity, with no
disclosed rate, no disclosed security, and no place in the fair-value tables. Twenty months later
DSSA is reported to have bought the borrower. The economic substance may be entirely benign — a
staged acquisition financed by vendor credit — but the disclosure does not permit an outside
shareholder to reach that conclusion, and the counterparty is a related party.

## 10.5 The coal business discloses no price and no cost

Seventeen years, five annual reports, seven investor decks, and not one average selling price, cash
cost or strip ratio for the business that is 89% of revenue. No customer is named for US$1.21bn of
sales to China. The one long-term supply contract that was ever named was terminated and replaced
with a contract to a related entity of the same buyer on the same day, with no volumes or prices for
either. The subsidiary that actually mines the coal, Golden Energy Mines, discloses its selling price
and its cost. The parent does not.

## 10.6 A cluster of subsidiaries at exactly 50.000%, still consolidated

Fourteen entities — six geothermal, several data-centre, and a hosting company whose assets grew
nearly tenfold in a year — moved from 99%-plus to precisely 50.000% during 2025 and remain fully
consolidated. No basis for retaining control is disclosed for any of them. US$84,107,522 was credited
to minority interests as capital paid in by outside shareholders, and it is never linked to a named
entity. If control is not in fact retained, a meaningful part of the growth balance sheet does not
belong on it.

## 10.7 Two hundred million dollars of sales left the disclosure perimeter in one step

The January 2025 reclassification of the Asia Pulp & Paper companies as unrelated (§1.5) removed
US$201.5m of related-party revenue from view without any change in the underlying trade. No date, no
new owner, no mechanism is given. It is the same undisclosed event as the change in who is named as
DSSA's controller. Whatever happened, an investor cannot see it, and it determines the boundary of
every related-party disclosure that follows.

## 10.8 Smaller things that add up

- **Goodwill fell exactly US$13,400,000 in 2021** and the FY2021 report contains no goodwill note at
  all.
- **The main mine's probable reserves print as negative 19.7 Mt** in the FY2025 report.
- **The data-centre capacity is stated as both 40 MW and 10 MW in the same annual report.**
- **The FY2022 balance sheet exists in two published versions about US$66m apart**, never reconciled.
- **The 600 MW of independent power plants** — the largest equity-accounted asset outside coal, worth
  US$173.8m and earning US$11.7m — simply stop being described in the investor decks after 2024,
  while remaining on the balance sheet.
- **Covenant levels have never been quantified**, on any facility, in any year, since 2009.
- **The general reserve is at 9% of its statutory minimum** and grows US$100,000 a year.
- Two directors held small share positions at the start of a year and zero at the end, unexplained,
  in two different years.
- **Two different names and two different amounts** are given in the FY2024 and FY2025 reports for the
  same July 2024 convertible loan (Kira SG One for US$15.5m; KB Securities for US$3.0m).

# MISSING DATA AND OPEN ITEMS

Ranked by how much the answer would change the analysis.

**1. What DSSA received for the GEAR group, the power plants, the Smartfren stake and the tower
company.** None of the four carries a disclosed price. Together they are most of the company's
former asset base. *Where it likely exists:* the information disclosures filed with the exchange on
10 August 2023 and around 30 April 2024, and the EGM materials for the 2 May 2023 meeting.
*What it would decide:* whether hundreds of millions of dollars of value left the company to related
parties, or entered it.

**2. What actually changed in January 2025 to make the Asia Pulp & Paper companies unrelated, and to
change the named controller from three Widjaja brothers to one.** *Where it likely exists:* the same
question is answerable from Indah Kiat's and Tjiwi Kimia's own FY2024 and FY2025 related-party notes,
which are public, and from any group reorganisation announcement. *What it would decide:* the entire
related-party perimeter, and whether the power plants were sold to a party that was about to stop
being related.

**3. Coal average selling price and cash cost per tonne, by year, for DSSA's mines.** *Where it
exists:* Golden Energy Mines' own annual reports disclose both. Pulling five years of GEMS filings
would let the coal segment be modelled properly instead of derived. *What it would decide:* whether
the 2025 margin compression is price, cost or mix, and how close the business is to break-even.

**4. Segment profit for the digital business in 2025 and 2026.** Withdrawn from the FY2025 report.
*Where it may appear:* the merged Moratelindo entity is separately listed and will report its own
numbers from 2026. *What it would decide:* whether roughly Rp157 trillion of DSSA's market value is
attached to a profitable business or a loss-making one.

**5. Who PT Pintar Nusantara Sejahtera and PT Media Utama Sejahtera are, and on what terms they
borrowed US$522m.** *Where it might exist:* Indonesian company registry filings; the exchange
disclosures for affiliated transactions if they are affiliates. *What it would decide:* whether 12%
of total assets is a genuine third-party credit exposure or a related-party placement.

**6. The basis on which fourteen 50.000%-owned entities are consolidated.** *Where it should be:* the
accounting policy note or Note 1; it is in neither. *What it would decide:* how much of the
geothermal and data-centre balance sheet is really DSSA's.

**7. What the US$490.6m of construction in progress is.** Project names, completion percentages and
expected dates are all absent. *What it would decide:* the timing of when 47% of net plant starts
earning, and the impairment risk if it does not.

**8. Covenant thresholds on US$1.48bn of debt.** Never quantified in any year. *What it would decide:*
how much headroom exists if coal prices fall again while the digital build continues.

**9. The interest rate, security and conversion terms on the US$400m Bali Media facility**, and the
terms of the reported Rp4 trillion acquisition of that company in July 2026. *Where it exists:* the
exchange disclosure for the July 2026 acquisition. *What it would decide:* whether the largest new
asset on the balance sheet is worth what it is carried at.

**10. First-half 2026 results.** Not in this document set. *What it would decide:* whether the 2025
margin compression continued, what the merged fibre business contributes, and how the debt has moved
since December 2025.

**11. Reclamation and mine-closure provision amounts.** The legal obligation is described in detail
every year; the amount is never given. Only US$32.5m of undifferentiated restricted funds appears.

**12. Average revenue per user, churn, and fibre network length for the internet business** — never
disclosed in any filing or deck.

**13. The mining services contracts.** Contractors are the largest single cost in the coal business
and no contract — rate, term, or volume — is disclosed anywhere.

**14. The composition of "others – net"** in 2020–2023 (US$8.4m, US$37.5m, US$40.3m, US$12.9m), and
the US$35–52m of "other income" that appears in the segment note but not in the expense note for 2024
and 2025.

**15. A published sum-of-the-parts valuation.** None exists in any source reached; the one broker note
found (KB Valbury, 29 July 2026) is explicitly non-rated and offers none.
