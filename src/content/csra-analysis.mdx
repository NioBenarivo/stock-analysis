**What it does:** grows oil palm on ~20,900 Ha of nucleus estates in North and South Sumatra, mills fresh fruit bunches (FFB) into crude palm oil (CPO) and palm kernel (PK) at three mills, and sells 100% domestically to rotating third-party refiners. Founded 1983, commercial operations since 1995, listed January 2020 (offer effective 30 Dec 2019) at Rp125/share.

**Market cap:** ~Rp1.48tn at ~Rp760/share (stockanalysis.com snapshot cached ~24 Jun 2026 — the only price found; treat as approximate). P/E ~5.6×, P/B ~0.9–1.0×, EV/EBITDA ~3.9× trailing [calculated]. FY2025: revenue Rp1,889bn (+77%), net income Rp268.8bn (+25%).

**Period covered:** FY2016–FY2025 audited, plus 1Q26 fragments from the May 2026 public expose deck. FY2019 and FY2020 figures come from comparative columns and five-year highlight tables inside later filings (no standalone AR2019/AR2020 was in the source set — see Sources).

**Sources in hand:** IPO prospectus (342pp), annual reports FY2021–FY2025 (376–420pp each), public expose decks May 2022–May 2026 (covering FY2021–FY2025), English press releases FY2021/22/24/25, web research pass 19 Aug 2026. Full table at the end.

## THE ONE THING TO UNDERSTAND FIRST

**The +77% FY2025 revenue growth that makes CSRA screen as the cheapest grower in the IDX palm universe is not plantation growth. It is bought fruit.**

CSRA's own estates produced 354,290 t of FFB in FY2025 — up just 10.0% on FY2024, on a yield of 19.2 t/Ha that is *below* the 20.4 t/Ha the same estates managed in FY2021. What changed is the mill network: PKS-2 (Oct 2023) and PKS-3 (June 2025) tripled the company from one mill to three, and to feed them CSRA bought **189,195 t of third-party FFB in FY2025 — 5.6× the prior year's volume — at a cost of Rp720.2bn, which is 58.5% of the entire group's cost of sales** [calculated, Note 26 AR2025]. Purchased fruit went from a rounding error to the single largest cost item of the company in one year.

The economics of that fruit are thin at best. At FY2025 average prices, one kg of purchased FFB yields ~Rp3,489 of CPO+PK output value [calculated: 20.7% OER × Rp14,061 + 4.7% KER × Rp12,296] against a stated average purchase price of Rp3,207/kg plus ~Rp134/kg of milling cost — a margin of roughly Rp150/kg, ~4% of output value. Using the *implied* purchase price from the cash numbers (Rp720.2bn ÷ 189,195 t = Rp3,807/kg) the bought-fruit book processed at a **loss**. The two figures cannot both be right (§3.5 shows the arithmetic); either way, the marginal revenue Rupiah in FY2025 earned a fraction of what the estate Rupiah earns. This is why gross margin collapsed from 45.4% to 34.8% and net margin from 20.1% to 14.2% in the best CPO price year since 2022.

So the correct reading of FY2025 is: a good, mid-yielding ~18,000-Ha mature estate business earning ~Rp2,000/kg on its own fruit, wrapped in a fast-growing, low-margin fruit-conversion business that now decides the group's reported growth rate and margin. The 5.6× P/E is not pricing a 77% grower; it is pricing the estate engine at mid-cycle plus a milling wrapper (§3.5, §3.7).

And while the market was looking at the growth, the register quietly closed: during FY2025 the public float fell from 20.0% to **6.71%** — a Rp79.7bn treasury buyback (4.64% of the company at avg ~Rp838/share, 6.7× the IPO price) plus absorption of street stock into eight national institutions that now hold 84.97% of the register. The founding families' holdcos own 80%; the board that runs the company paid itself Rp142.8bn in aggregate remuneration over FY2021–25 — nearly as much as the Rp177.5bn of dividends paid to *all* shareholders in the same period [calculated] (§1.5). Minority economics and minority liquidity are both shrinking. Everything else in this report — the director lending club (§1.4), the disclosure quality (30 catalogued internal contradictions, §3.8), the Rp604.5bn plasma lawsuit dismissed only on procedural grounds (§2.2) — has to be read against that backdrop.

The formative fact in the history is the pre-IPO roll-up. Every operating subsidiary except DAL was bought from the company's own controlling individuals in common-control transactions. The consideration was token cash against subsidiaries whose *book equity was negative at acquisition* — AR2021 Note 21 shows the mechanics: SSG bought for Rp249.5m cash against book value of **negative Rp53.2bn**; ABI Rp249.0m against negative Rp20.9bn; BKL Rp198.0m against negative Rp24.9bn; SKL's purchases generated Rp157.6bn of restructuring difference. The cumulative SNTRES of **negative Rp265.5bn** sits permanently in additional paid-in capital, which is why CSRA reports **APIC of −Rp259.6bn** to this day (−265.5bn SNTRES + 3.6bn net IPO premium + 2.3bn tax amnesty). Reported book equity is therefore depressed by the accounting of the family's own pre-IPO restructuring — the same PSAK 38 pattern seen at SSMS, though smaller and with the listco as buyer of negative-equity greenfields rather than seller.

## 1.4 Related party transactions

CSRA is unusual in the IDX palm peer set in that it discloses **zero commercial related-party transactions** — no CPO/FFB/fertiliser sales or purchases with affiliates in any year FY2016–FY2025. RPT penetration of revenue and COGS: 0.0% every year. There is no SSMS-style affiliated-offtaker issue here.

What there is instead is a **director lending club** running through the balance sheet for a decade:

**The core item: Rp33.225bn owed to the President Director, interest-free, since 2015.** Gita Sapta Adi personally lent Rp33.225bn to BKL (loan agreement 5 Jan 2015) — interest-free, unsecured, repayable only "after BKL benefits from its business activities." BKL has never operated. The balance has been identical for ten consecutive year-ends (FY2016–FY2025), extended at least three times. It is, functionally, quasi-equity from the President Director propping a dormant landholding entity — and the Bank Mandiri covenants **prohibit repaying shareholder debt** while they bind, so it cannot be repaid without bank consent anyway. In FY2025 the class grew to Rp37.225bn via a new Rp4.0bn interest-free loan from Erwin Kurniawan to DAL (2 Jan 2025).

**The revolving 6% loans, both directions.** A near-annual cycle of personal loans between the company and its two most senior executives, each settled and re-originated within a year or two:

None of this is large against a Rp2.5tn balance sheet — the point is the pattern: the listed company functions as a standing liquidity facility for the two executives who control it.

**The guarantees.** Gita Sapta Adi and Seman Sendjaja personally guarantee essentially every bank facility of the parent and every subsidiary — ~Rp765bn at FY2025 — and have since the pre-IPO syndicate (which also pledged the family's CSRA shares). This cuts both ways: it aligns them existentially with solvency, and it entangles company credit with two private balance sheets that are themselves borrowing from and lending to the company.

# 02 — MODEL

## 2.1 Product

Three products, one plant:

- **CPO (crude palm oil)** — 75.6% of FY2025 revenue. Commodity-grade, sold FOB/loco mill to domestic refiners.
- **Palm kernel (PK)** — 14.6%. The nut, sold to domestic kernel crushers (CSRA has no crushing plant).
- **FFB (raw fruit)** — 9.8%. Fruit sold unprocessed to other companies' mills — the legacy channel from when CSRA had one mill.

| CPO | 331.5 | 446.5 | 431.8 | 513.1 | 716.6 | 1,428.2 |
| Palm kernel | 41.3 | 70.7 | 65.2 | 56.2 | 98.4 | 276.8 |

The table is the business model transition in one panel. Through FY2022 CSRA was **as much a fruit farmer as an oil producer** — in FY2022 it sold more raw fruit (180,866 t) than it milled (174,180 t), because its single ageing mill couldn't absorb the estates' output. Each mill added since converts fruit revenue (sold at ~Rp2,200–3,200/kg) into CPO+PK revenue (worth ~Rp3,500/kg of fruit equivalent at FY2025 prices) — capturing the conversion margin on fruit it previously gave away to other millers. That part of the story is genuinely value-accretive. The part that is not is §2.3: the mills are now bigger than the estates.

**The business model in one paragraph:** CSRA grows fruit on ~17,600 Ha of mature nucleus estates at mid-pack yields, mills its own fruit plus a rapidly growing volume of purchased third-party fruit at three mills (nameplate 105–120 t/hr — the filings disagree, §2.4), and sells CPO and kernel entirely domestically to a rotating cast of large refiners (Musim Mas, Wilmar, Sari Dumai/Apical, Pacific Palmindo) with no offtake contracts, at prices that track the Indonesian domestic CPO market (= world price minus the export levy/duty wedge). It is a price-taker on both ends: FFB purchase prices are set by provincial formula/competition, CPO sale prices by tender.

Age profile of mature nucleus, FY2025: 4–7 yrs **2,019 Ha (11.4%)** · 8–17 yrs (prime) **11,337 Ha (64.3%)** · >18 yrs **4,288 Ha (24.3%)**. This is a decent age book: nearly two-thirds prime, and the >18-yr tail (4,288 Ha) is stable rather than growing because replanting is running continuously (immature area at a record 3,292 Ha). Contrast NSSS's 2030 replanting wall or AALI's ageing estate — CSRA does not have an age-profile cliff this decade. The soft spot is the young cohort: only 2,019 Ha aged 4–7 means organic production growth from maturation over the next 3–4 years is modest (~11% of mature area upgrading from young to prime yields), which is precisely why growth had to come from mills and bought fruit instead. (Note: the May-2026 deck's age chart for "Dec 2025" actually sums to FY2024's mature area — a reused/mislabeled chart, one of the 30 catalogued inconsistencies.)

Plasma is minimal — 910 Ha against 20,936 nucleus (4.3%), ten cooperatives, plasma receivables Rp71.3bn and rising every year. The 20%-plasma obligation era post-dates most of CSRA's plantings; but note that CSRA's single largest legal threat (§2.2 below) comes from its plasma partner, and the receivable's steady growth (Rp28.5bn → 71.3bn over six years) with no impairment discussion is itself worth watching.

## 2.4 Process — how the product is made and what it costs

### The physical process

Oil palm yields fruit year-round from year ~4, peaking years 8–17. Harvested FFB must reach a mill within ~24–48 hours before free-fatty-acid buildup degrades the oil. At the mill: sterilise (steam), thresh, press → **CPO** (20.7% of FFB weight at CSRA's FY2025 OER) plus **kernel** (4.7% KER); fibre and shell fire the boilers, empty bunches return to the fields. The 24–48h clock is why mills anchor estates and why fruit is bought from a tight radius: FFB is a perishable, local commodity while CPO is a storable, global one. Owning the mill converts the perishable local price into the global price — that spread is the milling margin CSRA has spent Rp700bn+ of capex to capture.

Extraction metrics by year: OER 20.1 (2019) → 20.3 → 20.5 → 21.2 → 21.0 → **20.7% (2025)**; KER 4.5 → 4.9 → 5.2 → 4.9 → 4.4 → 4.7%. OER dipped in FY2025 despite new equipment — consistent with a rising share of bought-in fruit (external fruit is typically less ripe/lower quality than own-estate fruit; the FY2025 dip landed exactly when purchases jumped 5.6×).

**Mill capacity — the filings disagree with each other.** AR2025 prose: PKS-1 45 + PKS-2 45 + PKS-3 30 = 120 t/hr. The company's own May-2026 deck: PKS-1 45 + PKS-2 **30** + PKS-3 30 = **105 t/hr**. The decks have shown PKS-2 at 30 t/hr since FY2023 (vs the 45 originally guided and still claimed in ARs). Never reconciled. At 105 t/hr × ~20hr × ~300 days ≈ 630kt annual capacity vs 502kt processed → utilisation ~80% [calculated, rough]; at 120 t/hr, ~70%. Utilisation itself is never disclosed.

## 2.6 Key variables — what actually moves the model

**1. Domestic CPO price (≈76% of revenue).** Sensitivity: ±Rp1,000/kg on ~102kt sold = ±Rp102bn revenue ≈ ±27% of FY2025 pretax profit [calculated]. The domestic price = world price − export levy/duty wedge; both legs move. FY2025 realised Rp14,061; Aug-2026 KPB North Sumatra tender ~Rp15,428 — the current year is running *above* FY2025's average.

**2. Bought-in FFB share and spread (58.5% of COGS).** The new dominant cost. The spread between per-kg output value (OER×CPO + KER×PK ≈ Rp3,489 at FY2025 prices) and the FFB purchase price (Rp3,207 stated / Rp3,807 implied — unresolved, §3.5) is the entire margin on 37.7% of throughput. FFB purchase prices are set by provincial formula off the CPO price with a lag, so the spread compresses when CPO falls fast and expands when it rises — a leveraged, unhedged basis position that did not exist two years ago.

**3. Own-estate yield (the profit engine).** 19.2 t/Ha FY2025, range 18.2–21.2 over five years. Each ±1 t/Ha on 17,644 mature Ha = ±17.6kt FFB ≈ ±Rp61bn of revenue at FFB value, nearly all of it margin [calculated] — weather (El Niño 2023 → the FY2024 trough), Ganoderma, and estate age drive it. Management has missed its own yield target **five years out of five**.

**4. OER (20.7%).** ±0.5pp OER on 502kt processed = ±2.5kt CPO ≈ ±Rp35bn revenue at FY2025 prices [calculated]. Bought fruit dilutes it.

**5. The policy wedge (levy + duty + mandates).** Levy raised to a flat 12.5% of reference price Mar-2026, explicitly to fund B40/B50 biodiesel subsidies via BPDPKS. Every levy increase transfers margin from upstream producers (CSRA) to biodiesel blenders; every mandate increase (B40 Jan-2025; B50 launched 1 Jul 2026 per trade press — though CSRA's own May-2026 deck still described B50 as postponed; unresolved at source level) tightens domestic supply and lifts the domestic price. Net effect on a domestic-only seller like CSRA has recently been positive (demand pull > levy drag, since CSRA doesn't pay the levy directly).

**6. Finance costs (Rp57.4bn, 8.25–8.50% on Rp765bn).** Interest cover 7.3× — comfortable now; the variable matters because every facility is floating-ish (periodically repriced) Bank Mandiri credit, cross-defaulted and cross-collateralised across the whole group, with two personal guarantors.

**7. The Tondi Bersama claim (binary).** Rp604.5bn + 5,000 producing Ha at risk if ever re-filed and lost on the merits — vs zero provision. Not a P&L variable until it suddenly is.

## 2.7 Demand

Indonesian domestic consumption is the only market that matters to CSRA (100% domestic sales). GAPKI FY2025: domestic consumption 24.7m t (+3.8%), of which **biodiesel 12.7m t (+11.0%) — now more than half of domestic use** — and food 9.8m t (−3.6%, being crowded out by fuel). Biodiesel demand is policy-manufactured: B40 (Jan 2025) → B50 (launched 1 Jul 2026 per ESDM decree of 26 Jun 2026 and trade press; company deck of May 2026 said postponed — flagging both). Each 10-point blend increment absorbs roughly 3–4m t of additional CPO domestically. For a domestic-only seller, the mandate escalation is a structural demand tailwind that has already shown up in CSRA's realised prices (Rp10,821 → 12,793 → 14,061 over 2023–25, +30% in two years while the Malaysian benchmark rose ~10%).

The check on the tailwind: the subsidy math. BPDPKS pays the biodiesel gap out of levy receipts (~Rp35.5tn budgeted 2025); if CPO prices rise, the gap narrows, but if the program outgrows levy revenue the state either raises the levy (hits exporters, indirectly lifts nothing for CSRA) or caps the mandate. GAPKI itself has warned of a domestic supply squeeze "as early as 2026."

## 2.8 Supply

Indonesia produced 51.6m t of CPO in 2025 (+7.3% — a strong rebound year), ~59% of world palm supply; Malaysia ~19m t. Structural supply is tight: the national tree stock is ageing, smallholder replanting (PSR program) runs far behind target, and the primary-forest moratorium plus the 2025 forest-area regularisation push (PP 45/2025) cap new planting. Exports 32.3m t (+9.5%) still clear at record value (USD35.9bn, +29%).

CSRA's place in this: 103.9kt of CPO = **0.20% of national production** [calculated]. It is a price-taking micro-cap in the world's largest producing country — nothing CSRA does moves its market, and everything its market does moves CSRA. Its supply-side advantages are local, not global: estates in established Sumatra palm belts with dense surrounding smallholder fruit supply (which is precisely what makes the bought-fruit mill model feasible), and a mature-but-not-old age profile that means production holds while several larger peers face replanting cliffs.

## 3.5 Unit economics

### The estate engine (own fruit)

- Own FFB cash cost: Rp1,154/kg; all-in with depreciation Rp1,348/kg (Rp477.5bn ÷ 354,290 t) [calculated].
- Own FFB market value FY2025: ~Rp3,209/kg (achieved raw-sale price) → estate margin ≈ **Rp1,861/kg all-in**.
- Per mature hectare: 19.2 t/Ha yield × Rp1,861/kg ≈ **Rp35.7m/Ha of estate-level gross profit** [calculated]. Against ~Rp99m/Ha of EV per planted hectare (EV Rp2,081bn ÷ 20,936 Ha), the estate alone earns a ~36% gross yield on its EV allocation at FY2025 prices — this is the good business inside CSRA.

### The milling wrapper (bought fruit) — showing the arithmetic

Per kg of purchased FFB at FY2025 averages:

```
 Output value: 0.207 kg CPO × Rp14,061  = Rp2,911
             + 0.047 kg PK  × Rp12,296  = Rp  578

```

**Caveat that changes the answer:** cash paid for third-party FFB per Note 26 was Rp720.2bn for 189,195 t = **Rp3,807/kg implied — 19% above the stated average price**. At Rp3,807 the same arithmetic gives **−Rp452/kg**, i.e. bought fruit processed at a gross loss, cross-subsidised by estate margin. The gap may be timing/inventory or freight-inclusive cost vs farm-gate price; no filing reconciles it. Either the bought-fruit business earns ~4% gross or it loses money — the filings genuinely do not let you tell, and that uncertainty sits on 58.5% of COGS. This is the single number to demand clarity on before believing the FY2026 guidance.

### Blended output cost

CPO cash cost net of kernel credit [calculated: (CPO/PK-pool COGS − PK revenue) ÷ CPO sold]: FY2024 Rp6,371/kg → FY2025 **Rp8,352/kg** — a 31% cost inflation in one year, entirely mix (bought fruit), not inefficiency. At FY2025's Rp14,061 ASP the blended margin was still Rp5,709/kg; at FY2023's Rp10,821 ASP the same cost structure would earn Rp2,469/kg — thin. The new CSRA is far more operationally levered to the CPO price than the old one.

Five-year (FY2021–25) totals [calculated]: CFO Rp1,733bn → capex Rp1,150bn → FCF Rp583bn → shareholder returns Rp257bn (44%) → the rest into cash/debt paydown later re-drawn. # SUMMARY VERDICT

**What the business is.** A competent, mid-scale, pure-domestic Sumatra planter — ~17,600 mature Ha at 19–21 t/Ha, OER ~21%, all-in fruit cost ~Rp1,350/kg, no age cliff, real cash conversion (five-year CFO 1.5× earnings), moderate leverage from a single bank — that has spent the windfall of the 2021–22 CPO boom converting itself into a miller. It now processes half a million tonnes of fruit a year, 38% of it bought from third parties at economics somewhere between thin and negative that the filings do not let an outsider pin down.

**What it earns through the cycle.** ~Rp140–160bn at mid-cycle prices, Rp270bn+ in strong price years, against a Rp1.48tn market cap — i.e. genuinely cheap on peak earnings, ordinary (~10×) on mid-cycle earnings, with a dividend too small (9% payout) to pay you for waiting.

**What would have to be true for it to work as an investment.** (1) CPO prices hold at 2025–26 levels — plausible given B50 and structural Indonesian supply tightness; (2) the bought-fruit spread stays positive as PKS-3 annualises; (3) the controlling families keep treating minorities tolerably — a resumed dividend, a cancelled or fairly-priced buyback continuation; ideally (4) a re-filed Tondi Bersama never happens. Under (1)–(3), FY2026 could print Rp400–580bn and the stock is a double even at a family-company multiple.

**What breaks it.** A fast CPO downswing squeezes the bought-fruit spread and takes the marginal 38% of throughput to outright losses while Rp250bn of current-maturity debt still needs servicing; the Tondi Bersama claim returns on the merits against the group's best estate; or — the quiet one — the float simply keeps shrinking until the multiple is whatever the family says it is. On the last point the direction of travel is already visible: dividend skipped in a record year, board pay running at 80% of all dividends ever paid, a buyback that retired a quarter of the float at ~1× book, and 30 catalogued internal inconsistencies in the company's own numbers.

**Position.** As a business: better than its Rp1.5tn price at any reasonable CPO deck — the estate engine alone justifies the EV. As a security: this is a minority ride on a vehicle the founding families are visibly consolidating, with disclosure quality (unreconciled capacity figures, contradictory auditor attribution, a phantom Rp3,807-vs-3,207 fruit price) that would be disqualifying at ten times the size. The cheapness is real and likely permanent-until-they-decide-otherwise. It belongs, if anywhere, in the same bucket as PSGO/BWPT: a small, information-poor, tightly-held CPO beta instrument — sized for the possibility that the next corporate action is not designed with you in mind.
