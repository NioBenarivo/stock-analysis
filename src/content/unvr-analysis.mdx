Indonesia's largest listed consumer-goods manufacturer: soaps, detergents, shampoo, toothpaste,
skin care, seasoning, soy sauce and juice, made in eight factories around Cikarang and Surabaya and
sold through independent distributors into small shops across the archipelago. Listed on the IDX since
January 1982 under the ticker UNVR. Eighty-five per cent owned by Unilever PLC through a chain of
Dutch holding companies.

Market capitalisation about **Rp64 trillion** at 4 September 2026 (Rp1,685 per share), against
**Rp280 trillion** at the end of 2020. The company has lost roughly three-quarters of its market
value in six years while remaining profitable and debt-free throughout.

Period covered: **FY2017 to FY2025, plus the first half of 2026.** Five annual reports (FY2021–FY2025)
were read; the FY2021, FY2023, FY2024 and FY2025 audited financial statements and notes were
transcribed in full, and the management discussion of every year with them. FY2022 figures come from
the FY2023 report's comparative column. Eight investor presentations covering 2021 to July 2026 were
read in full. Full source table at the end.

## THE ONE THING TO UNDERSTAND FIRST

PT Unilever Indonesia does not own the brands it sells.

Rinso, Pepsodent, Lifebuoy, Sunlight, Dove, Royco, Bango — the names that give this company its
place in Indonesian life — belong to Unilever PLC in London and are held for it by a Dutch company
called Unilever IP Holdings B.V. The Indonesian company rents them. It also rents the recipes and
the manufacturing know-how, and it buys a package of head-office services from the group. For all
of that it pays a set of fees written into four contracts, three of them signed on 11 December 2012
between the company and its own controlling shareholder.

Those fees came to **about seven per cent of everything the company sold in 2025**. That is not a
small line in a long list. It is roughly **half of the profit the business made before tax**, and it
is **nearly three-quarters of the entire "general and administration" expense line** — the line most
readers skim past because they assume it holds office rent and head-office salaries. Strip the fees
out and what is left of that line is about Rp0.8 trillion of actual administration in a Rp32 trillion
business. The rest is rent paid to the owner.

The rate was not always this high. Until 2012 the company paid about 3.5 per cent of sales. In
December 2012 it announced a schedule that would take the charge to 8 per cent by 2015. The shares
fell 27.5 per cent in two days — the worst two days in twelve years — because the market understood
immediately what had happened: a controlling shareholder had raised the price at which it sells its
own inputs to the company it controls, and the minority had no vote. What the accounts show from
2020 onward is not a new problem. It is that schedule, settled and running.

The comparison that makes the size of it visible sits inside the same group. Hindustan Unilever,
Unilever's other big listed Asian subsidiary, pays **3.45 per cent** of turnover for the same bundle
of trademarks, technology and central services — a rate set after an outside firm benchmarked it and
the audit committee reviewed the result in public. Unilever Indonesia pays roughly double, with no
equivalent public benchmarking anywhere in the record. On the 2025 revenue base the difference is
**Rp1.1 to 1.3 trillion a year** — about a third of the profit the Indonesian business now makes from
running itself.

Everything else in this report is downstream of that arrangement. The company pays out essentially
all of its earnings as dividends every year, so nothing accumulates; its own capital (what it owns
minus what it owes) has shrunk from Rp5.3 trillion at the end of 2019 to Rp4.5 trillion at the end of 2025 despite
earning about Rp34 trillion in between. It has sold its ice cream business and its tea business in the
last fifteen months and paid the proceeds out too. It carries no debt because it does not need any —
it does not invest enough to require it. What it is, structurally, is a licensed manufacturer and
distributor that converts Indonesian consumer spending into two streams flowing to the same
destination: a fee taken before profit, and a dividend taken after it, of which the parent receives
eighty-five per cent.

The evidence sits in §1.5 (the fee contracts and the numbers), §7.5 (where the fee lands in the
income statement), §08.1 (the arithmetic year by year) and §10.1 (why the tax authority appears to
be asking about it).

# 01 — OWNER

## 1.1 Group structure

The ownership chain has four links and no surprises in it, which is itself worth noting: there is no
Indonesian founding family, no local partner, and no pyramid of holding companies designed to
obscure anything. Control runs in a straight line from London.

Two features of this chart matter more than the percentages.

**The listed company has no subsidiaries.** It says so in every annual report. Everything it does, it
does inside one legal entity. There is no group to consolidate, no minority interests, no earnings
sitting in a company it part-owns. What you see on the income statement is what the business itself
earns — which makes the accounts unusually easy to read, and makes the size of the fee to the parent
unusually hard to hide.

**The suppliers sit outside.** PT Unilever Oleochemical Indonesia makes surfactants — the active
ingredients in detergent and shampoo. It is a Unilever
company, but it is not owned by the listed company. It is a sister. So when Unilever Indonesia buys
from it, that is a related-party purchase, priced by agreement between two arms of the same parent.
Those purchases have grown from **Rp38 billion in 2021 to Rp983 billion in 2025**, and the accounts
give no explanation for the change. 2021 was a trough — the same purchases were Rp193 billion in 2020
— so the increase from a normal base is about fivefold rather than twenty-six-fold. This is covered in §1.5.

There is one loose end. The company's own group chart in the FY2023 report showed an 88 per cent
holding in PT Gerai Cepat Untung, described as "in liquidation" — printed directly beneath the
sentence saying the company has no subsidiaries. The stake had been 75 per cent in FY2021. Neither
the increase nor the liquidation is narrated anywhere, and by FY2025 the company has vanished from
the chart.

**The float is now a regulatory problem.** Indonesia's financial regulator is raising the minimum
public float from 7.5 per cent to 15 per cent. UNVR's float was 14.03 per cent at the end of April
2026 and the buyback pushed it lower rather than higher. The company says only that it "will ensure
compliance once the rules are finalised."

The ultimate beneficial owners, disclosed for the first time in March 2026 under a new regulator
instruction, are named as **Fernando Fernandez, Srinivas Pathak and Prakash Kakkad** — the chief
executive and senior officers of Unilever PLC. They are the people the rule requires to be named,
not people with an economic interest of their own.

## 1.3 History — recap

Ninety years of building, then eight years of taking apart.

The first eighty years are an acquisition story. The company bought its way into tea in 1990, soy
sauce in 2000 and juice in 2008, and built factories to make what it sold. Every one of those moves
added a category, a plant and a set of Indonesian consumer habits it could serve.

Since 2018 the direction has reversed. Spreads went in 2018, ice cream in 2025, tea in 2026. Each
sale was announced as portfolio focus. Each produced a large cash sum. In each case the cash was paid
out to shareholders rather than reinvested — the ice cream proceeds were followed within three weeks
by an interim dividend larger than any the company had declared in three years. The businesses being
sold are the ones the Indonesian company built or bought for itself; the brands it keeps renting are
the ones the parent owns.

The turning point that explains the numbers, though, is 2012. That is when the price of being a
Unilever licensee in Indonesia roughly doubled. Everything the company has earned since has been split
between the parent's fee, the tax authority, and a dividend of which the parent takes 85 per cent.

## 1.4 Owner's character

There is no founding family here whose behaviour can be read across decades. What can be read is how
the parent has behaved as a controlling shareholder, and the record is consistent on four points.

**It raises its own price and does not explain.** The 2012 agreements have no stated end date, no
renegotiation mechanism and no evidence of independent benchmarking. The trademark and technology
royalties are stated as percentages of a turnover base that the notes say "has been defined in detail
in the respective agreements" — and never define. The result is that the contracted 3 per cent and 2
per cent cannot be reconciled to the amounts actually charged, because nobody outside the company
knows what they are charged on.

**It takes everything.** Dividends have equalled essentially 100 per cent of profit in every year
since at least 2018 — and in 2023, and again on the cash actually paid during 2024, they exceeded it,
funded out of the balance sheet. In 2025 the payout exceeded profit from continuing operations though
not the total including the disposal gain.
Nothing is retained. The company's own capital has been shrinking for six years.

**It changes management quickly and from outside.** Ira Noviarti, promoted in 2020 after three decades
at the company, lasted three years and left with no reason given beyond "change is inevitable." Her
successor came from Unilever Philippines. By the end of 2025 four of six directors were non-Indonesian
Unilever career staff and four of the six had been appointed during 2025 — three of them ten months before the year end and
one less than three months before it. One director, Vandana Suri,
left after ten months for Hindustan Unilever while simultaneously holding a global brand role at the
parent. The board is a rotation of group managers, not a management team with a stake in this company.

**It does not fund the recovery from its own pocket.** In December 2024 — the worst year in the
company's listed history, with equity down to Rp2.1 trillion — the company **borrowed Rp1.45 trillion
in three tranches from two banks on 19 December and paid a Rp1.56 trillion interim dividend on
the same day**. It
also declined, in July 2023, to renew a Rp3 trillion standby credit line the parent itself had
provided.

One counterweight is worth recording. Independent commissioners hold five of six board seats, the
audit committee is chaired by an independent commissioner who has held the role since 2019, and the
board includes people of genuine standing — Ignasius Jonan, a former energy and transport minister,
and Alissa Wahid. There is no evidence in nine years of filings of any regulatory sanction, competition
case or legal proceeding against the company. The governance apparatus is real. It has simply never
been the mechanism through which the fee to the parent is decided.

Four contracts, and one supply relationship, carry almost everything.

Two things stand out. The first is the direction of the ratio against profit: as the business shrank,
the fee did not shrink with it. **In 2020 the parent took just over a third of pre-tax profit; by 2024
it took more than half.** The fee is charged on sales, so it is close to a fixed cost against a
falling profit line — it absorbs the downside and gives none of it back.

The second is the merged line. The central service fee and the ETS fee are added together in every
single year, and no filing ever splits them. That single omission makes both caps untestable. It
matters most in 2023, when the merged charge reached **3.71 per cent of domestic sales** — above the
3 per cent cap on central services taken alone. That is defensible only if a meaningful part of it
was ETS under its own separate 1 per cent cap, which the company does not evidence. The FY2024 report
prints the 1 per cent ETS cap in the same paragraph as the merged figure of Rp1,004 billion and makes
no attempt to reconcile the two.

### The other flows

**Exports are not exports.** Roughly 3 per cent of sales go abroad, and essentially all of it goes to
other Unilever companies — Singapore, the Philippines, Malaysia, Japan, Vietnam and twenty more. The
Indonesian company does not sell to foreign customers; it supplies group affiliates at prices set
inside the group. When those affiliates stop ordering, the line simply disappears: Unilever Australia
went from Rp97 billion in 2020 to nil in 2023, and Unilever Myanmar from Rp115 billion in 2020 to
Rp0.6 billion in 2023 and nil by 2025. Export
sales fell 31 per cent in 2023 — that is an allocation decision made elsewhere, not a market outcome.

**Purchases from the sister surfactant plant are climbing fast.**

Buying more from a sister company is not wrong in itself — a domestic surfactant plant may genuinely
be cheaper than imports. But the share of the company's raw-material bill sourced from parties whose
prices are set inside the group has risen from 7.9 per cent in 2020 to 9.3 per cent in 2025, having
dipped to 4.2 per cent in between, and the accounts say nothing about why or on what pricing basis.

**Twenty-plus affiliates are listed every year as having "expense reimbursement" transactions with
the company, and no amount is given for any of them.**

**Board pay is disclosed only in total.** Directors and commissioners together took Rp80 billion in
2025 and Rp118 billion in 2023 — the year profit fell 10 per cent, director pay rose 36 per cent. No
individual figure has ever been published. Board pay fell 19 per cent in 2025, the same year the
workforce was cut by 967 people.

# 02 — MODEL

The company reports two segments. Home & Personal Care is roughly three-quarters of what it sells;
Foods & Refreshment is the rest. Below those two, management runs five business units — Home Care,
Personal Care, Beauty & Wellbeing, Foods, and the food-service arm — but no revenue figure has ever
been published for any of the five. Ice cream was a sixth until December 2025.

## 2.1 Home & Personal Care

### Product — what is sold

Three groups of everyday household goods, all bought weekly or monthly by nearly every Indonesian
household, all sold in sachets and small packs as well as bottles.

**Home Care** is laundry and cleaning: Rinso and Molto for washing and softening clothes, Sunlight
for dishes, Wipol, Vixal, Superpell and Cif for floors and bathrooms. The Indonesian home-care market
is worth about **€2.1 billion** and the company expects it to reach €2.3 billion by 2030 — modest
value growth of about 2 per cent a year against volume growth of 6 to 8 per cent, which is another
way of saying prices in this category are falling in real terms. Fragrance is now the battleground:
the company says scent and sensory claims account for **more than 40 per cent** of what Indonesians
buy in home care.

**Personal Care** is washing and grooming: Lifebuoy and Lux soap, Pepsodent and Closeup toothpaste,
Rexona deodorant, Dove, Zwitsal for babies. Worth about **€1.8 billion** — skin cleansing €956m, oral
care €591m, deodorants €247m — with spending per person around €30 a year and rising 4 per cent.

**Beauty & Wellbeing** is the premium end: Vaseline, Pond's, Sunsilk, Clear, TRESemmé, Glow & Lovely.
No market size is given. The company describes competition here as "eyeball competition" — the fight
is for attention on a screen, not shelf space in a shop.

The physical footprint is small relative to the revenue, and shrinking. Fixed assets were
**Rp7.1 trillion at the end of 2025 — 36 per cent of everything the company owns** — down from

No factory capacity figure, utilisation rate or production volume has been disclosed in any year. The
company cannot even keep the factory count straight — the FY2021 report says nine on one page and
eight on another, the FY2025 report says eight and prints four addresses — and no closure is disclosed
anywhere to explain the change.

### Purchase — what the business buys

Raw materials are about **72 per cent of the cost of everything sold** and just over a third of
revenue. What those materials are is never said. In nine years of annual reports, palm oil, crude
oil, chemicals and packaging resin are not named as inputs. A single investor slide in February 2026
mentions "palm oil and forex" once. There is no commodity hedging disclosed in any year.

The line worth noticing is the second one. **More than an eighth of what the company sells, it does
not make** — it buys the finished product from someone else and puts its brand on it. That share has
risen from under a tenth in 2020. Sixteen third-party manufacturing plants are acknowledged and none
is named.

Capital spending runs at **1.5 to 3 per cent of sales** on the cash measure — Rp0.6 to 1.1 trillion a
year — though the segment note puts it at Rp0.8 to 1.3 trillion. Depreciation and amortisation have
fallen from Rp1.1 trillion to Rp0.7 trillion over the same period. The company is not expanding its
plant; it is maintaining it. Four different capex figures appear for 2025 alone, ranging from
Rp862 billion in the cash flow to Rp1,321 billion in the segment note.

### Process — what it costs to make

The manufacturing story the company tells is entirely about efficiency, never about output. In 2025
it lightened the Sunlight bottle from 38 grams to 32 grams, cut spray-drying tower runtime to save
40 per cent of energy on that step, and installed solar panels. It says logistics cost per tonne
improved and "non-material supply chain costs" fell — without giving either figure.

The one number that connects effort to result: **cost per tonne improved 10.3 per cent in 2025 while
volume rose 4.8 per cent.** That is the whole disclosed picture of manufacturing performance.

### Distribute — how it reaches the shopper

This is where the company's real problem lives, and where its disclosure is worst.

Unilever Indonesia does not sell to shops. It sells to independent distributors, who sell on to the
roughly two million warungs, kiosks and minimarkets that carry its products. The distributors carry
the stock, extend the credit and employ the salesmen. The company's revenue is therefore what
distributors buy, not what shoppers buy — and the gap between those two things is what broke the
numbers in 2024.

The distributor count is reported four incompatible ways for the same period:

| Source | Period | Count |
|---|---|---|
| Public expose deck | Sep 2021 | more than 800 |
| Annual report | FY2021 | more than 800 |
| Annual report MD&A | FY2022 | **141** |
| Public expose deck | 2022 | **500** |
| Earnings call | Feb 2023 | **470**, down from 568 in 2020 |
| Public expose deck | Jun 2023 | **467**, down from 600 in 2019 |
| Annual report MD&A | FY2023 | more than **600**, in 250 groups |
| Annual reports | FY2024, FY2025 | **not stated at all** |

No two of these are reconciled anywhere. For calendar 2022 the published counts are 141, 500 and 470.
The last two annual reports drop the figure entirely while describing a "reset" and a shift toward
"larger distributors."

What the company does disclose about execution is a set of improvement percentages with no base:
direct coverage up 21 per cent in 2025, salesmen up 20 per cent, assortment up 18 per cent, digital
ordering 1.6 times higher in the first half of 2026 — every one a growth rate on an undisclosed
starting point. Nor is there a channel split: general trade, modern trade and digital commerce are
named in every report, but the share of sales each represents has not been published since 2022, when
digital commerce was said to be 5 per cent.

## 2.2 Foods & Refreshment

### Product

A much simpler business, and a better one. Royco stock cubes and seasoning, Bango sweet soy sauce,
Buavita juice, and — until March 2026 — SariWangi tea. It is smaller than Home & Personal Care but
its margin has been rising while HPC's has fallen: segment result margin **23.6 per cent in 2025
against 21.4 per cent** for HPC, having been half of HPC's level five years ago.

Royco and Bango are the two power brands and both were relaunched in 2025 with new formulations and
completely new packaging — the company calls it one of the most extensive relaunches in either
brand's history. Bango is genuinely strong: sweet soy sauce is a category where Indonesian cooking
habits favour incumbents, and the brand's share came back to near its prior-year level in 2025.

The refreshment half is in structural decline. Both juice and tea bags were "challenged by changing
habits and a shift to beverages seen as healthier or more modern, especially among younger
consumers." That is the honest reason the tea business was sold.

### Supply chain, purchase and process

Foods is made in the Savoury plant at Cikarang, opened in 2015 with a stated capacity of seven
million units a year of seasoning and soy sauce — the only capacity figure the company has ever
published, and never updated since. Inputs are agricultural and, again, never named.

### Distribute

Same distributor network as HPC, plus one channel HPC does not have: **Unilever Food Solutions**, the
business-to-business arm selling to restaurants, hotels, caterers and cloud kitchens through a mix of
direct key-account sales and wholesalers. It grew 28.6 per cent in 2022 and 9.7 per cent in 2023 —
the fastest-growing line in the whole company — and then disappeared from disclosure entirely. No
revenue figure for it exists in any source. In 2025 the company says only that it "deprioritised"
some categories and that profit strengthened.

Foods also has an unusual demand tailwind. The government's free-nutritious-meals programme runs
through thousands of new kitchens, and the company supports them through UFS with technical guidance
on food safety and menu design. It launched a fish-flavoured Royco stock cube in support of the
fisheries ministry's programme. Whether this converts into meaningful volume is not disclosed.

## 2.3 The business model in one paragraph

Unilever Indonesia buys commodity inputs, converts them in eight plants and sixteen contract
factories into everyday household products carrying brands it rents from its parent, and pushes them
through a few hundred independent distributors into small shops. It captures a gross
margin of about 47 per cent on that conversion. Out of that margin it spends about 23 per cent of
sales on marketing and selling — advertising, promotion and the cost of moving goods — and about 7
per cent of sales on the fee to its parent. What is left, roughly 14 per cent of sales as operating
profit and 11 per cent after tax, is paid out in full as dividends. The company retains nothing,
invests about 3 per cent of sales in plant, and carries no debt because a business that does not
accumulate does not need to fund anything.

# 03 — DEMAND

## 3.1 The market that has to grow for this to work

Everything Unilever Indonesia sells is bought by households out of weekly income. There are roughly
280 million Indonesians and the company reaches nearly all of them, so demand is not about winning new
categories; it is about how much the average household can spend and how often it restocks. Three
things determine that.

**Income and confidence.** GDP has grown at about 5 per cent through the period with inflation
contained at 2 to 3 per cent, which should be a good backdrop. Yet the company describes a market
"largely flat" in 2025 with confidence weakening late in the year, and its Foods section is blunter:
**upper and middle-income households maintained spending, while lower-income households cut both the
number of items they bought and the number of shopping trips they made.** That is the mechanism behind
falling volume in a growing economy — the poorer half of the customer base is buying less often and
smaller.

**Category value versus category volume.** The home care market grows 6 to 8 per cent in volume and
only about 2 per cent in value. Consumers are buying more litres and paying less per litre. The
company calls the cause "category devaluation" driven by "lower-priced players using aggressive
promotions and higher deal volumes." A market where volume grows three times faster than value is a
market where the premium incumbent loses even if it holds unit share.

**Where people shop.** The shift from traditional warungs toward minimarket chains and, more
recently, toward livestream and affiliate selling on social platforms, changes who holds the
relationship with the shopper. In the traditional trade a strong brand with a good distributor wins;
in a minimarket chain the retailer sets the assortment; on a livestream the creator does. The company
has responded by building a "social-first command centre," an affiliate network of more than 16,000
creators, and by switching from short-form affiliate video to affiliate livestreaming mid-2025 after
discovering the latter worked better. That is a real adaptation, but it is an adaptation to losing
control of the shelf.

# 04 — SUPPLY

Supply in a consumer-goods business means competition — who else can put a bottle of detergent on the
same shelf, and at what cost.

## 4.1 Who took the share

Each restatement is explained by a change of definition — nutrition redefined to include juice and

the Jakarta Post, gives the cleanest single comparison: **38.5 per cent in the third quarter of 2023

Whichever series is used, the direction is the same. No single series spans both ends, but on the
company's own earliest basis the level in 2022 was 38 to 39 per cent, and on the current
continuing-operations basis it is about 31 per cent — a gap the changes of definition alone do not
explain. On a market of that size, each point of share is several hundred
billion rupiah of annual revenue.

The winners are named nowhere in any Unilever Indonesia document. From outside sources they are:

- **Wings Group**, privately held, whose SoKlin and other home-care brands sit **7 to 20 per cent
  below Rinso** on shelf and who has been the most consistent share-taker in detergent.
- **Mayora**, listed, strong in food and beverage.
- **Paragon (Wardah)**, in beauty and personal care, built on halal positioning and local identity.
- **Aice**, in ice cream — the direct competitor to the Wall's business Unilever has now sold. No
  filing links the two; the connection is an inference.

The parent has said the quiet part out loud. At Unilever's capital markets day in November 2024,
Fernando Fernandez, then chief financial officer and from March 2025 chief executive, described
Indonesia as having "serious long-standing issues …
related with a lack of differentiation of our portfolio against a very strong local competitor that
tends to operate with a significant discount on pricing." That is the parent diagnosing a product and
price problem, in public, at the same time the Indonesian company's reports were attributing the
decline to consumer sentiment.

## 4.2 The cost-curve problem

A branded incumbent beats a discounter in one of two ways: it makes the product cheaper, or it makes
the product visibly better. Unilever Indonesia's disclosure suggests neither is currently working.

On cost, the company has no scale advantage it can demonstrate — no capacity or utilisation figures
exist, an eighth of its volume is made by contractors, and its raw-material cost is a single
undisclosed line. On top of manufacturing cost it carries the parent fee, which a Wings or a Mayora
does not carry at all. **That fee alone is about seven points of margin that a domestic competitor
starts without.** It is why a 7 to 20 per cent price gap against SoKlin is so hard to close: closing
it would consume the entire fee.

On differentiation, the company's 2025 answer is fragrance, formulation claims and social-media
storytelling — Rhamno formulation in Sunlight, whole-body deodorant formats, "skintellectual" skin
care. These are genuine product moves, and the awards record suggests the marketing is well made. But
the volume recovery of late 2025 and 2026 came with **negative price**, which is the market telling
you the differentiation is not yet worth a premium.

Three Unilever brands were in Indonesia's top ten most-chosen brands in 2020. By 2023 there was one.

# 05 — KEY VARIABLES

Five things move this company's profit. Everything else is noise.

**1. The parent fee — about 7% of sales, roughly half of pre-tax profit.**
It is charged as a percentage of sales, so it does not fall when profit falls. A one-point reduction
in the combined rate would add roughly **Rp320 billion** to pre-tax profit at the 2025 revenue base
`[calc: 1% × Rp31,943,461m]` — about 7 per cent more profit from continuing operations. A move to
Hindustan Unilever's 3.45 per cent would add roughly **Rp1.1 trillion**, or 24 per cent of pre-tax profit. Nothing else
on this list has that leverage, and nothing else is as entirely within one party's gift.

**2. Volume, not price.** Sales are about 32 trillion rupiah on a gross margin near 47 per cent, so
each 1 per cent of volume is roughly **Rp150 billion of gross profit** `[calc]`. Three years of almost
unbroken negative volume cost the company more than the boycott did. The 2026 recovery is volume-led with
price going backwards, so watch whether gross margin can hold as volume returns — in the first half
of 2026 it could not, falling 168 basis points.

**3. Marketing and selling spend — 23% of sales, the largest controllable line.**
At Rp7.4 trillion it is 1.6 times the operating profit. Advertising alone is about Rp2.7 trillion and
has been **flat in rupiah across 2023, 2024 and 2025** — the rising percentage-of-sales figure the
company reports is the denominator falling, not the spend rising. Cutting it produces immediate
profit and delayed share loss; that trade-off is the single clearest management choice in the
accounts.

**4. Raw-material cost — 72% of cost of goods sold, about a third of sales.**
Undisclosed by commodity, unhedged. A 10 per cent move in the raw-material bill is roughly
**Rp1.2 trillion** of gross profit `[calc: 10% × Rp12,154,861m]` — a quarter of operating profit. The
2023 margin recovery (from 46.3 back to 49.7 per cent) was almost entirely this line. The 2022
collapse was only about a third raw materials — bought-in finished goods and other manufacturing
overheads each cost more.

**5. The distributor stock cycle.** Because the company sells to distributors, its reported revenue
can diverge from consumer demand for a year or more in either direction. The 2024 reset stripped
about 30 per cent out of trade stock after a 25 per cent reduction over the two years before, which
is most of why 2024 revenue fell 9 per cent while consumer offtake fell less. That destocking is now
finished, which flatters 2025 and 2026 growth by the same mechanism in reverse.

Two variables that look important and are not. **Currency**: the company's foreign-currency exposure
is small and roughly balanced, and it hedges only with short forwards. **Interest rates**: it has no
term debt and, since December 2025, Rp5.7 trillion of cash.

# 06 — VICISSITUDES

## 6.1 The boycott of late 2023 — the shock the company will not name

On 8 November 2023 the Indonesian Ulema Council issued a religious ruling on support for Israel.
Within weeks, Unilever's brands were among those consumers were being urged to avoid.

The effect was immediate and it is quantified exactly once, in a single slide in the February 2024
investor deck: taking third-quarter 2023 sales as 100, the running rate fell to **74 in November
2023** and had recovered only to 92 by January 2024. Quarterly sales growth went from **+3.3 per cent
in the third quarter to −15.3 per cent in the fourth**, with volume down 13 per cent.

That number appears once and never again. The words "boycott" and "boikot" do not appear in any
annual report, any management discussion or any investor deck across the whole period. The vocabulary
used instead is "negative consumer sentiment," "hoax," "disinformation," "the geopolitical situation
in the Middle East," and — once, in the customer development section of the FY2023 report — "a public
narrative which instructs people not to buy Unilever products." The FY2025 report does not mention it
at all.

The response was to lean into religious credentials rather than to argue: halal certification
barcodes placed in stores, a mosque-cleaning programme with the Indonesian Mosque Council delivering
cleaning packages to more than 50,000 mosques, ninety prayer rooms renovated for the ninetieth
anniversary, and Sunlight-branded aid delivered "in conflicted area."

By 2026 the direct effect appears to have faded — four consecutive quarters of growth by mid-2026 —
but the parent still describes Middle East volatility as elevated, and the share lost in that quarter
has not come back.

## 6.2 What a repeat would do

The boycott is instructive precisely because it was survivable. Sales fell about a quarter at the
worst point, in November 2023, and 15 per cent across the fourth quarter as a whole; profit fell
10 per cent for the year; the company remained profitable, paid its full
dividend and took on no debt. The business is not fragile to a demand shock in the ordinary sense.

What it is fragile to is the combination. In 2024, the boycott aftermath, a competitive share loss
and a self-inflicted distributor reset arrived together, and the result was a 9 per cent revenue fall
and a 30 per cent profit fall — and the company still paid out 134 per cent of its earnings, borrowed
to do it, and let its own capital fall to Rp2.1 trillion. **The vulnerability is not the shock. It is
that the company has no retained capital to absorb one**, because the capital is removed every year
by design.

A repeat of 2024 from the 2025 base is worth working through, because it shows how little cushion
there is. All figures Rp million, `[calc]` throughout:

```
  Net sales, FY2025 continuing                              31,943,461
  less 9% revenue fall (the FY2024 experience)              (2,874,911)
  Revenue in the scenario                                   29,068,550
  Gross profit at the FY2025 margin of 46.9%                13,633,150   (was 14,997,094)
  less marketing and selling, held flat                     (7,396,945)
  less administration, held flat                            (2,963,445)
  less other expense, net, held flat                           (44,683)
  Operating profit before any fee relief                     3,228,077   (was 4,592,021)
  The fee sits inside administration and is charged on
  sales, so it falls 9% too:
    fee at 6.80% of 29,068,550                               1,976,661   (was 2,171,130)
    relief added back                                          194,469
  Operating profit in the scenario                          ~3,423,000   (was 4,592,021)
```

So a repeat of 2024 costs about **Rp1.2 trillion of operating profit, a quarter of it**, and leaves an
operating profit only about 1.7 times the fee paid to the parent. The company would still
be profitable. It would also, on its recent payout behaviour, still pay a dividend larger than its
free cash flow — which is what happened last time.

## 6.3 The earlier cycles, and the current one

The pandemic (2021) and the commodity spike (2022) are set out year by year in §7.5. Neither was as
damaging as 2024, and in both the company protected reported revenue at the cost of volume.

The recovery that began in the third quarter of 2025 is the first sustained improvement in four years
and is volume-led, which is the right kind. It also came with heavy price investment and a falling
gross margin, and the share price has round-tripped from Rp985 to Rp2,840 and back to Rp1,685 by
September 2026 — the market has priced this recovery once already and given it back.

### Working capital

The most striking movement in the whole balance sheet is what customers owe. **It has fallen by
two-thirds since 2020**, from Rp5.3 trillion to Rp1.7 trillion, and the collection period from 45
days to 20. Most of that is deliberate: fewer distributors, tighter credit, less stock pushed into
the trade. Some of it is simply lower sales.

Two cautions. First, the company sells receivables to BNP Paribas under a facility disclosed every
year, and those sold receivables are removed from the balance sheet — **the amount is never
disclosed**, so the real book and the real collection period are unknown. (The FY2025 report says
there were no transactions under the facility in 2024 or 2025, which helps for those two years only.)
Second, the quality of what remains deteriorated sharply: the expected loss rate on receivables that
are **not yet even due** jumped from 0.06 per cent to 3.23 per cent in 2025, and the provision on
that bucket rose from Rp1 billion to Rp56 billion.

Stock has barely moved in six years in absolute terms, but days on hand have risen from 44 to 52 —
the company is holding more weeks of stock against a smaller business.

### Fixed assets

Plant fell **Rp2.2 trillion in 2025**, of which Rp2.0 trillion left with the ice cream business.
Before that, the asset base had been flat to declining for five years while depreciation ran ahead of
capital spending in most of them.

One line deserves a flag. The company has booked a loss on disposing of plant in each of the last four
years, and in two of them the recovery was near-nil: in 2025 it disposed of assets carried at
**Rp270 billion for Rp11 billion of proceeds, a Rp259 billion loss**, and in 2023 a Rp365 billion book
value went for Rp5.7 billion. (2022 and 2024 recovered 29 and 24 per cent of book respectively.)
Almost all of the loss is charged into production costs rather than shown as a disposal loss, and
there is no impairment note in any year. Assets that realise 4 per cent of book in one year and
2 per cent in another were most likely already impaired and never written down.

There has never been term debt. Every borrowing in the whole period is unsecured working-capital

has never caused a problem. But it means the business runs on supplier credit, and supply-chain
financing arrangements sitting inside trade payables have grown to about **Rp1.6 trillion**.

cumulative operating cash flow over six years comfortably exceeds cumulative net income.

What it shows instead is an allocation problem. Over six years the business generated about
**Rp37 trillion of free cash flow** and paid out about **Rp34 trillion in dividends**, plus a further
Rp2.8 trillion a year on average in fees taken before profit, Rp2.5 trillion in the last two years. It invested about Rp4.6 trillion in plant across
the whole period — less than one year's dividend.

The FY2024 line is the tell: free cash flow of Rp2.9 trillion, dividends of Rp4.5 trillion, and
Rp1.45 trillion of December borrowing to bridge the difference.

Deloitte benchmarking and a public audit-committee review. Unilever Indonesia pays about 7 per cent
with no equivalent process disclosed anywhere in fourteen years.

## 08.2 The distributor reset — the self-inflicted half of 2024

Between 2022 and 2024 the company deliberately took stock out of its own trade channel. It cut trade
stock by about a quarter between December 2021 and December 2023, then by a further 30 per cent
during 2024, ending at what it called the lowest level in more than ten years.

The effect runs through the accounts in a specific order. Distributors bought less than they sold, so
reported revenue fell faster than consumer offtake. Receivables collapsed — collection days from 45
to 22. Overdue receivables fell from Rp533 billion in 2021 to Rp82 billion in 2024, and the bad-debt
line actually turned into a credit. Cash from operations fell 44 per cent because the company was
collecting from a shrinking book. Meanwhile its **own** stock rose, because production had not yet
adjusted.

# 10 — RED FLAG

## 10.1 A fee to the controlling shareholder that is half of profit, set by the controlling shareholder

The evidence is in §1.5 and §08.1. What makes it a red flag rather than an ordinary related-party
disclosure is the combination of five facts:

1. It is **about 7 per cent of sales and roughly half of pre-tax profit** — larger than any other
   discretionary item in the accounts.
2. It was set in 2012 by the party receiving it, with no vote by those paying it, and the market's
   immediate reaction was a 27.5 per cent fall.
3. The turnover base for the two royalties is never defined and the excluded products never
   quantified, so the contracted 3 and 2 per cent cannot be reconciled to what is actually charged.
4. The central service and ETS fees are merged into one number in every year, making both contractual
   caps untestable — and in 2023 the merged charge exceeded the central-service cap on its own.
5. The comparable rate inside the same group, publicly benchmarked, is **less than half**.

The money flow is straightforward: **Rp2.2 to 3.3 trillion a year leaves Indonesia as a fee, before
profit and before tax**, on top of 85 per cent of the dividend that leaves afterwards.

## 10.2 Three years of tax assessments, all involving cross-border withholding, all unresolved

Rp1.38 trillion paid under protest, carried as a full-value asset equal to nearly a third of equity,
with no provision and no decision on any of the three open years. Every one includes an Article 26
withholding assessment on payments abroad. The company does not say what is being challenged, and no
public record exists outside the filings. Detail in §08.4.

## 10.3 An affiliate disposal with no fairness opinion on the price

Rp7 trillion, from a Unilever subsidiary to a Unilever spin-off, with the only published independent
valuation covering the factory alone on a cost basis and expressly not opining on the transaction
price. Twenty-two per cent of independent shareholders voted against. The gain accounts for half of
2025's reported profit, and the proceeds were paid out as dividend within a month. Detail in §08.3.

## 10.4 An unexplained restatement of the parent fee

The FY2025 report restates the FY2024 parent charge down from **Rp2,416,060 million to
Rp2,119,402 million** — Rp297 billion — with no note anywhere explaining it. The implication is that
the ice cream and tea businesses had been carrying that much of the fee, but the company does not say
so. In the same restatement, FY2024 employee costs move from Rp2,490,297 to Rp2,170,665 and
depreciation from Rp708,484 to Rp462,633 — while the FY2025 report's own fixed-asset note still shows
Rp708,484 for the same year. Several other FY2024 lines were restated in ways that are internally
inconsistent: the depreciation of leased assets inside cost of sales is identical on both bases, as
are three lines inside marketing expenses, while everything around them moved.

Nothing here is evidence of misstatement. It is evidence that the FY2024 comparatives in the latest
report cannot be relied on line by line, and that a reader comparing 2023 to 2024 to 2025 in the
FY2025 report is comparing three different definitions.

## 10.5 Four years of losses on plant disposal, twice at near-nil recovery, with no impairment note

Four consecutive years of loss on disposal, and in two of them the recovery was near-nil —
Rp270 billion of book for Rp11 billion of cash in 2025, Rp365 billion for Rp5.7 billion in 2023 — with
almost all of the loss charged into production cost rather than shown as a disposal loss, and **no
impairment note in any year**. Assets realising 4 per cent of book in one year and 2 per cent in
another were carried above their worth.

## 10.6 Restructuring costs hidden inside ordinary employee costs

The FY2024 accounts include **Rp233,169 million of "strategic organisational productivity" cost —
severance and restructuring — inside ordinary employee costs**, not as a separate line. In FY2025 the
workforce fell by 967 people, of whom about 700 transferred with ice cream, leaving roughly 270
genuine reductions — and **no restructuring figure is given at all**. Employee benefit liabilities
fell 33 per cent that year, including about Rp295 billion of past-service-cost credits across the
pension and medical plans that are never explained.

## 10.7 Disclosure that contradicts itself on almost every operating metric

The distributor count is published as 141, 470 and 500 for the same year. The factory count is 9 and
8 within a single annual report, and 8 with four addresses in another. Market share has three
incompatible series. The brand count is 45 on the cover of the FY2025 report and 32 in its own
marketing section. Every management discussion from FY2021 to FY2025 states there were no material
commitments for capital investment, one paragraph before printing commitments of up to Rp5.6 trillion.
The receivable collection period is described in prose as 15 days and 35 days in the same reports
whose tables say 20 and 30. The FY2025 "target for next year" section is a lightly edited copy of the
FY2024 text, still written about 2025.

None of these individually matters. Together they mean the operating disclosures cannot be used to
build a series, which is why this report's operating tables all show every published version rather
than one.

## 10.8 What was checked and found clean

For balance, several things that could have been problems are not. There is **no legal proceeding,
regulatory sanction, competition case, halal-certification dispute or labour dispute** disclosed in
any year, and none found in public reporting for 2021 to 2026. The auditor is a KPMG member firm and
has been throughout. Cash from operations tracks reported profit closely, so there is no
earnings-quality gap of the kind that usually accompanies a shrinking equity base. There are no pledged
assets and no guarantees, and no consolidated group to hide anything in — with the two qualifications
this report has already made: the 88 per cent-held company that sat beneath a "no subsidiaries"
statement (§1.1), and the receivables facility whose amounts leave the balance sheet and are never
disclosed (§7.1). The problem with
this company is not that its numbers are wrong. It is what the numbers, correctly stated, describe.

# MISSING DATA AND OPEN ITEMS

Ranked by how much each would change the analysis.

**1. The split between the central service fee and the ETS fee, in every year.**
Merged into one line for six straight years. It is the largest and most volatile part of the parent
charge — it moved from Rp1,517bn to Rp919bn to Rp1,388bn to Rp865bn while sales moved a few per cent —
and merging it makes both contractual caps untestable. *Where it exists:* the two agreements
themselves, and the tax authority's own file. *What it would decide:* whether the 2023 charge breached
the 1 per cent ETS cap, and how much of the fee carries the 5 per cent mark-up.

**2. The turnover base actually used for the trademark and technology royalties.**
The notes say the definition is "defined in detail in the respective agreements" and never define it,
so the contracted 3 and 2 per cent cannot be reconciled to the Rp796bn and Rp510bn charged. *What it
would decide:* whether the effective rate is rising, and how much of the portfolio is excluded because
UNVR owns the trademark itself.

**3. What the FY2018–FY2020 tax assessments are actually about.**
Rp1.38 trillion is in dispute, each year includes an Article 26 cross-border withholding assessment,
and no filing states the issue. *Where it exists:* the Tax Court docket and the objection letters.
*What it would decide:* whether the royalty structure is under formal challenge, and whether a
Rp1.4 trillion asset is worth its carrying value.

**4. Volume, price and mix in the audited accounts.** No tonnage, no units, no average selling price in
any year. The volume series exists only in investor decks, only domestically, only from 2022, and with
fourteen quarters of missing splits. *What it would decide:* whether the 2026 recovery is real demand
or price investment.

**5. Ice cream on its own — revenue, margin, and the net assets transferred.**
Only the combined ice cream-plus-tea block is ever given. The disposal note has no schedule of assets
and liabilities and no reconciliation from Rp7 trillion of price to Rp4.8 trillion of gain. *Where it
exists:* the information circular for the January 2025 meeting. *What it would decide:* whether
Rp7 trillion was a fair price.

**6. A business-level fairness opinion on the ice cream price.** The published valuation covers the
factory alone, at cost. If no business-level opinion exists, that is itself the finding.

**7. Any market-share figure in an annual report.** Leadership in "9 categories" is asserted and the
categories are never named. The only absolute category share ever printed is ice cream's 62.7 per cent
of modern trade at the end of 2023. *Where it exists:* Nielsen and Kantar subscriptions.

**8. A consistent distributor count, outlet coverage and channel mix.** Four incompatible counts for
the same year, then silence for two years. No general trade / modern trade / e-commerce split since
2022. *What it would decide:* how much of the 2024 fall was destocking versus lost shoppers.

**9. Segment operating profit and return on capital.** About Rp2.5 trillion of marketing and
administration is left unallocated, so neither segment has a reported operating profit in any year.
Foods looks like the better business; there is no way to prove it.

**10. Unilever Food Solutions' revenue.** The fastest-growing line in the company at +28.6 per cent in
2022, then removed from disclosure entirely. No revenue figure exists in any source.
