**What it does:** Makes wooden building components (millwork, mouldings, gesso, doors, plywood, wooden blinds) and furniture (knock-down, fully assembled, case goods) in Sidoarjo and Lamongan, East Java. Almost all of it is shipped to US distributors. Also holds two natural-forest logging concessions in East Kalimantan (±163,425 ha), a West Kalimantan mangrove concession, and a new set of carbon and ecosystem-services vehicles.
**Listing:** IDX, IPO 21 Jun 2017 at Rp260. **Price Rp197 (21 Sep 2026 close). Market cap ≈ Rp1,234bn** on 6,263.96m shares outstanding (6,437.5m issued less 173.5m treasury shares).
**Period covered:** FY2016–FY2025 audited series, plus 1Q25 and 1Q26/1H26 datapoints from press releases and news.
**Sources in hand:** FY2025 Integrated Annual Report (314pp), 2017 IPO prospectus (252pp), investor decks FY2020 / FY2021 / 1H2024, and press releases FY20 / FY21 / 9M22 / FY24 / 1Q25. The web supplied carbon regulation, carbon prices, the Danish company registry, the Rimba Collective deal, tariffs and US housing. The full source table is at the end.
**Conventions:** IDR. "bn" means Rp billion. `[calculated]` marks my own derivations. `[web]` marks figures from outside the filings. USD/IDR 16,500 is assumed for all forward carbon arithmetic.

## THE ONE THING TO UNDERSTAND FIRST

**WOOD earns an operating profit and then pays almost all of it to its banks. The reason is an inventory pile that has grown for a decade and that the business has never converted into cash.**

| FY2025 | Rp bn | Anchor |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 2,630.1 | 100% |
| Operating income | 288.6 | 11.0% of revenue |
| Finance costs | (241.7) | **83.8% of operating income** `[calculated]` |
| Profit before tax | 104.6 | 4.0% of revenue |
| Inventories | 3,322.7 | **579 days of COGS** `[calculated]` |
| Advances for purchase (current + non-current) | 1,217.1 | 46% of revenue `[calculated]` |
| Gross interest-bearing debt | 2,935.2 | 7.8x EBITDA; net debt/EBITDA 7.6x `[calculated]` |

- Over FY2016–FY2025 the company reported **cumulative net profit of about Rp2,145bn** and **cumulative operating cash flow of about −Rp1,165bn** `[calculated; decks FY16–FY23, AR FY24–25]`.
- Operating cash flow exceeded reported profit in only one of those ten years, FY2024. Earnings became inventory, inventory was financed with short-term bank lines at about 8.2%, and the interest now absorbs most of the operating margin.
- The reported balance sheet looks safe (liabilities/equity 0.8x) mainly because **Rp976bn of land-and-building revaluation surplus** sits in equity. That surplus was largely booked in the IPO year, 2016.

The second structural fact is newer. **The forestry segment produced zero revenue in FY2025**, and the Annual Report codes both concession companies "no current production activity". The segment still lost Rp51.9bn. So the concessions no longer supply timber to the factories, even though the sustainability section still repeats the old "30–50% self-supply" line (§2.3). That is what makes carbon interesting:

- Conserving these forests costs WOOD almost nothing in lost timber.
- Any ecosystem-services or carbon income would drop largely to the bottom of a segment that currently only burns cash.

On the most likely route, a non-credit **payment-for-ecosystem-services deal with Rimba Collective** (Nestlé / Unilever / PepsiCo / P&G) announced in Dec 2025 over 160,000 ha in East Kalimantan, my arithmetic in §3.7 gives:

- **Rp19–56bn a year of attributable net profit**, against FY2025's Rp77.7bn.
- That is Rp3–9 per share.

This is meaningful, but it is not the thesis-changer that management's 2021 estimate (Rp155bn a year) or its 2024 talk of carbon reaching "25–50% of sales" implied.

Where this is evidenced: cash conversion in §3.2, inventory in §2.4, debt in §3.1, the forestry status in §2.3, and carbon in §2.3.6 and §3.7.

# 02 — MODEL

## 2.1 Product

**The business model in one paragraph.** WOOD buys tropical hardwood and plantation timber, and kiln-dries it using its own wood waste. It then machines the timber into primed mouldings, jambs, door parts and other millwork (called "building components"), plus a shrinking range of furniture. Almost all of it is sold FOB to a handful of US lumber and millwork distributors, who resell to US homebuilders and repair-and-remodel channels. The group has moved from being a furniture maker (2016: 63% furniture) to being a **US millwork contract manufacturer** (2025: 85% building components). Its competitive position rests on three things:

- US anti-dumping and countervailing duties on Chinese mouldings (45–252%) and cabinets.
- SVLK/FSC legality paperwork.
- Scale: it claims to be the #1 Indonesian wooden-furniture maker, with 5.2% share in 2016 (Euromonitor, per the prospectus).

### Raw material self-supply — the "30–50%" claim no longer holds

The AR sustainability section repeats the decks' line that "the Company's forest concession supplies 30–50% of wood to our factories". The same AR contradicts it three times:

1. **Both concession companies are coded "no current production activity"** (Note 1d).
2. **Forestry revenue was Rp0 in FY2025** (Rp4.7bn in FY2024; Rp119–159bn a year in 2014–16).
3. **Log inventory is exactly Rp6,490,747,759 in both years.** Nothing was cut or moved.

The factories are therefore now **~100% supplied by third-party timber**, bought at market prices. The strategic reason the prospectus gave for owning the concessions ("stability of raw material prices") no longer applies. What remains is ±163,425 ha of standing natural forest with 26–29 years left on the licences, which costs about Rp50bn a year to hold (forestry segment loss: Rp51.9bn in FY2025, Rp41.4bn in FY2024).

**Three checks on management's numbers** `[calculated]`:

1. **The FY21 arithmetic does not reconcile with itself.** Rp155bn/yr ≈ 2.1 Mt × US$5 × Rp14,750, which is the dryland estimate *alone*. The headline "3 Mt" would have been about Rp221bn.
2. **The implied carbon density is 3.6× what the buyer is now paying for.** 2.1 Mt ÷ 163,425 ha = **12.9 t/ha/yr**. The Wahea–Tabang outcome estimate is 14.5 Mt ÷ 25 yrs ÷ 160,000 ha = **3.6 t/ha/yr**. Benchmarks: Kuamut in Sabah (a converted logging concession, IFM) is about 9.6 t/ha/yr; Katingan (deep peat, REDD+) is about 37 t/ha/yr and is not comparable to mineral-soil dipterocarp forest.
3. **The Kandelia claim looks physically implausible.** The cited 19.02 Mt over 2022–31 on ~18,000 ha is **about 106 t/ha/yr**, nearly 3× Katingan's peat. The alternative figure in circulation (29.8 Mt over 40 yrs) is still about 41 t/ha/yr. Blue-carbon conservation projects usually credit an order of magnitude less. I use 10–20 t/ha/yr in §3.7.

**The regulatory gate is now open, but it is not free:**

- **Perpres 110/2025** replaced Perpres 98/2021 and recognises Verra, Gold Standard, Plan Vivo and others. **Permenhut 6/2026** (Apr 2026) lets PBPH (concession) holders run offset projects on natural production forest.
- The route is registration (SRUK) → independent validation → verification → ministerial approval.
- The state takes **10% of transaction value as PNBP** `[web: Ecobiz; consistent with the ministry's "Rp500bn PNBP on Rp5tn" for the first four approved projects]`, plus mandatory community benefit-sharing.
- **The first four forestry projects were approved on 6 Jul 2026 (~31 Mt). None is WOOD's.** IDXCarbon is tiny (Rp93.8bn traded in total to Jun 2026, at about Rp47k/t, all tech-based units, no forestry).

**The key structural point: the credit route and the PES route cannot both be sold from the same tonnes.** Rimba Collective's buyers (Nestlé, Unilever, PepsiCo, P&G) pay into an SPV for performance outcomes and receive proportional claims. If Wahea–Tabang = BRT + NKT, as the geography strongly implies, then **the dryland concessions are committed to PES, and the only clean carbon-credit asset left is Kandelia** (plus whatever IEK/EAS hold, which is undisclosed). Management's "certification by 4Q26" most likely refers to CCB (a label, not a credit) or to Kandelia.

- **Tailwind:** AD/CVD on Chinese mouldings (45–252%) and cabinets (48–293%).

- Management said in 1Q25 that building components were exempt from the reciprocal tariff. Their exposure under 301 is not disclosed.

- Rp3,333bn of stock with a 0.3% allowance. Each 1% write-down = Rp33bn = 43% of attributable NI `[calculated]`.

- **Steady state, on my arithmetic:** Rp19–56bn of attributable NI from Wahea–Tabang PES, plus Rp12–49bn from Kandelia credits if they issue. Removing the forestry segment's cash drain is a separate benefit. See §3.7.

- **China** was the dominant US supplier of mouldings and cabinets before AD/CVD. Its US millwork exports fell from ~US$287m (2017) to ~US$34m (2023) (decks, citing US trade data).

- **Competing suppliers** into the US moulding market are Latin American radiata-pine producers (Chile, Brazil), Vietnam, Malaysia and domestic US mills. Vietnam now faces a 12.5% Section 301 rate vs Indonesia's 10% `[web]`, a small relative advantage.
- **Structural risk:** WOOD is a price-taker with three distributor customers. When US demand falls, distributors cut orders rather than prices, which is why WOOD's revenue swings are so large.

- **Per-hectare view of the concessions** `[calculated]`: holding cost ≈ Rp51.9bn ÷ 163,425 ha ≈ **Rp318k/ha/yr (US$19)**. The implied Rimba Collective portfolio payment is about **US$67/ha/yr** (§3.7). Conservation pays about 3.5× what it costs to hold the land today.

# SUMMARY VERDICT

**What the business is.** WOOD is a US millwork contract manufacturer with furniture legacy lines, three distributor customers (63% of sales) and one end market (US housing). It also owns roughly 181,000 ha of standing forest and mangrove, which no longer feeds the factories. In good years (2016–21) it earned 10–11% net margins, but converted none of it to cash. In the current US housing slump it earns about 11% operating margin and pays 84% of that in interest.

**What it earns through the cycle.** Average 2016–25 net profit was about Rp215bn a year. The 2022–25 average is Rp123bn. Cumulative operating cash flow over ten years is negative. On the current asset base, **mid-cycle ROE is about 4–5% and ROIC is below the cost of debt.** The equity is cheap on book (0.28x) and not cheap on EV (11x EBITDA), which is the correct reading of a company whose book value is mostly revalued land and slow-turning inventory.

**The carbon question, answered:**

- **The opportunity is real but smaller than management has advertised.** The regulatory door opened in April 2026. The concessions are idle, so conservation has almost no opportunity cost. A Nestlé/Unilever/PepsiCo/P&G-funded PES programme has been signed on what is almost certainly WOOD's own two concessions.
- **My steady-state estimate is Rp19–105bn of attributable net profit a year (mid Rp62bn).** That is roughly +25% to +135% on FY2025 earnings, or Rp3–17 per share.
- It is not the Rp155bn a year promised in 2022, or "25–50% of sales".
- Two further points matter. **The dryland tonnes are probably committed to PES and cannot also be sold as credits.** And the Kandelia volumes being quoted look physically implausible.
- **Delivery record:** targets for 2022, 2023 and 2025 were all missed, and FY2025 shows no carbon revenue at all.

**What would have to be true for WOOD to work as an investment:**

1. PES and Kandelia cash actually starts arriving in FY2026–27, turning the forestry segment from −Rp52bn to positive.
2. Management releases at least Rp1tn of inventory into debt reduction.
3. US housing does not fall further, and the top-three distributors hold their volumes.

**If all three happen**, PBT could plausibly go from about Rp105bn to about Rp300bn+ (interest saving Rp100bn+, forestry swing Rp80bn+, carbon/PES). The equity, being a thin slice of a debt-heavy EV, would move several-fold.

**What would break it:**

- An inventory write-down (each 1% = Rp33bn).
- Loss of one of the three distributors.
- The EBITDA/interest covenant: 24% headroom to 1.25x.
- A failure to roll the 2026 bank lines.
- Governance leakage. The founders own 40% of the carbon vehicle personally; the parent takes Rp42bn a year in fees, rent and interest; related-party loans of Rp179bn include an interest-free one; and no dividend is paid.

**Position.**

- **Not an investment on its manufacturing earnings.** The cash conversion record disqualifies it.
- **A speculative option on two things:** carbon/PES monetisation and working-capital release. Carbon is worth perhaps Rp30–170 per share if it works (mid about Rp100, about Rp50 risk-weighted); the working-capital release is worth more but has never happened.
- **At Rp197, the market is paying roughly nothing for the carbon option and heavily discounting the book value.**
- It becomes interesting on evidence, not announcements: a non-zero forestry revenue line and falling inventory days in the same set of quarterly accounts. Until then, the carbon story is the fourth iteration of a narrative whose first three deadlines passed without revenue.

*This is research, not investment advice. I'm not a financial advisor.*
