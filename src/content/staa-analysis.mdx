## TL;DR — the eleven things that matter

1. **STAA is not really a plantation company any more; as of FY2025 it is a plantation bolted to a
   loss-adjacent refinery.** The Dumai refinery (STAOF) commissioned 10 July 2025 contributed
   **Rp4,910,509m of external revenue — 50.8% of the group — and Rp70,108m of segment profit, 3.1% of the
   group's**. Refinery segment margin: **1.4%**. Plantation segment margin: **24.6%**.

2. **The margin dilution is already visible and it is severe.** 1H2026 revenue +69.1% YoY; net profit +12.3%.
   Every Rp100 of *incremental* revenue in 1H2026 carried **Rp4.76 of operating profit and Rp3.75 of net
   profit** [calculated], against Rp26.90 and Rp21.20 respectively in the base business. EBITDA margin
   31.7% → 21.3% in twelve months.

3. **STAA buys in 43–49% of the fruit it crushes, every year.** Own FFB FY2025 1,084,499 t; mill throughput
   2,018,669 t; therefore **934,170 t (46.3%) purchased** [calculated, and confirmed by the OER arithmetic].
   Third-party FFB is **54–69% of COGS**. This is a *higher* bought-in share than AALI (38.7%) — the name
   usually held up as the cautionary tale. STAA is a miller and refiner that also happens to own estates.

4. **But the estates it does own are the best in the peer group.** Nucleus FFB yield **24.75 t/ha** in FY2025,
   rising every year since FY2020 (21.4 → 24.2 total). Against SSMS 21.2 and falling, and an industry average
   nearer 18–20. Average tree age 14 years — the sweet spot. This is the genuine asset.

5. **Land title lags planted area by roughly a third and nobody talks about it.** Certificated HGU+HGB
   **34,609 ha**. Note 13 planted area **41,796 ha**. MD&A headline **49,304 ha**. A further **19,171 ha of
   HGU is "still in process"** at end-FY2025. Management capitalises bearer plants on location-permit-only
   land "believing HGU will eventually be granted." AR2025 *does* name the **Satgas Penertiban Kebun Sawit**
   and the "One Map" policy as an industry risk that "could lead to administrative sanctions or land
   expropriation" — but **never as company-specific exposure, and never with a number**. The terms
   *kawasan hutan*, *PP 45/2025* and *PSDH* appear **nowhere in any filing**, and nothing has been booked.

6. **The pre-IPO cash extraction was larger than the IPO.** Rp1,206,205m of dividends paid in FY2021 —
   Rp956,000m of it in May 2021, nine months before listing — against **Rp526,688m of net IPO proceeds**
   raised from the public. The same AR2021 that discloses the payment states in its MD&A that *"the Company
   has never paid dividends because the Company is still expanding its business."*

7. **One lender, one collateral pool.** Bank Mandiri has been effectively the sole lender since a 2019
   refinancing, secured on **essentially the entire group land bank**, cross-guaranteed by the parent across
   nine subsidiaries. The DER covenant was relaxed from 200% to 300% on **17 December 2021** — between the
   November 2021 interim dividend and the year-end position.

8. **The related-party file is clean — genuinely.** No management fee, no royalty, no licence, no holdco
   service agreement, no rent. Shareholder loans existed 2018–H1 2021 at market rates and were extinguished
   at IPO. The only recurring RPT is FFB bought from a 50% associate at **0.6–1.3% of FFB purchases**. After
   SSMS and GEMS, this is a relief.

9. **The refinery was delivered 15 months late and its original target date was never acknowledged as
   missed.** Guided 1Q2024 → "end of 2024" → "April 2025" → commissioned 10 July 2025. The prospectus's own
   October 2023 completion date is never mentioned again in any subsequent filing.

10. **On B50: STAA gets nothing directly, its customers get a great deal, and the linkage is weakening fast.**
    STAA holds no biodiesel (BU BBN) allocation and cannot get one — its refinery makes food-grade RBD olein
    and stearin, not FAME. Its large buyers *are* allocation holders: Wilmar Nabati Indonesia (1,393,914 kL
    for 2026), Musim Mas (1,124,100 kL), Pelita Agung Agrindustri, plus Wilmar affiliates Sinar Alam Permai
    and Multimas Nabati Asahan. On the years with granular disclosure, **41–56% of named-customer revenue
    went to biodiesel-allocation groups (FY2021–FY2023)** — but by FY2025 that fell to about **11%**, because
    the refinery redirected sales to **Olam Global Agri (15.69%, the new #1)** and export markets. See §4.

11. **Valuation is not demanding but the earnings mix is deteriorating underneath it.** P/E ~7.3×,
    EV/EBITDA ~4.2×, P/B ~1.8×, yield ~7.0%, net cash Rp206bn. Management's own FY2026 projection is
    **revenue +15.7% and profit attributable to owners −15.6%**. They are telling you the margin compression
    continues.

# 01 — MANAGEMENT

## 1.1 What the company is, in one paragraph

PT Sumber Tani Agung Resources Tbk grows oil palm on roughly 49,300 hectares across North Sumatra, South
Sumatra, West Kalimantan and Central Kalimantan; crushes fruit — its own and, in roughly equal measure,
other people's — in ten mills; crushes the kernels in one kernel-crushing plant and one solvent-extraction
plant; and since July 2025 refines crude palm oil into RBD palm olein and stearin at a 2,000 tonne/day
refinery in Dumai, Riau. It listed on the IDX on 10 March 2022. It is controlled by one man, Suwandi
Widjaja, through two family holding companies, and has never had an outside institutional shareholder of
consequence.

**What is absent, and its absence is the finding:**

- **No management or service fee to either holding company.** None, in any year.
- **No royalty, no trademark licence, no technical assistance agreement.**
- **No rent** — the group does not lease its head office or any estate from an affiliate.
- **No intercompany loan to or from the parent.** Not one rupiah, in any year since FY2022.
- **No guarantee given to a related party outside the consolidation perimeter.**
- **No land bought from an affiliate.** (Contrast SSMS, where the parent sold its own plantation into a
  subsidiary at 81% above book five months before the listco bought the subsidiary.)

**The one thing that used to be there.** From FY2018 to H1 2021 the shareholders and one commissioner ran a
revolving short-term liquidity facility into the group — Malibu Indah Lestari, Kedaton Perkasa, PT Sumber Eka
Mandiri, and **Riswan Wijaya personally** — at **6.0–10.5% per annum**. Related-party interest expense peaked
at Rp6,815m (FY2020), 3.08% of consolidated interest expense. It was all repaid by June 2021, before listing.
This is normal pre-IPO family-company behaviour, disclosed properly, and it stopped.

**TL;DR :** on the related-party axis STAA is the best-behaved name in this series. The governance problems
here are of a different kind — undisclosed share transfers, unreconciled operational figures, and a
disclosure culture that answers questions nobody asked while leaving the obvious ones open.

**What I would still ask:** the group's *purchases* of FFB from third parties are Rp2.5 trillion a year, 46%
of throughput and up to 69% of COGS. The filings state only that no supplier exceeds 10% of net sales.
**No supplier is ever named, in any year, in any document.** For a company whose single largest cost line
is buying fruit from other people, that is the most material undisclosed relationship in the file.

**The Rp1.206 trillion.** In the twelve months before it sold shares to the public, STAA distributed
Rp1,206,205m to six private shareholders. The IPO then raised **Rp526,688m net** from those public
shareholders. The founders took out **2.3× what the public put in**, and the public's money went into a
refinery that arrived fifteen months late and earns a 1.4% margin.

This is not illegal and it is not unusual in Indonesian IPOs. It is, however, the single most important fact
about how this company allocates capital, and it is presented in the filings as follows: AR2021's MD&A
states verbatim that *"the Company has never paid dividends because the Company is still expanding its
business"* — in the same document whose cash flow statement, equity statement and Note 39 all record the
Rp1,206,205m payment. AR2022's front-matter "Dividend History" table shows only the Rp10/share post-IPO
tranche. **You have to open the financial statements to find the Rp1.2 trillion.**

**Since listing, the payout discipline has been reasonable and improving:** 45.1% → 43.2% → 46.8% → 50.9%.
There is no stated policy target — the prospectus guided "up to 30% of 2022 net profit" and the company has
exceeded that every year since, which is a positive surprise, but also means the guidance was meaningless.

> *"Domestically, risks arise from the 'One Map' policy and oversight by the Palm Oil Plantation Control

- **The strings *kawasan hutan*, *PP 45/2025* and *PSDH* return zero hits in all six filings.** The

- **Nothing is booked.** The FY2025 selling-expense and G&A lines show no anomalous jump of the kind that

  fruit bought in, STAA's exposure runs through its **suppliers'** legality as well as its own — and **no

**On the available evidence STAA has not booked, and does not quantify, any forest-estate exposure.** That
is either genuinely good news — its estates sit on legitimately titled or titling-in-progress agricultural
land — or it is the same silence AALI and SSMS maintained until the charge appeared. With 19,171 ha in HGU

is larger.**

**Variable two — the bought-in FFB price.** Because 46% of throughput is purchased, STAA's margin is a
*spread*, and the spread compresses when FFB prices rise faster than CPO prices. In FY2023, the one bad
year, COGS hit 73.3% of revenue and gross margin fell to 26.7% precisely because purchased-fruit costs did
not fall as fast as the CPO price did. **This is the single biggest swing factor in the model, and there is
no disclosure of the FFB purchase price in any year.** `NOT DISCLOSED.`

**Variable three — the export levy.** Selling expenses include "export incl. export tax," which went
Rp39,676m (FY2024) → **Rp461,407m (FY2025), a 1,063% increase.** That is the refinery's export volume
meeting Indonesia's export levy regime. From **1 March 2026 the CPO export levy rose to 12.5%** of the
government reference price (PMK 9/2026); at August 2026's reference price of US$996.52/MT that is
**US$124.56/MT of levy plus US$148/MT of export duty — US$272.56/MT, or 27.4% of the reference price,
deducted before an exporter sees anything.** Refined products sit in lower tiers (10–12%), which is precisely
why building a refinery is rational, but the direction of travel is a rising tax on this company's newest
revenue stream. **STAA has never quantified its own levy payment in any filing.**

**Variable four — the CPO price itself.** Bursa Malaysia CPO was **RM4,724/tonne on 13–14 August 2026**, a
four-month high; the KPBN Inacom price was **Rp15,688/kg**. Ciptadana's 2026 sector assumption is
~RM4,500/tonne. STAA's realised FY2025 CPO ASP of Rp14,294/kg against a current spot of Rp15,688 implies the
current price environment is supportive.

**Variable five — weather.** NOAA has put a high probability on a strong El Niño in late 2026, with the
production impact landing in **2027**. STAA's estate at average age 14 is at peak yield and therefore has
the most to lose in absolute tonnage from a drought year.

## 4.2 Where the money actually goes — and why the formula matters more than the subsidy

This is the part that is usually described loosely and gets the analysis wrong.

**The plumbing.** BPDPKS (now branding itself BPDP) collects a **levy on CPO and derivative exports** and
uses it to pay licensed biodiesel producers the gap between the biodiesel market index price (**HIP
Biodiesel**) and the diesel index price (HIP Solar), on every kilolitre they deliver under their allocation.
**The cash goes to the biodiesel producer. It does not go to the planter.** No source describes any BPDPKS
payment reaching a company that is purely an upstream CPO grower.

**The levy, at current rates:**

| August 2026 | US$/tonne |
|---|---|
| Government CPO reference price (HR CPO) | **996.52** |
| Export duty (bea keluar) | 148.00 |
| **Export levy (pungutan ekspor), 12.5% from 1 Mar 2026 (PMK 9/2026)** | **124.56** |
| **Total export-side deduction** | **272.56 — 27.4% of the reference price** |

Tiering by product: Group II (CPO) **12.5%**, Group III 12%, Group IV 10%, Group V 7.25%. **Refined products
sit in the lower tiers** — which is precisely the economic argument for building a refinery.

**Now the formula, which is the finding.** The HIP Biodiesel is set monthly as:

```
HIP Biodiesel = (average KPB CPO price  +  US$85 per tonne)  ×  870 kg/m³  +  transport cost
                 ─────────────────────      ──────────────      ─────────      ─────────────
                 the feedstock cost         fixed conversion    density        regulated
                 PASSED STRAIGHT THROUGH    margin              factor         allowance
```

Worked example, February 2026: CPO reference Rp14,496/kg, FX Rp16,832/US$ → **HIP Rp13,856/litre.** The
series has tracked the CPO price upward all year: Rp13,856 (Feb) → Rp14,562 (Jul) → **Rp14,924 (Aug 2026).**

**`TL;DR :` the biodiesel subsidy is cost-plus, not a fixed margin.** A biodiesel producer's regulated
revenue is *defined* as its feedstock cost plus a fixed US$85/tonne conversion allowance. If CPO gets more
expensive, HIP rises by exactly the same amount and the producer's margin is untouched.

**Why that is a bigger deal for a CPO seller than the subsidy itself.** Under a fixed-price or
fixed-margin subsidy, a biodiesel producer would fight hard on feedstock cost, because every rupiah saved on
CPO drops to its bottom line. Under a cost-plus formula it does not: the producer is **structurally
indifferent to the CPO price** on the portion of its intake going into biodiesel. It has removed from the
market — for ~36–39% of national CPO production under B50 — the buyer's usual incentive to bargain the
price down.

*Caveat, stated plainly:* the formula is sourced and verifiable; the inference that producers therefore do
not bargain hard on feedstock is **my reasoning, not a sourced claim.** In practice producers still tender
competitively, and the reference price used in the formula is itself a regulated benchmark rather than each
producer's actual purchase price. But directionally, a large, price-insensitive, state-guaranteed buyer pool
is unambiguously good for anyone selling CPO into Indonesia.

## 4.5 So does B50 help STAA?

### The direct channel: no

STAA is not among the 26 BU BBN allocation holders. It cannot become one with its current assets: **its
Dumai plant is a physical refinery and fractionation line producing RBD palm olein, RBD palm stearin and
PFAD — food and oleochemical grade.** Biodiesel is FAME, made by transesterification, which is a different
plant entirely. To qualify STAA would need to build a dedicated FAME unit, obtain an EBTKE biodiesel
producer licence, and win an allocation with a supply contract to a fuel distributor. **Nothing in any STAA
filing, deck, bulletin or public statement indicates any intention to do so.**

**`REVISED:` STAA does discuss the mandate — as a market condition, never as a revenue source.** The word
"biodiesel" appears 8 times in AR2021, 13 in AR2023, **25 in AR2024** and **16 in AR2025**; "B50" appears
4 times in AR2024 and 7 in AR2025. Every occurrence is macro commentary in the market-outlook section, and
it is competent commentary. AR2025's own numbers:

> *"The implementation of the B40 program consumes approximately **15.6 million kilolitres of biodiesel,
> equivalent to 14 million tons of CPO**, while the planned transition to B50 is estimated to consume
> **17.5–18.0 million tons of domestic CPO**. Full implementation of B50 could **reduce export volumes by
> 3–4 million tons**, thereby tightening global supply and potentially pushing up prices, while **increasing
> the industry's dependence on BPDP export levy funds**."* — AR2025, MD&A

Three observations on that passage. **First, STAA's own estimate (17.5–18.0m t) sits at the low end of the
18.8–20m t range analysts use** — a slightly more conservative view of the demand impulse. **Second, the
company itself writes "B45–B50"** elsewhere in the same report, which settles the terminology question: the
shorthand is in an audited annual report, not just in market chatter. **Third, and most telling, the only
consequence STAA draws for itself is the last clause** — *"increasing the industry's dependence on BPDP
export levy funds."* That is a company describing the mechanism as a **cost and a dependency**, not as a
benefit accruing to it. Nowhere in any filing does STAA claim B40, B50 or BPDPKS as a driver of its own
revenue, margin or outlook.

**STAA is a net payer into this system, not a recipient.** It pays the 12.5% export levy on its crude
exports and 10–12% on refined, which funds the subsidy that goes to Wilmar, Musim Mas, Apical and Sinar Mas.

### The indirect channel: yes, and it is real

1. **Demand.** B50 pulls an incremental 6–7 million tonnes of CPO into domestic use, taking biodiesel from
   ~25% to ~36–39% of national production and compressing exports by 2–7 million tonnes a year. A price-taker
   producing 0.83% of national CPO benefits from that unambiguously.
2. **Price behaviour of the buyer.** Because the HIP formula is cost-plus, the marginal domestic buyer for a
   third of the country's CPO has a state-guaranteed conversion margin that is unaffected by what it pays for
   feedstock. That is a structurally friendlier buyer than an export refiner competing on world margins.
3. **The domestic-export spread narrows.** Historically Indonesian domestic CPO trades at a discount to
   export parity because of the levy. Diverting a third of supply into a mandated domestic outlet compresses
   that discount.
4. **Sell-side agrees, in general terms.** Ciptadana's Overweight call on the 2026 sector explicitly rests
   on 18.8m tonnes of B50 feedstock absorption, naming AALI, LSIP, TAPG, DSNG, SSMS, SIMP, BWPT and TBLA as
   beneficiaries. Indo Premier's Buy on STAA cites "positive sentiment from the B50 program." **Note that
   STAA does not appear in Ciptadana's list at all.**

### The counter-argument, which deserves equal weight

**The mandate is funded by taxing the people it supposedly helps.** The 12.5% export levy is deducted from
the export price, which depresses the domestic reference price, which flows through to the farmgate FFB
price. Indonesian smallholder associations (POPSI) and trade press have argued consistently that the levy is
a **net transfer from planters to biodiesel producers**. On August 2026 numbers the export-side burden is
US$272.56/tonne, **27.4% of the reference price**.

For STAA specifically the levy is visible and growing: the "export incl. export tax" line went from
Rp39,676m in FY2024 to **Rp461,407m in FY2025**, a 1,063% increase. That is a real, booked, growing cost.
The demand benefit is diffuse and unquantified; the levy cost is concrete and on the P&L.

**And there is a mechanical trap.** If B50 succeeds in compressing exports by 2–7 million tonnes, the levy
*base* shrinks — but the subsidy *pool* required grows with the mandate. CNBC Research has flagged that the
government may then need to **raise the levy rate further**. A rising tax on a shrinking export base, to
fund a subsidy paid to someone else, is a bad loop to be on the wrong side of.

### The second-order effect nobody discusses: STAA's own refinery competes with biodiesel plants

This is the nuance that makes STAA's position genuinely different from a pure planter's, and I have not seen
it addressed in any STAA disclosure or in any analyst note.

STAA's Dumai refinery needs roughly **600,000 tonnes a year of CPO feedstock at nameplate** — more than the
430,182 tonnes the group produced in FY2025. It draws from the same national CPO pool as every biodiesel
plant. As B50 pulls an incremental 6–7 million tonnes into FAME production:

- **STAA's upstream segment gains** — a bigger, more price-insensitive domestic buyer pool for its CPO.
- **STAA's downstream segment loses** — it must outbid, for the same feedstock, buyers whose output price is
  a regulated cost-plus formula. STAA's RBD olein and stearin have **no such formula**; they compete on
  ordinary world refining spreads, and a 600,000 t/yr plant has no scale advantage against Wilmar's or
  Apical's multi-million-tonne complexes.

The evidence that this displacement is already happening: **GAPKI's 2025 data shows food-sector domestic CPO
consumption fell 3.6% while biodiesel absorption rose 10.97%** — at B40, before the incremental B50 volume
arrives.

**Net:** the upstream tailwind is larger in absolute rupiah than the downstream headwind, because the
plantation segment earns Rp2,074,818m against the refinery's Rp70,108m. But the direction is unhelpful: B50
raises the cost of the input to the segment STAA just spent Rp972bn building, while raising the price of the
output of the segment it already had. **STAA has partially hedged itself into the wrong side of its own
tailwind.** *(This is my synthesis, not a sourced claim.)*

Indonesian CPO demand.

STAA *was* comparably exposed — 55.7% of FY2023 revenue went to allocation-linked groups — and has spent
the two years since building a refinery that redirected a third of its output into export markets and
handed its number-one customer slot to a Singapore trading house. **It has reduced its B50 exposure by
roughly four-fifths, by choice, immediately before B50 arrived.**

**Both companies sell to PT Sinar Alam Permai** — TAPG at 21.1%, STAA at a peak of 25.07% now down to 6.92%.
That single Wilmar refinery in Sungai Gerong is the shared node between these two names.

**The honest framing of what this means.** Neither company receives a rupiah of biodiesel subsidy. Both pay
the levy that funds it. The mandate helps them only through the price of CPO, which helps every Indonesian
producer equally regardless of who they sell to. **So "TAPG's customers benefit from B50" is true and
important as a description of where the value in the palm chain is being directed — but it is not, by
itself, a reason TAPG's earnings benefit more than STAA's from a given CPO price.** What it does tell you is
something subtler and arguably more useful: **it identifies which link in the chain the Indonesian state has
chosen to enrich.** That link is the licensed biodiesel producer — six private groups plus TBLA and SMAR —
and every upstream planter, STAA and TAPG alike, is on the paying side of that transfer while capturing only
the diffuse price benefit.

**If the question is "how do I own the B50 program directly?", the answer on the IDX is TBLA or SMAR, not a
planter.** If the question is "which planter benefits most from higher domestic CPO demand?", the answer is
whoever has the most tonnes and the least cost — which, on yield of 24.75 t/ha, is arguably STAA, subject to
the 46% bought-in ratio that gives back some of the gain on the purchase side.

## 4.7 The risks to the whole thesis

1. **B50 slips.** The Minister has explicitly conditioned the pace on crude and CPO prices. The rollout is
   already phased, and the technical rules were not issued when it launched.
2. **The levy rises again.** If exports compress as forecast, the funding base shrinks while the subsidy bill
   grows. A further rate increase would land directly on STAA's newly enlarged export line.
3. **Feedstock competition intensifies** for STAA's refinery, exactly as described in §4.5.
4. **The quota is not actually rising.** The S&P Global "quota flat" headline is unreconciled against the
   +12.5% revision. If the quota stays at 15.65m kL, the incremental demand story is much weaker.
5. **STAA is not on anyone's B50 beneficiary list.** Ciptadana names eight planters; STAA is not one of them.
   In a sector re-rating driven by B50 sentiment, the stock with no coverage may simply not participate.

1. **Refinery economics.** The derivatives segment earned Rp70,108m on Rp5,063,280m of sales — 1.4%. What

   purchase costs?

sandbagging: this management sandbags revenue and profit *upward*, not downward.

## Closing note on method and coverage

This report is built from the six primary filings, three public expose decks, six investor bulletins and a
dated web pass listed in the sources table — approximately 2,800 pages. Extraction was done by subagents
writing structured notes to disk; the consolidated data pack, the arithmetic and the analysis are mine.

**Where the coverage is thin, and what it would change:**

- **The prospectus is only half-readable.** Pages 1–546 have a corrupted font map. The recovered sections —
  the legal opinion and the audited notes — gave the cap table, the UBO, the acquisition history for TPAI
  and SCK, the debt register and the FY2018–FY2020 customer list. **What is lost is the business chapter,
  the full HGU register with per-certificate expiry dates, the named suppliers, the family-relationship
  disclosure and the risk factors.** A clean copy of that prospectus would answer four of the ten questions
  in Appendix B.
- **No 1Q2026, 2Q2025 or 3Q2025 management report was supplied.** The 1Q2026 figures in Appendix C are
  derived by subtraction. If you have those bulletins, the quarterly refinery ramp becomes visible.
- **The FY2024 public expose deck is image-only** and was recovered by OCR. Figures taken from it carry
  lower confidence and are flagged where used.
- **STAA has no fundamental sell-side coverage.** The only broker note found is a one-page technical Buy
  from Indo Premier (6 April 2026, target Rp1,460). There is no consensus to check my numbers against, and
  no independent model of the refinery's economics exists in the public domain.

convention used across this series.*
