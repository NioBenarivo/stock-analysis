*(formerly PT Adaro Minerals Indonesia Tbk, formerly PT Jasapower Indonesia)*

**What it does.** Digs metallurgical coal — the kind of coal used to make steel, not to
make electricity — from five concessions in Central and East Kalimantan, and, since
December 2025, smelts aluminium at a new 500,000-tonne-a-year plant in North Kalimantan.

**Where.** Mines at Lampunut and Haju, upriver on the Barito in Central Kalimantan;
smelter at the Kalimantan Industrial Park Indonesia (KIPI) in Bulungan, North Kalimantan.
Head office Jakarta. Books kept in US dollars.

**Owner.** 85.02% held by PT Alamtri Resources Indonesia Tbk (IDX: ADRO, until November
2024 called PT Adaro Energy Indonesia), which is itself controlled by the Thohir,
Soeryadjaya, Subianto and Rachmat families through PT Adaro Strategic Investments.

**Size.** 40,882,331,500 shares. At Rp1,655 (4 September 2026) that is about
**Rp67.7 triliun, roughly US$3.8 miliar** `[calculated, at ~Rp17,900/US$]`. Free float
11.97% at end-2025 on the company's own count.

**Period covered.** FY2019 (balance sheet only) to FY2025, plus 1H2026 from press
reports of the interim filing.

**A live compliance point.** The Jakarta exchange raised its minimum free float from
7.5% to 15% on 31 March 2026, with a deadline of 31 March 2028 for companies of ADMR's
size. ADMR's own FY2025 report puts its float at **11.97%** — below the new minimum. See
§10.9.

**Sources in hand.** Five annual reports (FY2021–FY2025, 1,937 pages), three annual
earnings releases, two public-expose decks, six Adaro group quarterly presentations, and
web research. Full list and what each actually covers is in the Sources table at the end.

## THE ONE THING TO UNDERSTAND FIRST

**In five years of filings, this company has never once told anyone what it gets paid for
a tonne of coal, or what a tonne costs it to produce.**

That is not an exaggeration or a reading of fine print. Six annual reports, five earnings
releases and two public-expose presentations give the change in the average selling price
— "down 25%", "up 42%" — and never the price. They give the change in cash cost per tonne
and never the cost. The financial statements report coal revenue as a single line with no
split by grade, no tonnage, and no price. There is no year in which an outsider can
compute what the coal actually fetched from anything the company published.

This matters because of who buys the coal. Since December 2020, Maruwai Coal — the mine
that produces about 94% of the group's output — has appointed Adaro International
Singapore as its **exclusive** agent for every tonne it exports. Lahai Coal signed the
same arrangement in March 2024. Both were extended to run to the end of 2027. Adaro
International Singapore is owned by the same parent that owns ADMR. It takes a commission
described in the notes only as "a certain percentage of sales". Half of ADMR's revenue in
2025 went to that one affiliate.

So the top line is a price set between two companies with the same owner, disclosed only
as a percentage change, sold through an exclusive agent whose fee is not disclosed either.
And the bottom of the cost stack is the mirror image: 47% of what it costs ADMR to produce
and deliver coal in 2025 was paid to affiliates — Saptaindra Sejati moves the earth,
Maritim Barito Perkasa runs the barges, Indonesia Bulk Terminal handles the ships, Adaro
Logistics manages the fuel. ADMR owns the licence and the wash plant. Almost everything
between the pit and the customer belongs to someone else in the same family.

The aluminium business, which is now the reason people own the shares, is built the same
way. ADMR holds 65% of the smelter company; the other 35% is split between a bauxite group
whose alumina refinery is majority-owned by China's largest aluminium producer, and a
Singapore company incorporated ten months before it paid US$60 million for its stake and
whose owner cannot be identified on any public register. The 1,060-megawatt coal-fired
power station that runs the smelter is **not inside the smelter company** — it belongs to
PT Kaltara Power Indonesia, elsewhere in the parent's group. Which means the smelter's
single largest cost after alumina is a price charged by an affiliate, on terms that have
never been published, to a company ADMR only two-thirds owns.

None of this says the numbers are wrong. The audits are clean, the cash is real, and the
tax bills have been paid. What it says is that the reported profit of this company is, to
an unusual degree, a **choice** made by the group that owns it — how much margin to leave
in the listed vehicle rather than in the trading arm, the contractor, the barge operator
or the power station. An investor in ADMR is not buying a coal mine and a smelter. They
are buying a licensed position inside somebody else's supply chain, on terms they cannot
see and cannot check.

Where this is evidenced: the marketing agency in §1.5 and §2.1.5; the affiliate cost stack
in §2.1.4; the smelter's ownership and power in §2.2; the reconstruction of what the
numbers would look like if you could see them in §08.A and §08.B; and the full list of
what is missing in the Missing Data section.

Two things about this chart are worth pausing on.

**The mines moved house and nobody said why.** Through the FY2021, FY2022 and FY2023
reports, all five concession companies sat under ATDI. From the FY2024 chart onward,
Maruwai and Lahai — the only two that produce — appear under a different holding company,
PT Alam Tri Cakra Indonesia, which ADMR owns 90.84% of rather than 99.99%. No filing
narrates a transfer, states a price, or explains why the two producing mines were moved
into a vehicle with a 9% outside shareholder. The subsidiary tables in the same reports
still list Maruwai and Lahai among ADMR's direct mining subsidiaries at 99.99%.

**ATCI is not just a holding company.** It is also the entity that lent money to two
outside parties: Rp526.7 miliar to PT Persada Sentral Mineral, written off in full in
2025, and Rp157 miliar to PT Harapan Insani Millenia, a land and industrial-estate
developer that became a related party in July 2024 — after the loan was made. Both are
covered in §1.5 and §10.

## 1.3 History — recap

Read as a whole, the pattern is short and consistent.

The assets are old — the contracts date from 1998–2000 and were BHP's before they were
Adaro's. The listed company is new: it was a dormant services shell until August 2021,
when the group dropped the coal assets into it, charged itself US$762 million for them in
four days, funded that with its own money, and floated 16% of the result four months later
into the strongest coking coal market in history. The float was 179 times oversubscribed
and the shares were worth twelve times more eight weeks after listing.

Since then the group has been steadily taking the company back. The two other group
holders were bought out in June 2024. The parent has crept from 68.55% to 85.02%. The free
float has fallen from 16.16% to 11.97%. And the cash the coal generated has gone into an
aluminium smelter that ADMR owns two-thirds of, is powered by a plant ADMR does not own,
and sells to customers ADMR has never named.

## 1.4 Owner's character

This section reads the record rather than the rhetoric. Six things stand out.

**They price internal transfers to the rupiah, and the price always clears.** In 2021 the
group sold the coal companies to ADMR for US$381.9 million and simultaneously funded ADMR
with US$195 million of equity and a US$186.9 million loan — US$381.9 million exactly. The
money went out of one pocket and back into the other on the same day. Under Indonesian
accounting rules for deals between companies under common control, the difference between
what was paid and what the assets were worth on the books does not go through profit; it
goes straight to a reserve. So a US$383.0 million charge landed in equity, and ADMR's
book value **fell** from US$259 million to US$205 million in a year when it earned
US$157 million of profit. That deduction is still sitting on the balance sheet today, at
US$234.5 million.

**They do not tell you the price of anything.** Not the coal price. Not the cash cost. Not
the marketing commission ("a certain percentage of sales"). Not the management fee ("to be
agreed separately in writing"). Not the interest rate on a single borrowing, related-party
or bank, in any year — every one is "LIBOR plus a certain percentage", "SOFR plus a certain
percentage", "a fixed rate at a certain percentage per annum". Not the covenants on
US$1.08 miliar of smelter debt, only that the borrower is in compliance. This is
consistent across five annual reports and is clearly a policy rather than an oversight.

**Volume promises are beaten; spending promises are not.** Every year since listing, the
company has guided a sales volume and then beaten the top of its own range by 2–4%. Since the smelter started it has overshot the top of its capital-spending range by 62%
in 2024 and **104% in 2025**. (The 2023 comparison is not like for like: that year's
guide of US$70–90 million covered metallurgical coal only and explicitly excluded the
smelter, and coal capex came in near US$37 million — well below the guide, not above
it.) No release has ever
described the capex overshoot as a miss. Each one explains it as "driven by aluminium
smelter construction," as though that were news.

**Bad news is restated as good news rather than announced.** The smelter's start date has
moved from "early 2025" (November 2022) to "first quarter 2025" (May 2023) to "on track
for a 4Q2025 completion" (February 2024) to "focus on achieving commercial operation by
the end of 2025" (March 2025) to partial testing beginning in the last quarter of 2025 and
full output "during 2026". That is more than a year of slippage, disclosed one quarter at
a time, and never once called a delay in any document.

**The rename came after the customer walked.** In November 2022 the group signed a
memorandum with Hyundai on supplying aluminium. It was never mentioned again in any
filing. Independent reporting says Hyundai withdrew over the smelter's coal-fired power.
Two years later the group renamed itself from Adaro — a name with 25 years of coal
attached to it — to AlamTri, and put the metallurgical coal and metals businesses under
the new name while the thermal coal went into a separately named company. Whatever the
internal logic, the effect is that a coal-powered aluminium smelter is now marketed under
a brand with no coal in it.

**Minority holders got paid once, and then the balance sheet was re-geared.** The first
dividend in the company's history — US$120 million, a 27% payout — was declared in June
2025. In the same year the group drew US$422.9 million of new bank debt and spent
US$661.7 million of capital, so cash fell by US$241.5 million and the company went from
US$316.8 million of net cash to US$343.9 million of net debt. The dividend was not funded
out of surplus; it was funded alongside the borrowing.

One counterweight is worth stating, because the record supports it. When the coal cycle
turned in 2025 and the realised price fell about a quarter, the company held its cost of
sales flat in absolute terms while lifting volume 12% — cost per tonne sold fell from
about US$103 to US$92 `[calculated]`. That is real operating discipline, not an accounting
effect. Whatever else is true, they run the mine well.

## 1.5 RPT — related-party transactions

"Related party" here means a company owned by the same group. ADMR deals with its own
family at both ends of the business, and the notes disclose the amounts, the counterparty
and the relationship — but never the pricing.

There are four channels.

For its first three years as a listed company, four out of every five dollars ADMR owed
was owed to its own parent. The loans were unsecured, carried no covenants, ran at an
undisclosed margin over LIBOR and then SOFR, and — importantly — **unpaid interest was
rolled into the principal**, so the debt grew without cash leaving. In 2020 the balance
Maruwai owed Alam Tri Abadi was US$450.7 million against a stated facility of
US$350 million; the excess was accumulated interest.

All of it was terminated early on 13 December 2024 and repaid in cash — US$323.8 million
in a single year. That is the cleanest thing in the file: the company that was 83%
financed by its parent in 2021 owed it nothing by the end of 2024, and replaced that
funding with a bank syndicate.

### Channel four: money going the other way

Three items sit here, and they are the least explained things in the filings.

**PT Persada Sentral Mineral.** In December 2023, ATCI lent this company Rp376.7 miliar,
raised in March 2024 to Rp526.7 miliar (US$31.4 million), secured on a pledge of all of
PSM's shares, for "general corporate purposes". PSM is described as a third party; nothing
says who it is or what it does. In FY2025 the entire loan was **provided against in full**
— written down to nil — and, in the same breath, the maturity was extended to 31 December
2026 and the interest terms changed so interest is payable at maturity rather than
periodically. Writing a loan off while extending it is an odd pair of decisions.

**PT Harapan Insani Millenia.** ATCI lent this company Rp16 miliar in November 2021,
raised to Rp157 miliar in March 2022, for buying land and building an industrial estate.
The loan was fully repaid in October 2024. The disclosure adds one line: **"since July
2024, HIM has become a related party."** ADMR lent Rp157 miliar to an outside company that
subsequently joined the family.

**The standing agreement to buy the parent's Australian coal.** On 21 September 2021 —
three months before the IPO — ADMR and Alam Tri Abadi signed a "Basic Agreement" setting
out terms under which ADMR would acquire the shares of Australian coal mining companies
indirectly held by the parent, and any Indonesian mineral mining companies the parent
comes to own in future. It is subject to due diligence and agreement on price and
mechanism. It has been carried in the notes every year since, including FY2025, and
nothing has ever been executed under it. It is a standing option, held by the parent, to
sell assets into the listed company.

## 2.1 Metallurgical coal

### 2.1.1 Product — what is actually sold

Coal used to make steel does a different job from coal used to make electricity, and the
difference is physical. A blast furnace is a tall column: iron ore goes in the top, hot gas
comes up from the bottom, and the whole stack has to stay porous under hundreds of tonnes
of load. What holds it up is coke — coal that has been baked until it softens, swells and
re-solidifies into a hard, porous lump. Only certain coals do that. Those are coking coals,
and the best of them, low in ash and low in phosphorus, are **hard coking coal**.

ADMR sells two grades, marketed since September 2023 under the name *Enviromet*:

| Product | Mine | What it is | Share of FY2025 sales |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Hard coking coal, crucible swelling number 9 | Maruwai — Lampunut | Premium coke feed, ash washed to about 4.5%, low phosphorus. From 2024 a second, medium-ash 6.5% variant was added. | 5.85 Mt — **93%** |
| Semi-soft coking coal, crucible swelling number 6 | Lahai — Haju | Weaker coke; a mill can only blend a limited share of it before the coke column loses strength, so it sells at a discount | 0.43 Mt — 7% |
| "Green coal" | Maruwai | A thermal by-product of the wash plant | included above |

Crucible swelling number is a laboratory measure of how much a coal expands when heated —
a simple proxy for how well it will coke. Nine is near the top of the scale; six is
middling. That single fact is why 93% of the tonnage carries the revenue.

**Revenue is one line.** The financial statements report "sales of coal" as a single
figure with no split by grade, no tonnage and no price, in every year. Everything below
about price per tonne is therefore derived — see §08.A.

### 2.1.2 Supply chain — the physical map

The mine is about 300 kilometres up the Barito river from the sea, and the river is too
shallow for a ship. That geography dictates the entire cost structure: the coal has to be
handled four times before it reaches a vessel, and ADMR owns almost none of the handling.

**Named customers.** Only three have ever been named across five years: Square Trading
Singapore (10.2% of revenue in 2022), PT Risun Wei Shan Indonesia (13.2% in 2024, 8.4% in

**Contract structure is essentially spot.** At the end of 2025 the two mines had firm
commitments to deliver 1.6 million tonnes — against 6.28 million tonnes sold in the year.
A year earlier the figure was 2.5 million tonnes. So somewhere between a quarter and a
third of a year's output is contracted forward at any time, and the rest is sold as it is
produced. There is no long-term offtake agreement disclosed anywhere.

**The royalty is charged on a benchmark, not on what the coal fetched.** Under the Coal
Contract of Work, the government takes a production levy of between 5.0% and 13.5% of the
price per tonne depending on calorific value and the official benchmark price level. Two
consequences follow, and they explain an oddity in the accounts.

First, ADMR's contracts are grandfathered. The FY2025 report states plainly that when the
government replaced fixed royalties with a progressive scale in 2022 (Government Regulation
15/2022) and again amended it in 2025 (Regulation 18/2025), **"for CCoW holders, the
provisions on taxation and non-tax state revenue apply in accordance with the CCoW
provisions until the end of the CCoW term."** Competitors who converted their contracts to
the newer mining-permit regime face a scale running up to 28%. ADMR does not. That is a
structural cost advantage worth several percentage points of revenue, and it lasts as long
as the contracts do.

Second, since March 2025 the government's benchmark price has been **mandatory** as the
basis for export transactions, and since August 2025 (Ministerial Decree 268.K/2025) a
producer may sell below the benchmark under a pre-existing contract but must still
calculate its tax and royalty **as if it had sold at the benchmark**. The benchmark itself
is set purely on calorific value — there is no coking-coal line in it. So ADMR's steel
coal is assessed for royalty as though it were thermal coal of the same energy content.
When coking coal is expensive relative to thermal, that is a saving; when it is not, the
royalty base can exceed what the coal actually sold for.

That is the most plausible explanation for the following, which otherwise makes no sense
against a maximum statutory rate of 13.5%:

| Royalty as % of revenue `[calculated]` | FY2022 | FY2023 | FY2024 | FY2025 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| | **16.7%** | **14.6%** | **12.7%** | **12.8%** |

A flat rate on realised revenue cannot produce 16.7%. A flat rate on a benchmark that sat
above the realised price can.

## 2.2 Aluminium — PT Kalimantan Aluminium Industry

### 2.2.1 Product

Primary aluminium: metal made from alumina by passing an electric current through it, cast
into ingots. Phase one is **500,000 tonnes a year**. The stated full design is 1.5 million
tonnes in three equal phases, of which only phase one is funded or built. Phase three is
described in the decks as "green aluminium", to be powered by hydro.

Commissioning began in December 2025. The first commercial shipments — about 35,000 tonnes,
to the United States and South Korea — left in June 2026. The company's plan is to reach
the full 500,000-tonne rate during 2026.

### 2.2.2 Supply chain — three inputs, none of them disclosed

Making a tonne of aluminium is a simple recipe with expensive ingredients:

```
   1.9 tonnes ALUMINA            supplier: NOT DISCLOSED
        +
  ~14.8 MWh ELECTRICITY          from PT Kaltara Power Indonesia — an AFFILIATE,
        +                        1,060 MW captive coal plant, OUTSIDE the smelter company
   ~0.42 t CARBON ANODE          made on site at the anode plant
        ↓
   1 tonne PRIMARY ALUMINIUM     buyer: NOT DISCLOSED
```

Every one of those arrows is a gap in the filings. There is no alumina supplier named in
any document, in any year. There is no power purchase agreement, tariff, term or capacity
disclosed. There is no offtake agreement beyond the November 2022 Hyundai memorandum,
which was never mentioned again after FY2022.

What can be established from outside the filings:

- **The power plant is a 1,060–1,100 MW coal-fired station owned by PT Kaltara Power
  Indonesia**, built by China Energy Engineering Corporation, first power January 2026.
  Five hundred thousand tonnes of aluminium needs about 7.4 terawatt-hours a year; 1,060 MW
  at a normal load factor produces about 7.9 `[calculated]`. **The power plant is sized for
  exactly this smelter and nothing more.** Phase two of the smelter cannot run on it, and
  phase two of the power plant is reported to be short roughly US$1.1 miliar with no
  confirmed lenders.
- **The Mentarang hydro project that underwrites the "green aluminium" story is 1,375 MW,
  US$2.6 miliar, half-owned by the parent — and has slipped from end-2029 to early 2031.**
  Phase one runs on coal, and will for at least five years.
- **The alumina is almost certainly Indonesian and probably cheap.** Indonesia refines
  about 7 million tonnes a year and exports roughly two-thirds of it, so domestic alumina
  is in surplus. Alumina is currently trading at about 10–12% of the aluminium price
  against a long-run average of 14–17% — the most smelter-friendly ratio in a decade.

# 03 — DEMAND

## 3.1 Metallurgical coal — the buyer is a blast furnace, and that is the whole question

Coking coal has exactly one large use: making iron in a blast furnace. If a mill makes
steel by melting scrap in an electric furnace instead, it buys almost no coking coal. So
demand for ADMR's product is not "steel demand" — it is **blast-furnace steel demand**.

| | 2024 | 2025 | 1H2026 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| World crude steel, Mt | 1,887 | 1,849 | 931.5 (−0.7% y/y) |
| China, Mt | 1,005 | 961 | 500.0 (**−3.0%**) |
| India, Mt | 149 | 165 | 87.0 (**+7.1%**) |
| Share made by blast furnace route, world | — | **69.4%** | — |
| Share made by electric arc furnace | — | 30.3% | — |

Two forces, pulling opposite ways.

**China is shrinking, and it doesn't buy by sea anyway.** Chinese steel demand fell 7.1% in
2025 — far worse than the 2.0% decline forecast a few months earlier — as the property
construction bust worked through, and output fell another 3.0% in the first half of 2026.
That is bad for coking coal in aggregate. It is less bad for a seaborne producer than it
looks, because China increasingly takes its coking coal overland from Mongolia and Russia
by rail rather than by ship. ADMR's China revenue has already collapsed from 20% of the
total in 2023 to under 2% in 2025 — that adjustment has largely happened.

**India is the growth, and India has to import by sea.** Indian steel output rose 7.1% in
the first half of 2026 and its demand is forecast to grow 7–9% a year. India has almost no
domestic coking coal of usable quality. Its imports rose 10% in 2025 and one widely cited
forecast puts them at 115 million tonnes by 2030. A producer sitting in Kalimantan has a
freight advantage into the Bay of Bengal over Queensland.

**Indonesia itself has become a real customer**, which no filing anticipated. Domestic
sales went from 1% of revenue in 2021 to roughly a third by 2024, on coke plants built to
serve the nickel and steel industries. That is demand ADMR can reach by barge, without an
ocean vessel, and it is the least commented-on structural change in the business.

**The decarbonisation risk is real but slow.** Hydrogen-based direct reduced iron replaces
coking coal entirely, and it is the long-run threat. It is not a near-term one: 69% of
world steel still runs through a blast furnace, green hydrogen costs US$4–7 a kilogram
against roughly US$1.50 needed to compete, and every flagship project — Stegra in Sweden,
HYBRIT at SSAB, ArcelorMittal Hamburg — has slipped past 2030. The credible window for
cost-competitive hydrogen steel is the mid-2030s at the earliest. The marginal blast
furnace being built today is in India, not being closed in Europe.

## 3.2 Aluminium — a tighter market than coal

Aluminium demand grows with electrification, construction, packaging and transport, and
supply is capped in an unusual way: **China, which makes about 60% of the world's
aluminium, has a self-imposed ceiling of 45 million tonnes a year and is running close to
it.** That cap is the single most important fact in the aluminium market, because it means
incremental global demand has to be met from outside China.

| | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Global supply/demand balance | deficit ~100 kt | deficit 200–760 kt, depending on source |
| LME cash price, annual average | US$2,632/t | above US$3,300/t in September 2026; all-time high ~US$3,873/t in January 2026 |
| US Section 232 tariff on imported aluminium | 25% from Feb 2025 | **50% from June 2025** |

Indonesia is a net importer of aluminium and has been buying most of it from China. A
domestic smelter therefore has a natural home market as well as an export one. Against that, Indonesia's own announced smelter pipeline is enormous — 13.5 million
tonnes of projects against about 1.5 million tonnes of nameplate capacity actually built — and at least one analysis argues rising
Indonesian output is itself the main threat to the US$3,000 price. Almost none of that
pipeline will be built, but the direction of the risk is clear.

That is a genuine moat of a sort — the licences are old, the deposits were proven by BHP,
and nobody new is building a coking coal mine in Indonesia. It is also a warning: the
company is a small, price-taking participant in a market set in Queensland, selling a
product one grade below the benchmark, and it publishes nothing that would let anyone see
what discount it accepts.

Nameplate is not output: several of these are still ramping, and one industry study put
actual Indonesian primary aluminium production at roughly 1.0 million tonnes in 2026.
ADMR is one of four operating smelters in the country and, on nameplate capacity, tied

The constraint is not alumina — Indonesia refines around 7 million tonnes a year and
exported roughly two-thirds of it in 2025, which is why feedstock is cheap right now. The
constraint is **bauxite, the ore that alumina is made from.** Indonesia banned bauxite
exports in June 2023 to force domestic refining, and the domestic mining quota for 2026 is
18–22 million tonnes against projected demand of 25 million. On a reserve base of roughly
one billion tonnes and projected 2030 demand of 65 million tonnes a year, the country has
about twelve years of ore at the pace it is planning to consume it. That is a
national-level problem rather than an ADMR problem in the near term, but it sits upstream
of every domestic alumina contract this smelter will ever sign.

## 4.3 The regulatory economics — what the government takes

| | ADMR (Coal Contract of Work) | A converted mining permit holder |
|---|---|---|
| Royalty | **5.0%–13.5%** of price per tonne, by calorific value and benchmark level, fixed by contract | progressive, 15%–28% by benchmark level, under Regulation 18/2025 |
| Grandfathering | **Yes** — the FY2025 report states the CCoW terms apply until the contract ends | No |
| Royalty base | the government benchmark price, not the realised price | same |
| Domestic supply obligation | 25% of production; no exemption for coking coal is disclosed in any source | same |
| Export proceeds | **100% must be held in an Indonesian account for 12 months** since 1 March 2025 | same |

Three of those rows are worth holding onto.

The **grandfathering is a real and durable advantage** — competitors converting to the new
permit regime face up to 28%, and ADMR does not. It lasts as long as the contracts do,
which for Maruwai is to around 2049.

The **domestic supply obligation has bitten before**. In 2022 Maruwai accrued
US$47.5 million against a shortfall, needed a ministerial letter in January 2022 to escape
the coal export ban, and eventually paid US$11.6 million into the compensation fund in
December 2023 after the rules were rewritten. US$27.4 million of the original accrual was
reversed in 2023 — which is why that year's operating expenses look artificially tiny. No
source establishes that metallurgical coal is exempt from the obligation; it should not be
assumed.

The **export proceeds rule changed materially in March 2025**: exporters must now park
100% of foreign exchange earnings in an Indonesian account for twelve months, up from 30%
for three months. Maruwai and Lahai have complied and the money sits inside "cash and cash
equivalents". It is cash the company owns but cannot freely move offshore.

One forward risk worth flagging: from 2027 the government intends to route exports of
certain strategic commodities through a state entity, Danantara, with domestic-supply and
export-duty obligations attached. If coal is included, it would put a state intermediary
between ADMR and its customers. Nothing in the filings addresses this.

# 06 — VICISSITUDES

**2020 — the only loss.** Revenue US$123 million, net loss US$29 million, gross margin
negative. Lampunut had just started and the fixed cost of a new mine sat on 1.9 million
tonnes of production. The lesson is straightforward: this business does not work below
roughly 2 million tonnes a year.

**January 2022 — the export ban.** Indonesia banned all coal exports on 1 January 2022
after the state power utility's stockpiles ran critically low. Maruwai needed a ministerial
letter dated 25 January 2022 to be released from it. The company accrued US$47.5 million
in 2022 for domestic-supply shortfalls, reversed US$27.4 million of it in 2023 when the
penalty regime was replaced by a compensation fund, and finally paid US$11.6 million in
December 2023. Nothing about the underlying obligation has changed since.

**2022 — the up-cycle, and what it disguised.** Coking coal hit US$635 a tonne in March
2022. ADMR's revenue per tonne reached US$284 and gross margin 59%. Every valuation and
every expectation formed in that window was formed at the top of the sharpest coal cycle in
fifty years.

**2023–2025 — the grind down.** Revenue per tonne fell 45% in three years
`[calculated]`. The company met it by nearly doubling volume, which held revenue roughly
flat until 2025 and then stopped working: FY2025 revenue fell 16% and profit 39%. A full
cycle, top to bottom, in three years.

**2025 — the balance sheet flipped.** Net cash of US$317 million at the end of 2024 became
net debt of US$344 million at the end of 2025, on US$662 million of capital spending and a
US$120 million dividend. Free cash flow was **negative US$485 million** `[calculated]`. The
group ran the largest capital programme in its history straight into the bottom of the
coal cycle.

**What a repeat would do.** The 2025 trough is the test case, and the company survived it
comfortably — it still earned US$264 million and covered its interest many times over. The
difference now is gearing. At FY2025 net debt is 0.95 times EBITDA, comfortable, but the
smelter facility amortises hard: contractual repayments rise from US$28 million in 2026 to
about US$160 million in 2030. If coking coal returned to US$190 a tonne while the smelter
was still ramping, ADMR would be servicing that schedule out of a coal business earning
roughly US$360 million of EBITDA and an aluminium business earning little. That is tight,
not broken — and it is the scenario the September 2026 coal price rally has, for now,
taken off the table.

**The unfunded second phase.** Phase two of the smelter is announced at another million
tonnes. Its power plant is reported to be short about US$1.1 miliar with no confirmed
lenders, and the hydro project meant to supply the "green" phase has slipped to 2031.
Nothing obliges ADMR to build it. But the capacity number in the presentations is 1.5
million tonnes, and only 500,000 of it is financed.

substitution can continue: the wash plant runs at 525 tonnes an hour, the barge conveyors
at 4,500, and the reserves at the producing mines are about thirteen years deep.

**The cost side has genuinely improved.** Cost per tonne fell from US$117 to US$92 across
three years while the strip ratio rose. That is real.

## 8.B What the smelter is actually worth, and to whom

The company has never published the project's cost, its power arrangement, its alumina
supply or its offtake. What follows is built from the balance sheet plus published industry
benchmarks, with the arithmetic shown.

**Step one — the capital.** KAI's total assets were US$1,413 million at end-2025, against
500,000 tonnes of capacity. That is **US$2,826 per installed tonne** `[calculated]`.
Published benchmarks for recent greenfield smelters in Russia, Indonesia and Iran run
US$5,000–6,000 per tonne. KAI looks half price.

It is not. The difference is that **the power station is not in the company.** An
independent 2023 study costed this project at about US$2 miliar for the smelter *plus* its
1,100 MW captive coal plant — roughly US$4,000 a tonne, and squarely inside the benchmark
range. The plant belongs to PT Kaltara Power Indonesia, elsewhere in the parent's group. So
KAI's balance sheet shows the pot line and the jetty, and not the thing that runs them.

The practical consequence: **the economics of this aluminium must be judged on about
US$2 miliar of capital, not on the US$1.4 miliar ADMR consolidates** — and the return on
the missing half accrues to a different company inside the same group, through a power
tariff nobody has published.

**Step two — the funding.** The committed facility is US$981.4 million plus Rp1,547.9
miliar, roughly **US$1,076 million** in total `[calculated, at ~Rp16,300/US$]`. Against
US$1,413 million of assets, that is **76% debt-funded** `[calculated]`. The residual, about
US$337 million, is equity and trade liabilities, of which ADMR's 65% share is roughly
US$219 million `[calculated]`. The minority partners have put in US$162 million of cash
subscriptions since 2022.

So ADMR's own cash at risk in this project is a few hundred million dollars against a
US$1.4 miliar consolidated asset and a US$2 miliar economic project. That is either
excellent capital efficiency or a warning about where the value accrues, depending on the
power tariff.

**Step three — the earnings, at the full rate.**

```
  Revenue    500,000 t × US$3,300/t LME              =  US$1,650 million
  Cash cost  500,000 t × US$1,800–1,900/t constructed = (US$900 – 950 million)
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Cash margin                                        ≈  US$700 – 750 million
  Less depreciation on ~US$1.4bn over 25 years       = (US$56 million)
  Less interest on ~US$1.08bn at, say, 7%            = (US$76 million)
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Pre-tax profit, order of magnitude                 ≈  US$570 – 620 million
  ADMR's 65% share, after 22% tax                    ≈  US$290 – 315 million
```

That would be roughly the same again as the coal business earned in 2025. It is also, on
these assumptions, more profit than the smelter cost ADMR in equity — inside two years.

**Every number above is constructed, and three of them could be wrong in the same
direction.** If the affiliate charges US$60 per megawatt-hour rather than US$40, cost rises
by nearly US$300 a tonne. If alumina reverts from 11% of the metal price to 15%, cost rises another US$285. If the LME price falls back to the US$2,600 used in mid-2026 broker
scenarios, revenue falls US$350 million. Stack all three and the cash margin roughly halves.
The company has disclosed nothing that would let anyone narrow any of them.

## 8.C The three sleeping concessions

Juloi, Kalteng and Sumber Barito hold **78.8 million tonnes of reserves and about 870
million tonnes of resources** — 44% of reserves and 87% of resources. Together they carry
US$33 million of assets. They have never mined a tonne.

| | Reserves, Mt | Resources, Mt | Feasibility study | Assets, US$m | Start date |
|---|---:|---:|---|---:|---|
| Juloi Coal | 55.5 | 804.4 | completed 2019 | 26.7 | none announced |
| Kalteng Coal | 17.7 | 50.9 | completed 2019 | 2.3 | none announced |
| Sumber Barito Coal | 5.6 | 15.0 | completed 2019 | 4.3 | none announced |

Four facts sit oddly together. The reserve numbers have not moved by a single tonne in five
annual reports. The modifying factors behind them are only at pre-feasibility level. The
coal quality assumption underpinning them cites a study dated **May 2011**. And the
contracts' 30-year clocks do not start until first mining, so there is no expiry pressure
forcing a decision.

The generous reading is that these are a genuine option: proven deposits, contracts that
cannot lapse, waiting for a price or an infrastructure link that makes them work. The
sceptical reading is that a fifth of the reported reserve life of this company rests on
three shells with 2019 studies, 2011 quality data and no plan — and that when the FY2025
report's resource table quietly changed the stated group equity in these blocks from
83.84% to 100% without explanation, nobody asked why.

Either way, the number an investor should use for reserve life is **thirteen years at the
producing mines**, not twenty-four.

# 10 — RED FLAG

Eight things in this file deserve suspicion. They are ranked by how much money moves
through them.

## 10.1 Both ends of the coal business are priced by the family, and neither price is disclosed

Half of FY2025 revenue went to one affiliate under an exclusive marketing agency that runs
to December 2027, at a commission described only as "a certain percentage of sales". At the
other end, 47% of cost of revenue went to affiliates — a ratio that has risen every single
year since 2020. Add the commission and the management fee and affiliates took
US$285 million of a US$621 million cost base in FY2025 `[calculated]`.

The money flow:

```
   STEEL MILLS  ──pay──►  ADARO INTERNATIONAL SINGAPORE  ──pay ADMR "a price"──►  ADMR
                          (affiliate, exclusive agent,          │
                           commission undisclosed)              │ pays out 47% of cost of sales
                                                                ▼
                          SAPTAINDRA (mining) · MARITIM BARITO (barging) ·
                          INDONESIA BULK TERMINAL · ADARO LOGISTICS — all affiliates
```

What makes this a red flag rather than a normal group arrangement is the absence of any
price disclosure at all. The notes say transfer prices are set "based on international
benchmarks, adjusted to coal specifications" and "on contracted prices" — but never
which benchmark, which adjustment, or what the result was. A shareholder holding part of
the 12% float has no way to test whether the margin left in ADMR is the market's or the
parent's choice.

## 10.2 The smelter's power station sits outside the smelter, and its tariff is unknown

Electricity is about a third of the cash cost of making aluminium. KAI's electricity comes
from a 1,060-megawatt captive coal plant owned by **PT Kaltara Power Indonesia**, a
different company in the same group. There is no power purchase agreement, tariff, term or
capacity in any ADMR filing — the power supplier was named once, in the FY2023 annual
report, and then dropped from FY2024 and FY2025 entirely.

The effect is that the profitability of a US$2 miliar project is set by a price charged
between two affiliates, one of which ADMR only owns 65% of, and the number has never been
published. A US$10 per megawatt-hour difference in that tariff is US$74 million a year at
full production `[calculated]` — a third of what the entire coal business earned in 2025.

## 10.3 22.5% of the smelter is owned by a company nobody can identify

Aumay Mining Pte Ltd paid Rp595.1 miliar (US$38.4 million) in December 2022 for 22.5% of
the smelter. It was incorporated in Singapore on 16 February 2022 — ten months earlier. Its
registered business is "wholesale trade of a variety of goods without a dominant product",
not mining or metals. It is an exempt private company and files no public accounts. **Its
beneficial owner does not appear on any public register.**

The other minority holder, Cita Mineral Investindo at 12.5%, is at least identifiable: an
Indonesian bauxite miner in the Harita group. But the alumina asset that makes it a logical
partner — the Well Harvest Winning refinery — is **56% controlled by Shandong Weiqiao, the
parent of China Hongqiao, the largest aluminium producer in the world.** No supply agreement
between any of these parties and KAI is disclosed.

So the entire 35% minority of a US$1.4 miliar asset consists of one party whose feedstock
runs through a Chinese competitor and one party who cannot be identified. That combination
is worth naming plainly.

## 10.4 The 2021 restructuring cost minority holders US$234 million of book value they never voted on

The group sold its own coal companies to the vehicle it was about to float, at a
US$172 million premium to book on each of two legs done four days apart, and funded the
buyer with its own money. Under common-control accounting the excess could not go through
profit, so US$383.0 million was charged straight to equity — and US$234.5 million of it is
still deducted from paid-in capital today, five years later.

The IPO investors who bought in four months afterwards bought a company whose book value
had been reduced by that amount, in a transaction settled entirely inside the family before
any of them existed as shareholders. The prospectus-era pricing was set in the strongest
coking coal market in fifty years.

## 10.5 Two loans out to parties that should not have been borrowing from a coal miner

**Persada Sentral Mineral** — Rp526.7 miliar (US$31.4 million), lent for "general corporate
purposes", secured on a pledge of all its shares. Written off in full in FY2025. In the same
amendment, the maturity was extended to December 2026 and the interest terms changed to
payment at maturity. Writing a loan down to zero while extending it and deferring its
interest is not a normal pair of decisions. No filing states who PSM is, what it does, or
why a metallurgical coal company was lending it money.

**Harapan Insani Millenia** — Rp157 miliar, lent from November 2021 to buy land and build an
industrial estate. Repaid October 2024. The notes add, without comment: *"Since July 2024,
HIM has become a related party."* ADMR made a substantial unsecured-in-substance loan to an
outside company that subsequently joined the group. That sequence is worth an explanation
and does not receive one.

Together these are the only two occasions on which ADMR has lent money outward, and both
involve counterparties whose relationship to the group is either undisclosed or arrived
late.

## 10.6 Management pay and the parent's fee both jumped several-fold in the year of the board overhaul

The management fee paid to the parent ran between US$1.75 and US$1.90 million for five
consecutive years. In FY2025 it was **US$8.77 million**. Key management compensation ran
between US$1.7 and US$2.9 million for five years. In FY2025 it was **US$9.45 million**.
Neither increase is explained anywhere. Both landed in the year the president director was
removed, the board was rebuilt, and the company was renamed.

The compensation figure is disclosed only as one aggregate covering commissioners, directors
and division heads across the whole group, with no per-person split and no prior-year
comparison in the governance chapter — so even the aggregate cannot be read against
anything.

## 10.7 The independence of the "independent" commissioners does not survive reading their biographies

Of five commissioners, three are openly affiliated with the controlling shareholder,
including the president commissioner, who is simultaneously the president director of the
parent, president director of the three control vehicles above it, and a named ultimate
beneficial owner of ADMR itself. Of the two labelled independent:

- One spent 2012–2019 as president director of the group's own power company and 2014–2021
  as president commissioner of two more of its power subsidiaries, and is concurrently an
  independent commissioner **of the parent**.
- The other sits on the audit committees of both **the parent and the spun-off sibling
  company**.

No board member other than one director owns any shares. The nomination and remuneration
function is performed by the board of commissioners itself; there is no separate committee.
And when the June 2025 meeting voted on the board overhaul, it drew 2.43% against — the only
contested item in the record, and roughly a fifth of the free float.

## 10.9 The parent has been buying the float down, into a rule that requires it to go up

The Jakarta exchange raised the minimum free float for listed companies from 7.5% to 15%
on 31 March 2026. Large companies have until 31 March 2028 to comply, and the rule also
tightened the definition — shares held by anyone above 5%, by directors, or under
transfer restriction no longer count as float.

ADMR's float has been going the other way. It was 16.16% at the IPO and stayed there
through 2024. In 2025 the parent bought a further 481.3 million shares in two tranches
and reached 85.02%, and the FY2025 report states the public holding as **11.97%** — with
another 3.01% held by "affiliates of the controlling shareholder", which under the new
definition would not count either.

So on the company's own numbers ADMR is roughly three percentage points below a minimum
it has two years to meet, and the movement over the past year has been away from
compliance rather than toward it. Nothing in the FY2025 report acknowledges the rule or
says how the gap will be closed. The two ways to close it are a placement of new shares
— which dilutes — or a sell-down by the parent, and neither has been announced.

## What was checked and found clean

For balance, several things that could have been red flags are not. The audit opinion has
been unmodified every year, from PwC Indonesia, with one key audit matter (the mine closure
provision). There is **no litigation, arbitration or legal contingency disclosed in any
year** — the only disputes are routine tax assessments, all small and all settled. Cumulative
operating cash flow over six years is 1.04 times cumulative net profit, so the earnings have
converted to cash. Tax has been paid at a 22–23% effective rate throughout, in line with the
statutory rate. And the shareholder loans that once made up 83% of liabilities were repaid
in full and in cash.

# MISSING DATA AND OPEN ITEMS

Ranked by how much each would change the analysis.

**1. The absolute selling price and cash cost per tonne of coal — every year, every
document.** Not disclosed anywhere in five annual reports, five earnings releases or two
public exposes; only percentage changes. Without it there is no verifiable margin per tonne
and no way to test the transfer price to the affiliate agent. §8.A derives a proxy that
matches the disclosed percentage changes exactly, but it is a derivation. *Where it might
exist:* the quarterly activities reports, or a broker with access to management. *What it
would decide:* whether the group leaves a market margin in the listed company.

**2. The smelter's power tariff and its supply agreement with PT Kaltara Power Indonesia.**
Never disclosed; the supplier was named once in FY2023 and dropped thereafter. Worth roughly
US$74 million a year per US$10/MWh at full rate `[calculated]`. *What it would decide:* the
smelter's cost position, and whether the return on the project accrues to ADMR or to the
affiliate that owns the power station.

**3. The smelter's alumina supply — counterparty, volume, pricing formula.** Never
disclosed. Alumina is about a third of cash cost, currently unusually cheap at 10–12% of the
metal price against a 14–17% long-run norm. *What it would decide:* whether today's margin
is structural or a market window.

**4. The smelter's offtake.** Nothing beyond a November 2022 memorandum with Hyundai that
was never mentioned again. No customer, volume, term or pricing basis for 500,000 tonnes a
year. *What it would decide:* whether the metal is sold at LME plus a premium, or into a
related trading arrangement like the coal.

**5. The smelter's total project cost as a single figure.** Never given. Only the facility
size, the annual capital spend mixed with mine infrastructure, and the subsidiary's total
assets. *What it would decide:* the project's return on capital, which cannot currently be
computed.

**6. The interest margin on every borrowing, in every year.** All described as "LIBOR/SOFR/
JIBOR plus a certain percentage" or "a fixed rate at a certain percentage". US$1.08 miliar
of committed debt with no published rate. Covenant ratios and headroom are also undisclosed;
only "in compliance" is stated. Syndicate members other than the agent are never named.

**7. Whether metallurgical coal is exempt from the 25% domestic supply obligation.** No
source establishes an exemption, and the company paid US$11.6 million into the compensation
fund for its 2022 shortfall, which suggests it is not exempt. *What it would decide:* how
much of a growing output must be sold domestically at capped prices.

**8. Why the two producing mines moved from ATDI to ATCI.** The FY2024 and FY2025 charts
place Maruwai and Lahai under a holding company ADMR owns 90.84% of rather than 99.99%. No
transfer, price or reason is narrated anywhere, and the subsidiary tables still show them at
99.99%.

**9. Who PT Persada Sentral Mineral is, and the basis of the US$31.4 million write-off.**
Relationship undisclosed; loan written off in full and simultaneously extended.

**10. The beneficial owner of Aumay Mining Pte Ltd**, holder of 22.5% of the smelter. Not on
any public register.

**11. Depreciation inside cost of revenue.** Never split out, so the company's own "cash
cost" cannot be reconciled to its accounts in any year.
