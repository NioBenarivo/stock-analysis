**What it is.** An Indonesian kretek (clove cigarette) group based in Surabaya, East Java. It
makes hand-rolled and machine-rolled clove cigarettes under the *Wismilak*, *Diplomat* and
*Galan* names, a small line of premium cigars, and — increasingly — the filter rods that go
inside cigarettes, which it sells to other cigarette companies including direct rivals. Founded
1962; listed on the IDX in December 2012.

**Scale (FY2025).** Revenue Rp6.38 triliun. Net profit Rp419 miliar. Total assets Rp3.21
triliun. About 3,170 employees across four factories. Roughly 4.2 billion cigarettes sold plus
6.2 billion filter rods.

**Market.** Share price around Rp1,650–1,680 (early September 2026), 2,099,873,760 shares in
issue, market capitalisation about Rp3.5 triliun. Trailing twelve-month earnings per share of
roughly Rp276 `[calculated]` put the shares near six times earnings. Final dividend for FY2025
of Rp104.4 per share, approved 18 May 2026.

**Period covered.** FY2020 through FY2025 in full, with the first half of 2026 from press
reporting. Sources listed at the end.

## THE ONE THING TO UNDERSTAND FIRST

Wismilak's largest single expense is not tobacco, not cloves, and not labour. It is the tax
stamp the government requires on every packet.

In Indonesia a cigarette maker must buy paper excise bands — *pita cukai*, the little printed
strips sealed across the top of a pack — from the customs office before it can sell anything.
The cost of those bands ran to about **Rp2.8 triliun in 2025: fifty-six rupiah in every hundred
of cost of goods sold, and forty-four rupiah in every hundred of revenue**. The company's
biggest supplier, and its biggest single unpaid bill at year end, is the local customs and
excise office. Everything else — leaf tobacco, cloves, paper, wages, the four factories — fits
into the remaining forty-four percent of cost.

That fact by itself would only make Wismilak a tax collector with a factory attached. What makes
it interesting is the shape of the tax.

The rate per stick is not one number. It depends on two things: **how the cigarette is made**,
and **how many the company makes**. Hand-rolled cigarettes are taxed at roughly a third of the
rate on machine-rolled ones, because hand-rolling employs a lot of people and the government
protects those jobs. And within each method, producers are sorted into bands by annual output.
Make more than three billion machine-rolled sticks in a year and you leave the cheaper band; the
tax per stick jumps by about two-thirds. Make more than five hundred million hand-rolled sticks
and you move up a rung there too.

So the tax is not a flat percentage a company simply passes on. It is a **staircase**, and where
a company stands on it is a decision the company makes by choosing how much to produce. Grow
past a step and the tax bill on *every* stick — not just the extra ones — resets higher.

Wismilak runs its business against that staircase, and the volume record shows it plainly. Its
machine-rolled output sat at 2.98 billion sticks in 2022 — 99.4% of the three-billion step,
close enough that stopping there looks deliberate rather than accidental. Its hand-rolled output
sat within a few percent of five hundred million for three consecutive years — 512, 498 and 473
million — then in 2023 the company let it jump to 882 million, accepting a near-doubling of the tax rate on that product in
exchange for the freedom to grow it. Management says so in its own words: it "maintains the
sales volume of machine-rolled and hand-rolled cigarettes to be just below the excise level."

This single mechanism explains almost every odd number in the accounts: why hand-rolled volume
more than doubled in one year, why machine-rolled volume *fell* by a fifth in 2024, why the
company runs more than half its balance sheet as inventory, why margins move without prices
moving, and why the share price jumps ten to fifteen percent whenever the finance minister says
the tariff will not rise.

It also sets up the question this report ends on. In 2025 Wismilak sold about **3.11 billion**
machine-rolled sticks — over the step for the first time. Management told the press in 2026 it
expects to remain in the cheaper band. The company also owns a second, fully licensed cigarette
manufacturer, PT Galan Gelora Djaja, which has produced nothing since 2007 and which the
accounts say is being kept alive "expecting to resume the operations when Gelora Djaja expands
its business." What happens at the top of that staircase — and how the group intends to handle
it — is the thing to watch. §05 and §08 work through the arithmetic; §10 sets out what is and
is not established.

# 01 — OWNER

## 1.1 Group structure

The listed company is a holding company that also runs one factory of its own. It does not make
cigarettes. Read the register of business activities filed with the company registry and it says
Wismilak Inti Makmur makes *cigarette flavourings and cigarette components, among them regular
and mild filter rods*, and holds shares in other companies. Everything a consumer would
recognise as Wismilak is made and sold one level down.

Three things about this chart matter more than they look.

**First, the listed parent's own business is the fastest-growing part of the group.** Filter
rods — the cellulose-acetate plugs at the mouth end of a cigarette — are made by Wismilak Inti
Makmur itself, not by a subsidiary. That business grew from Rp183 miliar of outside sales in
2020 to Rp1.42 triliun in 2025, a near-eightfold rise, and now supplies about a fifth of group
revenue. It is discussed in §2.2.

**Second, the manufacturing and the selling sit in different companies, and both are counted.**
Gelora Djaja makes cigarettes and sells them to Gawih Jaya; Gawih Jaya sells them on to shops.
In the segment note the manufacturing arm shows revenue of Rp6.25 triliun and the distribution
arm Rp5.52 triliun, of which Rp5.38 triliun is the same product counted twice and taken out
again. The group's real outside revenue is Rp6.38 triliun. The point of the structure is that
Wismilak controls its own route to the shop rather than handing product to a wholesaler, which
is what lets it manage volume so precisely against the tax steps.

**Third, there is a licensed cigarette factory sitting idle inside the group.** Galan Gelora
Djaja was set up in 1994 "to keep pace with business development", made cigarettes until 2007,
and has done nothing since. Ronald Walla — now the group's president director — was its
president director from 2002 to 2007. It carries about Rp8 miliar of assets and a small
unusable tax loss. The company states in its accounts that it will not close it because it
expects it to start again when Gelora Djaja expands. Nineteen years is a long time to hold a
dormant manufacturing licence for that reason alone. §10 sets out why this is worth noticing.

### The names above the names

The 2024 public-expose presentation draws the register as a family tree rather than a list, and
it is the only document in the set that does. Above the four registered holders it shows four
more names connected by lines: **Dipoyono Winarko (1.46%)** above Liantini Winarko (3.62%);
**Gitawati Winarko (2.91%)** above Ronald Walla; **the Jenny Kurnialim family (1.46%)** above
Sugito Winarko. Stephen Walla and Indahtati Widjajadi stand alone. The 2025 and 2026 decks
replaced this with a plain pie chart of five slices and dropped the extra names entirely.

The company never states the family relationship between any of these people. Each director's
biography carries a field headed "family relationship", and each says only that the person has
an *affiliation* with major shareholders or with fellow board members. Willy Walla, president
commissioner until his death in 2022, is described in the accounts as "parent of a shareholder
and member of key management." That is as close as the filings come to admitting the obvious.

## 1.3 History — recap

Sixty years in one paragraph: a family hand-rolling business that added machines in 1988, found
a hit brand in the low-tar segment in the 2000s, floated a minority of itself in 2012 and has
raised no outside money since. Nothing was ever bought and nothing was ever sold. There have
been no acquisitions, no disposals, no joint ventures, and no partners in any subsidiary. Growth
came from adding product lines inside the same four factories.

The one genuine change of direction happened in 2017 and did not involve cigarettes at all. The
listed parent went into partnership with Celanese, a global acetate producer, to make filter
rods, and started selling them to other cigarette companies. That business was worth Rp183
miliar in 2020 and Rp1.42 triliun in 2025. It is the only part of the group that is not
governed by the excise staircase.

## 1.4 Owner's character

The record shows a family that treats volume as a policy lever, holds its stake without adding
to it, and pays out roughly half of what it earns — but is willing to stop paying out when it
wants the cash.

**They manage the business to a tax rule, openly and consistently.** This is not a reading
between lines. Successive directors' reports say the company keeps its cigarette volumes "just
below the excise level", and the volume series bears it out year after year (§05). It is an
unusual thing to say out loud, and an unusual thing to organise a company around. It means the
owners have chosen a smaller, more profitable business over a larger, less profitable one — for
most of the period covered.

**They spend on the business before they spend on themselves — recently.** Spending on plant
and equipment ran at Rp19–50 miliar a year from 2020 to 2023, then jumped to Rp203 miliar in
2024 and Rp178 miliar in 2025, plus another Rp125 miliar of deposits on machines not yet
delivered. Almost all of it went into machinery, and most of that into the filter business. The
dividend was cut by four-tenths in 2025 — from Rp107.1 to Rp64.8 a share — in a year when profit
rose four-tenths. That is a deliberate swap of shareholder cash for factory capacity, and it was
followed a year later by a raise back to Rp104.4.

**They are conservative with debt but not with working capital.** The group carried no bank debt
at all at the end of 2021 and 2022 — the note in the accounts was even retitled from "bank
loans" to "bank loan commitments" to reflect it. Borrowing came back in 2023 and peaked at
Rp424 miliar in 2024, then fell to Rp319 miliar. The money did not go into buildings. It went
into stock: inventory rose from Rp669 miliar in 2020 to Rp1.83 triliun in 2025 and now sits at
57% of everything the company owns. §07 works through what that means.

**The two deaths.** The president commissioner died in September 2022 and a director died in
September 2023. Neither event is narrated as a governance matter anywhere in the governance
chapters — the 2022 death appears as a footnote to a shareholding table. The board reshuffled
quickly and quietly: Indahtati Widjajadi moved up to president commissioner, Stephen Walla
joined as commissioner, a second independent commissioner was added, and the audit committee was
replaced in full. In May 2025 the board of directors went from four to six.

**Longevity is a feature and a risk.** Two of the six directors were born in the 1940s: Krisna
Tanimihardja is 80 and Sugito Winarko 76. A third new director appointed in 2025, Erni Lisawati,
is 75. The president commissioner is 75. This is a board that has held together for decades and
shows no visible succession plan below Ronald Walla, who is 53.

narrow for the whole period. There are **no sales to related parties at all**, and purchases from
them are trivial. What exists is almost entirely rent: the operating companies lease their

Three observations.

**The tobacco purchase is the only one with real money in it, and it is growing.** PT Karya Bumi
Subur — owned by the same shareholders — supplied Rp73 miliar of raw material in 2025, up 35% on
2024. The company says this is 1.56% of total purchases in both years. That ratio is small and
has been stable, which is the reassuring part. The concerning part is that the accounts never
state how the price was set. Across every year read, there is **no statement anywhere that
related-party transactions were on arm's-length terms** — the notes give the counterparty, the
nature and the amount, and stop. For a group whose largest input is a farm commodity with no
public benchmark price, that omission does more work than it appears to.

**A property lease was quietly capitalised in 2025.** Until 2024 the rent paid to PT Bumi Inti
Makmur was a modest expense of Rp1.4 miliar. In 2025 a new long lease with the same landlord
appeared on the balance sheet at Rp20.3 miliar — twenty-nine times the total of every leased
asset the group had carried the year before. Almost all of it relates to Jl. Raya Darmo 36–38,
Surabaya, where the group took a full floor in August 2024 and then the whole building. The
accounts do not explain the change and do not say how the rent was benchmarked.

**A director of the listed company sits on the board of its landlord.** Sugito Winarko, a
director and 7.30% shareholder of Wismilak, is a director of PT Bumi Inti Makmur. He is on both
sides of the rent. This is disclosed plainly in his biography, and the governance chapter records
no mechanism for handling it beyond a general statement that the audit committee reviews
potential conflicts.

**What board members are paid is not disclosed.** The accounts give a single combined figure for
all commissioners and directors of the whole group — Rp32.8 miliar in 2024 rising to Rp39.1
miliar in 2025 — with no split between the two boards and no per-person figure. The governance
chapters describe the process (the committee proposes, the shareholders' meeting decides) and
give no number at all. Nor is the audit fee disclosed anywhere in five years of filings.

# 02 — MODEL

Wismilak runs two businesses that share four factories but obey different rules.

The **cigarette business** — hand-rolled and machine-rolled kretek, plus a rounding-error line of
cigars — is a taxed consumer product. What it earns per stick is set by the retail price the
company chooses minus the excise band that price and its output volume put it in. Roughly 78% of
revenue.

The **filter-rod business** — the acetate plug at the mouth end of a cigarette — is an
industrial component sold business-to-business, with no excise on it at all. About 22% of
revenue and rising fast. Wismilak sells these to other cigarette makers, including companies it
competes with on the shelf.

Revenue is stated **including** the excise the government charges — that is the required
Indonesian treatment and it is why the headline number is so large relative to what the company
actually keeps. Take the excise out and the business is a third smaller than it looks (§07.5).

Exports are trivial and shrinking: Rp27 miliar in 2025, down 58% on the year, on a group turning
over Rp6.4 triliun. Filter rods do go to Europe and Canada, but the volumes are tiny. This is a
domestic Indonesian business.

## 2.1 Cigarettes

### Product

A kretek is a cigarette in which shredded clove is blended with the tobacco. It crackles when it
burns — *kretek* is the sound — and it is what roughly nine in ten Indonesian smokers smoke.
Wismilak makes them two ways.

**Hand-rolled (SKT — *sigaret kretek tangan*)** are rolled by hand by workers seated at benches,
without a filter in the traditional form. They burn stronger, cost less to buy, and are taxed far
more lightly, because the government uses the tax gap to protect the jobs. Brands: *Galan
Kretek*, *Galan Prima*, *Wismilak Spesial*, *Wismilak Slim*, *Wismilak Satya*, *Wismilak
Heritage*, and the *ARJA* family launched in 2022 with fruit variants added since.

**Machine-rolled (SKM — *sigaret kretek mesin*)** are made on high-speed machines, filtered, and
carry the heavier tax. This is where the money is. Brands: *Wismilak Diplomat* (1989), *Diplomat
Mild* (2005) and *Diplomat evo*, with a long tail of flavour capsule versions — Berry, Guava,
Lychee, Mango — added from 2023 onward.

**Cigars** are a prestige line made since 2000 and sold in the low billions of rupiah. They exist
for the brand, not the accounts.

The commercial logic of the brand ladder is straightforward. *Diplomat* is the anchor. Everything
launched since 2022 is a variant of an existing name with a capsule or flavour twist, aimed at
smokers in their twenties for machine-rolled and thirties-to-forties for hand-rolled, at a price
point the company describes as "middle." Wismilak does not compete at the top of the market
against Sampoerna's *A Mild* or at the bottom against the cheap-tier flood; it sits in between,
and says so.

Rp1.32 triliun of raw and supporting materials — leaf tobacco and cloves, mostly. At the 2025
rate of consumption that is well over a year of supply. This is deliberate and it is how the
industry works: tobacco is harvested once a year, quality varies by season, and a blend cannot
be changed without changing the taste of the cigarette. A maker that runs out of the right leaf
cannot simply buy more; it has to wait twelve months. So the balance sheet holds the harvest.

The consequence is that Wismilak's return on capital is set less by its factories than by how
long it holds leaf and how well it bought it. Inventory has run between 115 and 178 days of cost
of sales across the six years covered `[calculated]`.

### Process — how a cigarette is made and what it costs

Physically the process is short: leaf is graded and conditioned, blended with cut clove and
flavouring, then either handed to rollers at benches or fed into a machine that rolls, attaches
the filter and cuts. Packs are sealed, the excise band is applied, and cases go to a logistics
hub. Hand-rolling is the part that employs people: of about 3,170 employees, roughly 2,030 work
for the manufacturing subsidiary and about 69% of the group are classified non-staff.

### Distribute — how it reaches the smoker

Wismilak sells through its own subsidiary rather than handing product to a wholesaler. Gawih Jaya
runs four regional logistics hubs, 23 distribution areas from Medan to Banjarmasin, two stock
points and about two dozen agents, and it employs a field marketing division whose job is to
police shelf presence — availability, visibility, freshness, point-of-sale material. In 2023 the
company said its partner programme reached 18,460 traditional shops.

The customer list in the receivables note shows what the modern half of that looks like. The
largest named debtors at the end of 2025 include **Alfamart (Rp15.9 miliar), Indomaret (Rp15.5
miliar), Indogrosir (Rp11.0 miliar) and Alfamidi (Rp1.8 miliar)** — the convenience-store chains
that now dominate Indonesian cigarette retail. No single customer reaches 10% of revenue in any
year, and the company says so each year.

Money comes back fast. At the end of 2025, 86% of receivables were not yet due and less than
Rp120 juta of Rp199 miliar was more than ninety days late. Receivable days run at about
eleven `[calculated]`. Cigarettes are a cash trade; the working capital tied up in this business
is all on the input side, not the customer side.

Pricing is not free. Every excise band a company chooses carries a **minimum retail price** it
must charge — the *harga jual eceran*, printed on the pack. A producer cannot undercut its own
band. So the price ladder and the tax ladder are the same ladder, and moving down-market to
defend volume means moving into a band with a lower minimum price *and* a lower tax rate, which
is exactly what the whole Indonesian industry has been doing since 2023 (§03).

## 2.2 Filter rods and OPP film

### Product

A filter rod is a length of cellulose acetate fibre, wrapped in paper, that gets cut into
filters and glued to the end of a cigarette. Wismilak makes them in the listed parent company
itself, sells some to its own factories and the rest to other cigarette manufacturers. It also
makes oriented polypropylene film — the clear plastic wrap on a pack — reported in the same line.

The product range has moved sharply up-market. In 2020 it was plain acetate rods and filter tips
for roll-your-own. By 2026 it includes flavour filters (granule, spray, thread), single, double
and dual-hollow capsule filters — the ones that click and release a burst of menthol or fruit —
and charcoal combination filters. Capsules are what the whole Indonesian market has been adding
to its cigarettes, and Wismilak sells the part.

### Supply chain and purchase

The key input is acetate tow, and the key supplier is **PT Celanese Indonesia Operations**, whose
2017 partnership with Wismilak started this business. Celanese was the group's second-largest
trade creditor in 2024 at Rp82 miliar. Flavour compounds come partly from China (Jiangxi Huabao
was a Rp21 miliar creditor in 2024). Machines are imported: this is where nearly all the capital
spending of 2024–2026 has gone.

Wismilak holds a **bonded-zone licence** at Surabaya, obtained in 2019, which lets it bring in
imported inputs without paying duty upfront when the output is exported or moved on under
customs control.

### Process and economics

This is the part of the group that has genuinely changed shape.

# 03 — DEMAND

Indonesia is the second-largest cigarette market in the world by volume and the largest kretek
market by definition, since almost nobody outside the country smokes them. Roughly 300 billion
sticks a year are sold legally. That market is not growing, and the way people buy inside it has
changed sharply since 2023.

**The market is shrinking slowly in volume.** National production was 244.3 billion sticks in
2024 on the figures reported by industry trackers, and the first eight months of 2025 ran 2.8%
below the same period of 2024. August 2025 was the weakest single month in five years. Wismilak's
own FY2025 report repeats this: cigarette production fell about 2.8% through October 2025.

**But the mix is moving toward exactly the product Wismilak grew.** The decline is concentrated
in machine-rolled cigarettes:

| National volume by type, billion sticks | 2023 | 2024 | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Machine-rolled kretek (SKM) | ~117.7 | 96.5 | −18% |
| White machine-rolled (SPM) | ~4.4 | 3.2 | −27.8% |
| **Hand-rolled kretek (SKT)** | ~59.4 | **67.3** | **+13.3%** |

Figures as reported by industry sources citing customs data; see Sources.

Hand-rolled cigarettes have been taking share for four straight years. Wismilak's own market-share
slide, sourced to Nielsen, tracks the same movement: hand-rolled went from 21% of the Indonesian
market in 2021 to 30% by March 2024, machine-rolled from 75% to 65%.

**The reason is price, and price is set by tax.** Every year from 2020 to 2024 the government
raised excise by around 10% on average, and it raised it far harder on machine-rolled than on
hand-rolled. A pack that has to carry Rp1,231 of tax per stick cannot be sold at the price a pack
carrying Rp223 of tax can. Smokers whose income did not rise as fast as the tax did what smokers
everywhere do: they traded down. Sometimes that meant switching to a cheaper brand from the same
maker, sometimes to a smaller producer in a lower band, and sometimes to hand-rolled.

The government's own data shows the trade-down directly. Between 2023 and 2024 volume in the
highest-taxed band fell 7.0% while volume in the lowest band rose 13.3%.

**A slice of the market has left the tax system altogether.** Untaxed cigarettes — packs with no
excise band, or a forged one — sell 30–40% below legal prices. Customs seized 816 million sticks
in the first ten months of 2025, 37% more than a year earlier, and puts the annual revenue loss
at roughly Rp15 triliun. Enforcement is intensifying, which the legal producers welcome, but the
existence of a cheap illegal tier caps how far the taxed price ladder can be pushed.

**The demographics behind all of this are stable and large.** About a third of Indonesian adults
smoke, overwhelmingly men, and the population is still growing. This is not a market in
structural collapse of the kind seen in Western Europe. It is a market where the price is being
pushed up faster than incomes, and consumers are responding by moving down the ladder rather
than quitting.

**Where Wismilak sits.** It is a mid-price producer with roughly 1.7% of national legal volume
`[calculated: 4.17 billion sticks in 2025 against national production of 244.3 billion in 2024 —
the closest year for which a national figure was found]`. Its own strategy statement is to hold the middle:
not the premium tier where the excise is heaviest, not the cheap tier where illegal product
competes. Every product launched since 2022 has been a flavour or capsule variant priced into
that middle, and the November 2025 launch of a capsule hand-rolled cigarette — which the company
says is the first patented product of its kind in Indonesia — is that strategy applied to the
category that is actually growing.

## 4.2 The excise regime — how producer take is actually set

This is the single most important regulatory fact in the report, so it is worth stating fully.

Two things determine the tax on a Wismilak cigarette:

**1. How it is made.** Hand-rolled is taxed at roughly a third of machine-rolled, band for band.

**2. How many the factory makes in a year.** Producers are sorted into *golongan* — bands — by
annual output, and the thresholds are fixed in the ministerial regulation:

| Product | Golongan I | Golongan II | Golongan III |
|---|---|---|---|
| Machine-rolled kretek (SKM) | more than **3 billion** sticks/year | 3 billion or fewer | – |
| White machine-rolled (SPM) | more than 3 billion | 3 billion or fewer | – |
| Hand-rolled (SKT/SPT) | more than **2 billion** | more than **500 million** up to 2 billion | 500 million or fewer |

The tariff per stick attached to each band, as set by PMK 191/2022 and then frozen from 2025:

| Rp per stick | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SKM Golongan I | 865 | 985 | 1,101 | 1,231 | 1,231 | frozen |
| **SKM Golongan II** | 525–535 | 600 | **669** | **746** | **746** | **frozen** |
| SKT Golongan I | 330–425 | 345–440 | 361 | 378 | 378–483 | frozen |
| **SKT Golongan II** | 200 | 205 | **214** | **223** | **223** | **frozen** |
| SKT Golongan III | 110 | 115 | 118 | 122 | 122 | frozen |

2021 and 2022 rates from the tariff table reproduced in Wismilak's own FY2021 annual report;
2023–2025 from published summaries of the ministerial regulations; 2026 from the finance
minister's announcement of 26 September 2025. See Sources.

Three features of this table drive everything:

**The hand-rolled discount is enormous and permanent.** Hand-rolled Golongan II pays Rp223 where
machine-rolled Golongan II pays Rp746 — under a third. Even the top hand-rolled band pays less
than the bottom machine-rolled band. Any producer that can shift volume from machine to hand
saves roughly Rp500 per stick of tax.

**The step between machine-rolled bands is worth about 40% of the tax bill.** Golongan II at
Rp746 against Golongan I at Rp1,231 is a ratio of 1.65 to 1, and it has been almost exactly 1.65
every year since 2023. On three billion sticks the difference is roughly **Rp1.5 triliun a
year** — more than three times Wismilak's entire net profit.

**Since 2025 the tariff has been frozen and the retail price floors raised instead.** For 2025
the government left the per-stick tax unchanged and raised the minimum retail prices by 5–19%
depending on band. For 2026 it froze the tariff again — the finance minister announced this on
26 September 2025 after consulting the industry association — and has signalled no rise for 2027
either. This is a deliberate change of policy after years of double-digit increases, made because
the tax rises were pushing consumers into the illegal market and hollowing out collections. For a
producer, a frozen tariff plus a rising price floor is the best possible combination: the cost
per stick is fixed and the price it is allowed to charge goes up.

## 4.3 Raw material supply

Wismilak buys everything and grows nothing, so this is a price-taking position.

**Tobacco leaf.** Indonesian tobacco comes mainly from East Java, Madura, Central Java and
Lombok, harvested once a year in the dry season. Quality and price swing hard with the weather:
a dry season that is too wet ruins the crop, and an extremely dry one concentrates it. Reported
farmgate prices in Madura ran Rp45,000–70,000 per kilogram in 2023 and Rp60,000–80,000 in 2024 —
roughly a fifth higher at the midpoint of the range, a third higher at the bottom of it. Wismilak's own directors' report for 2024 names tobacco
price spikes since 2023 as a challenge managed "via inventory strategy", and the 2025 report says
supply was "relatively more stable." There is no consolidated national price series in the public
data that this exercise could retrieve; see §Missing data.

**Cloves.** Indonesia produces roughly three-quarters of the world's cloves and is also the main
consumer of them, because of kretek. The price is volatile on a multi-year cycle driven by tree
biology — clove trees crop heavily in some years and lightly in others. Export unit values, the
only continuous series obtainable, ran from US$3.66 per kilogram in 2020 to a peak of US$7.01 in
2023 and back to US$5.58 by early 2025. Export prices are not farmgate prices, but the shape of
the move — up 90% then down 20% — is the shape the input cost took.

Those two commodities are most of the Rp1.80 triliun of raw materials Wismilak consumed in 2025,
and the Rp1.32 triliun of leaf and clove it was holding at the year end. This is why the company
buys a year or more ahead: it is buying a hedge as much as a raw material.

**Acetate tow** for filters comes from Celanese and is a global petrochemical derivative, priced
in dollars. A ten-percent move in the rupiah is disclosed as worth about Rp5.4 miliar of pre-tax
profit — small, because most of the group's costs are domestic.

Five things move this business. They are listed in order of how much profit each one carries.

### 1. Which excise band each product sits in — worth Rp1.5 triliun

has in that segment. The FY2026 press commentary confirms the pressure: hand-rolled volume in
early 2026 was running about 20% below the prior year on "aggressive competitor pricing", and the
company's answer was a new patented capsule product rather than a price cut.

### 2. Excise tariff policy itself — worth roughly Rp256 miliar per 10%

A 10% increase in the machine-rolled Golongan II tariff costs about Rp232 miliar on 2025 volumes;
add hand-rolled at Rp23 miliar and the total is **Rp256 miliar** `[calculated]`, against a net
profit of Rp419 miliar. If a producer could not pass any of it on, a 10% tariff rise would take
three-fifths of the year's earnings. In practice some is passed on and some is not, which is
why gross margins move without any visible change in the business.

The freeze now in place for 2025 and 2026, and signalled for 2027, is therefore the single
biggest piece of good news this company has had in a decade. It also explains the share price:
Wismilak roughly doubled between September 2024 and September 2026.

### 3. Machine-rolled volume — the profit engine

Machine-rolled kretek is 64% of revenue and a much larger share of profit, because its price per
stick is the highest and its labour content is the lowest. It is also the most volatile line in
the accounts: down 27% in 2024, up 43% in 2025, up again in the first half of 2026. Every 100
million sticks is worth roughly Rp130 miliar of revenue at 2025 prices `[calculated]`.

### 4. Filter rods — the growth line and the only unregulated one

Now 22% of reported revenue and about 40% of what the company keeps after excise `[calculated]`.
It has grown every year without exception, at rates between 17% and 126%. Its price per rod
tripled in five years on a shift to capsule and flavour products. Almost all recent capital
spending has gone here. It is also the only part of the business that does not depend on excise
policy at all — and the only one whose customers are competitors.

### 5. Tobacco and clove prices — the input the company cannot control

Raw materials are Rp1.80 triliun of cost, 82% of everything the company spends other than tax.
Leaf and clove prices move by tens of percent from harvest to harvest. Wismilak's only defence is
to hold a year of stock, which is why more than half its balance sheet is inventory and why its
reported profit and its cash flow diverge so widely (§07.4).

### What does *not* move the model

Interest rates barely matter: a half-point move is worth about Rp1.9 miliar. The currency barely
matters: a ten-percent move is worth about Rp5.4 miliar. Exports are 0.4% of revenue. There is no
commodity hedging programme, no derivative book and no offtake contract disclosed anywhere.

# 06 — VICISSITUDES

**The annual excise increase, 2015–2024.** The recurring shock in this business is not a
recession, it is a budget. For a decade the tariff rose about 10% a year and sometimes far more. Wismilak's own 2023
presentation charts the average increase at nil in 2018, 10.0% in 2019, **23.0% in 2020**, 12.5%
in 2021 and 12.0% in 2022. Each rise arrived as a step change in the cost of every stick, and each time the
whole industry lost volume as smokers traded down. Wismilak survived these better than its bigger
peers precisely because it sat in the cheaper band; the pain fell hardest on Golongan I, whose
volume dropped 7% in 2024 alone. The freeze from 2025 removes this shock for at least two years.

**COVID, 2020–2021.** Volumes held up better than most consumer categories — smoking is a habit,
not a discretionary purchase — and Wismilak's revenue actually grew 37% in 2021. But the
tobacco-processing industry's contribution to national output contracted 9.6% in the first
quarter of 2021 before recovering, and the company describes household purchasing power as not
having returned to pre-pandemic levels by the end of that year.

**The 2020 customs penalty.** In September 2020 the customs directorate issued six collection
letters against Gelora Djaja for administrative penalties on excise totalling **Rp9.48 miliar**.
The company paid in full, appealed, and carried the whole amount as a receivable for two years.
In 2022 the court granted the appeal in full and the money came back. It appears in the 2022 cash
flow statement as its own line. This is the only genuine dispute with the excise authority in the
period, and Wismilak won it.

**The 2024 volume collapse.** Machine-rolled volume fell 27% and revenue 18% in a single year;
net profit fell 40% from Rp495 miliar to Rp299 miliar. The company attributes this to the 10%
excise rise under PMK 97/2024 and the resulting market contraction. It is also the year that put
a billion sticks of clearance under the three-billion step. Whichever reading is right, the
episode shows how fast this business can lose a third of its earnings.

**The 2023 cash squeeze.** Revenue rose 32% and profit doubled, and the company still *used*
Rp200 miliar of cash in operations, because inventory rose 76% in one year. Cash fell from
Rp712 miliar to Rp279 miliar. Nothing was wrong; the company was buying leaf ahead of a
production ramp. But it is a clear demonstration that reported profit and cash are very
different things here.

**Two deaths in successive Septembers.** Willy Walla, president commissioner, in 2022; Muhammad
Warsianto, director, in 2023. Both were replaced within months. There is no visible operational
disruption in the numbers, and no succession disclosure of any kind.

**What a repeat would look like.** The two shocks that would bite hardest now are (a) crossing
into machine-rolled Golongan I, which on 2025 volumes would add roughly Rp1.5 triliun of tax and
wipe out several years of profit at once unless prices moved, and (b) a return to double-digit
annual tariff rises after 2027. Neither is priced into a business trading at about six times
earnings, in either direction.

Three readings come out of this table.

**Machine-rolled is the only product where the company has real pricing power.** Its achieved

company chose to trade margin per stick for volume, and it worked in absolute terms, but it means
hand-rolled is now a price-competitive commodity fight, which is exactly what the FY2026 press
commentary describes.

**Filters became a genuinely different product.** Rp228 a rod against Rp66 five years earlier is
not inflation; it is a different thing being sold.

sticks", which is precisely the next hand-rolled step.

These can both be true. The band is set on *production* volume recorded by the customs authority,
not on the sales volume the company charts, and the two differ by stock movements and by how
output is split between licensed factories. But they cannot both be true indefinitely if
machine-rolled keeps growing at the 2025–2026 rate. Four paths, each with a different consequence:

| Path | What it means | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Hold under 3bn** | machine-rolled volume is capped again, as in 2023–24 | growth has to come from hand-rolled and filters; the 2024 experience says earnings fall a third when machine-rolled is squeezed |
| **Cross into Golongan I** | tax per stick goes Rp746 → Rp1,231 | roughly Rp1.5 triliun a year of extra cost on 2025 volumes — three and a half times net profit — unless recovered in price |
| **Split production between licensed factories** | output allocated across more than one licence holder, each under its own limit | this is what the dormant subsidiary would make possible; see §10 |
| **The rule changes** | thresholds merged or raised, as has been publicly proposed | removes the constraint but also removes the advantage over larger rivals |

The company discloses no factory capacity, no production volume, and no band assignment in any
filing. A reader cannot tell from the accounts which path is being taken. That is the single
largest gap in Wismilak's disclosure.

## 8.3 The two halves of the group have very different economics

The segment note separates manufacturing from distribution, and the difference is stark.

| FY2023, Rp miliar | Manufacturing (Gelora Djaja) | Distribution (Gawih Jaya) |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue before elimination | 4,714.0 | 4,507.1 |
| Gross profit | 998.7 | 301.6 |
| Gross margin | 21.2% | 6.7% |
| Operating profit | 570.4 | 53.6 |
| Assets | 2,883.1 | 577.6 |

The distribution arm turns over almost as much as the factory and earns a fifth as much on it. It
exists to control the shelf, not to make money. That is a defensible reason to own it — Wismilak's
whole strategy depends on being able to steer volume by product line, which is much harder to do
through an independent wholesaler — but it means roughly Rp578 miliar of assets sit in a business
earning a 9% return.

The parent's own operations are the reverse. Its standalone accounts show total assets of Rp1.46
triliun in 2025 and net profit of Rp302 miliar, though most of the profit in past years came from
dividends received from the subsidiaries rather than from filter sales. One number is worth
noting: the parent received Rp154 miliar of dividends from its subsidiaries in 2024 and only Rp10
miliar in 2025. The operating companies kept their cash in 2025 rather than sending it up.

# 09 — IMPORTANT TRANSACTIONS

**The 2012 flotation (18 December 2012).** 629,962,000 new shares at Rp650, raising about Rp409
miliar gross. PT Putri Gelora Djaja, the group's printing arm, was divested beforehand. This is
the only capital the company has ever raised, and there has been no rights issue, bond or further
share issue in the fourteen years since. The share premium of Rp346 miliar still sits unchanged
in the equity accounts.

**The 2017 Celanese partnership.** Wismilak Inti Makmur, the listed parent, went into partnership
with PT Celanese Indonesia to build a filter-rod business. It is the only diversification in the
group's history and it now supplies about 22% of reported revenue and about 40% of revenue net of
excise. Everything that followed — the 2018 quality certification, the 2019 bonded-zone licence,
the five machines in 2024, the capsule and dual-hollow products of 2025–26, and the export push
into Europe and Canada — is a continuation of it.

**The 2020 customs penalty and its reversal.** Six collection letters in September 2020 for
Rp9.48 miliar of administrative penalties on excise. Paid in full, appealed, carried as a
receivable for two years, and won in full in 2022 with refunds of Rp8.46 miliar and Rp1.03
miliar. Worth recording because it is the only substantive dispute with the excise authority in
the period, and because the company's English-language translation of the second refund figure
(Rp1,207,839,000) does not reconcile to the total while the Indonesian figure (Rp1,027,839,000)
does.

**The 2022 buyback.** 27,900,000 shares — 1.33% of the company — bought from 1 August 2022 for
Rp16.52 miliar, at an average of about Rp592. Resold in two tranches, in 2023 and 2025, for
Rp29.6 miliar combined. Net gain of Rp20.7 miliar to paid-in capital. 12.97 million shares
remain in treasury.

**The 2023 hand-rolled tier move.** Not a transaction in the legal sense, but the most consequential
decision in the period. Hand-rolled volume was allowed to break the 500-million step, taking the
tax rate on that product from Rp118 to Rp214 a stick and roughly doubling the volume. Revenue
from hand-rolled rose 80%, group operating profit doubled, and the balance sheet absorbed Rp694
miliar of extra inventory to feed it.

**The 2023 family share transfer.** 196,039,780 shares — 9.34% of the company — moved from Gaby
Widjajadi to Indahtati Widjajadi during 2023. Not narrated anywhere in the filings.

**The 2024–26 capital programme.** Rp203 miliar of plant spending in 2024, Rp178 miliar in 2025,
and Rp125 miliar of deposits on machines not yet delivered at the end of 2025. Funded by a Rp91
miliar Permata term loan (since repaid), a step-up in short-term borrowing, and the 40% dividend
cut for FY2024. Almost all of it is filter capacity.

**The 2025 related-party lease.** A long lease over Jl. Raya Darmo 36–38, Surabaya, from PT Bumi
Inti Makmur — a company owned by the same shareholders and with a Wismilak director on its board
— capitalised at Rp20.3 miliar, twenty-nine times the group's entire leased-asset base the year
before. No pricing basis disclosed.

**The 2025 board expansion.** Two directors added at the May 2025 annual meeting, taking the
board from four to six. One of them, Surjanto Yasaputera, simultaneously holds the corporate
secretary role — the officer responsible for disclosure and investor relations is now also a
member of the board he is meant to report on.

# 10 — RED FLAG

Four things in this filing set deserve suspicion. None is proof of anything; each is set out with
the evidence and the money flow so the reader can weigh it.

## 10.1 The dormant licensed factory and the excise step

**The facts.** PT Galan Gelora Djaja is a fully incorporated cigarette manufacturer, 99.74% owned
through Gelora Djaja, which produced nothing after 2007. It carries about Rp8 miliar of assets
and an unusable tax loss of Rp29 juta. Every annual report and every set of accounts read for
this exercise states, in the same words, that the company "has no intention to close GGD as it is
expecting to resume the operations when Gelora Djaja expands its business." That is nineteen
consecutive years of holding a licensed cigarette factory open against a future expansion.

**Why that is worth noticing.** The excise band is assigned to the *pengusaha pabrik* — the
licence holder — not to the group. Indonesian commentary on the production-band system has for
years pointed out that a group operating more than one licence can allocate output between them
and keep each below the threshold. One widely-read piece put the point in its headline: the
production-limit scheme opens a gap for excise avoidance. This is a publicly discussed structural
feature of the rule, not a hidden one, and proposals to merge or lower the thresholds have been
made partly in response to it.

**The arithmetic.** Gelora Djaja sold 3.11 billion machine-rolled sticks in 2025 and is growing
into 2026. The step is three billion. Staying below it is worth about Rp1.5 triliun a year. A
second licence holder inside the group would be worth exactly that.

**What is not established.** There is no evidence in any document read that Galan Gelora Djaja
has been reactivated, that any production has been allocated to it, or that the company intends
to. Its assets have not moved. Its stated purpose — to restart when Gelora Djaja expands — is
also, on its face, a perfectly ordinary reason to keep a subsidiary alive. The flag here is that
the group holds an option whose value has just become very large, and that the filings give a
reader no way to see whether it is being exercised. If Galan Gelora Djaja begins producing, that
is the fact to look for.

## 10.2 Related-party dealings with no stated pricing basis

Across six years of accounts, **not one related-party transaction carries a statement of how the
price was set.** The notes give the counterparty, the relationship, the nature and the amount, and
stop. There is no "arm's length" representation, no benchmark, no independent valuation.

That would matter less if the amounts were trivial. Two are not.

**Tobacco purchases from PT Karya Bumi Subur**, a company owned by the same shareholders, ran
Rp54 miliar in 2024 and Rp73 miliar in 2025 — a 35% rise in a year. Tobacco has no public
reference price in Indonesia, quality grading is subjective, and this is the company's largest
input. A reader has no way to test whether the price paid to a related supplier is the price a
third party would have charged. The disclosed ratio — 1.56% of total purchases — is small and
stable, which is the reassuring part.

**Property rent from PT Bumi Inti Makmur**, also owned by the same shareholders, with Wismilak
director Sugito Winarko on its board. It went from Rp1.4 miliar of annual expense to a Rp20.3
miliar capitalised lease in one year. The head-office building at Jl. Raya Darmo 36–38 was taken
one floor at a time from August 2024 and then in full. No valuation is given.

The direction of both is the same: money moving from the listed company to companies the
controlling family owns privately, in amounts that are growing, with no pricing test disclosed.

## 10.3 Five years of nothing to report

Across five annual reports the company discloses: **zero material litigation, zero administrative
sanctions from any regulator, and zero whistleblower reports.** The 2021 and 2022 reports present
"nil sanctions" as an achievement. The governance chapters name no regulatory risk factors at all
— no excise policy risk, no health regulation, no plain packaging, no illegal cigarettes, no
advertising restriction. The only risks named are credit, liquidity, interest rate, currency and
tobacco price.

Two of those claims are contradicted by the company's own financial statements. Gelora Djaja and
Gawih Jaya settled tax assessments in 2023, 2024 and 2025 including explicit administrative
penalties, and Gelora Djaja paid Rp9.48 miliar of excise penalties in 2020. Those are regulatory
sanctions by any ordinary meaning, disclosed in the notes to the accounts and absent from the
governance chapter that is supposed to report them.

Separately, the FY2024 governance chapter states — in both languages — that there *were*
conflict-of-interest transactions during 2024, one paragraph after stating there were no material
transactions with any party. The FY2025 report says there were none. This is most likely a
translation defect, but the report as filed says it.

Five consecutive years without a single whistleblower report, in a company of 3,170 people
operating in a heavily policed excise regime, says either that the culture is exceptional or that
the channel is not used.

## 10.4 The pattern of what is not disclosed

Omissions here are not random. The things Wismilak does not disclose are precisely the things
needed to evaluate the strategy it says it is running: no factory capacity or utilisation in any
year, no production volume anywhere, no statement of which excise band any product sits in, and
no excise tariff table since FY2021 — the FY2021 report reproduced the full schedule, FY2022 gave
narrative only, and the three reports since give nothing at all, even as the variable became more
important. Board remuneration and the audit fee are absent from five years of governance
chapters, and the filter business, now the growth engine, has no disclosed margin, capacity or
contract terms.

None of these is individually unusual for an Indonesian mid-cap. Together they mean that the two
questions that decide this company's earnings — how much it can produce, and what tax band it is
in — cannot be answered from its filings. The full ranked list is in the missing-data section
below.

# MISSING DATA AND OPEN ITEMS

Ranked by how much each would change the analysis.

**1. Production volume and factory capacity, and the excise band actually assigned.** This
decides everything in §05 and §08.2. It exists — the customs authority holds it, and the company
must know it — and appears in no public filing. Without it, a reader cannot tell whether the
three-billion step has been crossed, is about to be, or is being managed around. Likely sources:
the customs directorate's licence registry, a direct question at the annual meeting, or the
company's own answer in a public-expose question session.

**2. Whether PT Galan Gelora Djaja has been or will be reactivated.** Would settle §10.1. Visible
in future accounts as production, revenue or asset movement in that subsidiary, or in the customs
registry as an active licence.

**3. The independent auditor's report.** Not present in the text layer of any of the five annual
reports — the pages exist in the contents lists but did not convert. The firm is
KAP Kosasih, Nurdiyaman, Mulyadi, Tjahjo & Rekan with partner Mulyadi for at least FY2021–FY2022;
the opinion type, the partner for later years, and any emphasis of matter are unverified for
FY2023–FY2025. Would come from the standalone audited financial statements filed with the IDX.

**4. Pricing basis for related-party transactions.** Absent for every transaction in every year.
The tobacco purchases from PT Karya Bumi Subur and the property rent from PT Bumi Inti Makmur are
the two that matter (§10.2).

**5. FY2024's own financial statement notes.** The FY2024 annual report's financial-statement
pages (roughly pages 191–283) are scanned images with no text layer. Every FY2024 figure in this
report comes from the comparative column of the FY2025 accounts, which is complete but carries
the FY2025 auditor's presentation. The original FY2024 notes were not read.

**6. Board and director remuneration.** No figure anywhere except one combined group total. The
split between commissioners and directors, and any per-person disclosure, is missing in all five
years.

**7. National tobacco leaf and clove price series.** No consolidated national farmgate series
could be retrieved. What exists is regional anecdote (Madura leaf at Rp45,000–80,000/kg,
2023–2024) and clove export unit values (US$3.66–7.01/kg, 2020–2025). Since raw materials are 82%
of non-tax cost, a proper series would materially sharpen §04.3 and §05. Likely sources: BPS
producer price statistics, Ditjen Perkebunan, Pusdatin Kementerian Pertanian.

**8. Whether the golongan change takes effect during the year or the following January**, and
whether any deferral facility applies. Determines the timing of any step-up, not its size. Likely
source: the text of PMK 191/2022 and PMK 109/2022 directly.

**9. The 2026 minimum retail price schedule.** The 2026 tariff freeze is confirmed; whether the
minimum retail prices were raised again, as they were for 2025, is not.

**10. National market share by company** and Wismilak's own share of national volume. No source
surfaced. The 1.4% figure used in §03 is derived from Wismilak's own volumes against a national
total and is marked `[calculated]`.

**11. The status of plain packaging.** The implementing health-ministry regulation under PP
28/2024 was targeted for issue by 26 July 2026. Whether it was actually issued, softened, or
deferred is unconfirmed as of the date of this report.

**12. The 2025 donations jump.** Rp1.37 miliar to Rp18.62 miliar with no explanation in any
document read.

**13. The mechanism of the 2023 transfer of 196,039,780 shares** from Gaby Widjajadi to Indahtati
Widjajadi — sale, gift or inheritance — and its price if any.

**14. Hand-rolled tier thresholds confirmation.** The 500 million / 2 billion stick boundaries are
confirmed from published summaries of the regulation, not from the regulation text itself. The
three-billion machine-rolled threshold is confirmed from two independent sources but both trace to
2019-era reporting; no source found reports it changing since.
