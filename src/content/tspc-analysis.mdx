powdered milk, which also runs a national delivery business that carries other companies' goods.
Head office Jakarta; 17 factories around Greater Jakarta and East Java; 46 sales branches.
Market capitalisation **Rp 12.4 trillion** at Rp 2,740 per share (4 September 2026).
Period covered **FY2019–FY2025**, with events through August 2026.

## THE ONE THING TO UNDERSTAND FIRST

The company is being quietly bought back by the family that controls it, and the stock exchange has
just changed the rules in a way that makes that impossible to continue.

Tempo Scan has one large shareholder, a private holding company called PT Bogamulia Nagadi. At the
end of 2019 it owned 80.44% of the shares. At the end of 2025 it owned 90.31%. By August 2026 it held 91.04%,
after a further purchase of 2.11 million shares on 19 August — though almost all of that year's
increase came from purchases earlier in 2026 that are visible only in the ownership filings, not in
any report. The number of shares in existence has not
changed since 2021, so every point of that increase came out of the hands of ordinary investors on
the open market. The portion of the company available to the public — what the exchange calls the
free float — has fallen from roughly one share in five to fewer than one in eleven.

Nothing in six years of annual reports explains this. Not one sentence. The share register is
printed each year and the number simply moves. There has been no tender offer, no announced buy-back
programme, no stated intention. The purchases are disclosed only where the law forces them to be:
in routine ownership filings lodged with the exchange, one transaction at a time.

Here is why that matters now. On 31 March 2026 the Indonesia Stock Exchange raised the minimum free
float from 7.5% to **15%** of shares, with a phase-in for larger companies: at least 12.5% by
31 March 2027 and 15% by 31 March 2028. Tempo Scan is at roughly 9%. It is not drifting toward
the new rule; its controlling shareholder is actively walking away from it. Something has to give
within about eighteen months, and there are only three ways it can go.

- **The family sells shares back.** To get the float from 9% to 15%, roughly 272 million shares
  would have to return to the market — about Rp 750 billion at today's price `[calculated]`. That
  is a large block of stock arriving into a thinly traded name.
- **The company issues new shares.** Around 321 million new shares sold to the public would achieve
  the same ratio while raising roughly Rp 880 billion `[calculated]` — for a company that already
  holds Rp 4.6 trillion of cash it does not need.
- **Neither happens.** The exchange's ladder of penalties runs from warnings and fines to
  suspension, to the "special monitoring board", to forced delisting. A company shunted onto the
  monitoring board trades under different rules and at a different valuation.

So the fact that reframes everything else is this: you are not buying a normal minority stake in a
listed consumer-health company. You are buying a sliver of a family business that has been shrinking
that sliver on purpose for seven years, and which now faces a regulatory deadline that forces a
decision. Whether that decision is a share sale, a share issue, or an exit from the exchange
altogether changes what your investment is worth far more than next year's cough-medicine volumes
will. The ownership record is in §01; the free-float arithmetic is in §10.

# 01 — OWNER

## 1.1 Group structure

The listed company sits one step below a private holding company and one step above about thirty
operating businesses. Nothing complicated happens in between — there is no offshore chain, no
double-decker holding structure, no stapled entity.

The company organises itself into five divisions but reports its accounts in only three segments —
Pharmaceuticals, Consumer Products & Cosmetics, and Distribution Services. Manufacturing and the
supporting companies have no segment of their own; their costs are absorbed into the three that do.
That matters when reading the numbers, and §2.1 explains how.

## 1.2 The owner

**PT Bogamulia Nagadi** is the parent. It is private, files nothing publicly, and for five of the
six annual reports read here the entire disclosure about it was a single sentence saying it is the
parent company. Who owned *it* was never quantified.

That changed once. The FY2025 report carries a new table, headed "controller", naming **Handojo
Selamet Muljadi** as holding 73.76% of Tempo Scan indirectly. The timing lines up with new
beneficial-ownership reporting rules rather than with any change in the business, so read it as a
disclosure that was always true and has only now been written down.

Handojo Selamet Muljadi ran Tempo Scan as President Director from 1995 until 2020, then moved
upstairs to become President Commissioner — the chairman of the supervisory board — a position he
still holds. He is also President Director and majority shareholder of Bogamulia Nagadi. So the same
person owns the parent, chairs the listed company, and ran it for twenty-five years before that.

### The tell in the boilerplate

Indonesian annual reports print a standard sentence beside each director's biography: that the
person has no affiliation with other directors, with the commissioners, **or with the majority
shareholder**. Almost everyone at Tempo Scan gets the full sentence. Three people get a shortened
version that stops before the last clause.

| Name | Role at Tempo Scan | Also | What the sentence omits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Handojo Selamet Muljadi | President Commissioner | President Director and majority holder of Bogamulia Nagadi | Silent on commissioners *and* on the majority shareholder |
| I Made Dharma Wijaya | President Director | Vice President Director of Bogamulia Nagadi | Omits "or the majority shareholder" |
| Diana Wirawan | Vice President Director | Vice President Director of Bogamulia Nagadi | Omits "or the majority shareholder" |

The company is drawing this distinction itself, correctly. The two most senior executives of the
listed company are also board members of its parent. That is not hidden, but it is worth stating
plainly: the people who run Tempo Scan day to day answer to a board they also sit on at the level
above.

## 1.4 The owner's character, read from the record

Nobody states a philosophy in these filings, so it has to be inferred from what the company
repeatedly does. Four habits show up consistently.

**It hoards cash and refuses leverage.** Tempo Scan has held more cash than debt in every single
year examined, and the gap has more than doubled — from Rp 1.7 trillion of net cash in 2019 to
Rp 3.6 trillion in 2025. It repaid its only long-term bank loan in March 2023 and cancelled the
facility three months later. It keeps large credit lines open with four banks and draws almost
nothing on them. Management describes this as "prudent financial management implemented for the last
few decades", which is a fair description of a balance sheet that earns interest income roughly
three times its interest cost.

**It spends on brands rather than on assets.** Over seven years the company put Rp 3.0 trillion into
factories and equipment and roughly Rp 11 trillion into advertising and promotion. The second number
is the real capital budget. §05 explains why that spending is also the most important variable in
the business.

**It pays dividends steadily, not generously.** The payout has run between roughly a third and a
half of profit every year, comfortably inside free cash flow except in 2021 and 2022. Two
consecutive years — FY2022 and FY2023 — paid an *identical* total to the rupiah, Rp 125 per share,
despite profit rising 18% in between. The dividend is set by habit, not by a formula tied to
earnings.

**It buys, it does not sell.** Aside from the Beiersdorf exit, every corporate action in seven years
moved ownership upward: minority stakes stepped up to control, affiliates absorbed, a Nigerian
subsidiary raised from 95% to 99.4%, and the shares of the listed company itself steadily bought in.
The direction of travel is consolidation.

The apparent collapse in FY2025 is not a change in behaviour. PT Tempo Digital Nusantara, the group
e-commerce company that accounted for nearly all of those sales, became a consolidated subsidiary in

modest — under Rp 100 billion against Rp 13 trillion of assets. There is no evidence here of value
being drained through affiliated dealing. Whatever the concern about this company is, it is not this.

Key management pay — commissioners and directors combined, never split — rose from Rp 29.4 billion
in 2020 to Rp 50.8 billion in 2025. Against Rp 1.4 trillion of profit, that is not a large number.

# 02 — MODEL

## 2.1 The shape of the business, and a trap in the accounts

Tempo Scan does three separable things.

1. It **makes and sells its own branded health products** — painkillers, cough remedies, vitamins,
   herbal medicines and infant milk. This is the Pharmaceuticals segment.
2. It **makes and sells its own branded household and beauty products** — baby powder, hand and body
   lotion, soap, men's grooming — and separately runs the Indonesian counters and stores for two
   foreign cosmetics houses. This is the Consumer Products & Cosmetics segment.
3. It **buys other companies' finished goods and resells them** through its own national delivery
   network. This is the Distribution Services segment.

Here is the trap. Tempo Scan's own products are physically delivered by the same trucks, from the
same warehouses, by the same 46 branches as the third-party goods. But the revenue from its own
products is booked in Pharmaceuticals or Consumer Products, not in Distribution. The Distribution
segment's reported revenue is only the resale of **outside** companies' goods. So "Distribution" in
the accounts is not the logistics operation — it is the reselling book. The logistics operation is a
shared cost sitting inside the other two segments.

That distinction explains an otherwise strange picture: a segment with a third of the revenue and a
gross margin of 8%, sitting alongside two segments earning 49% and 53%.

| FY2025 | Revenue Rp bn | % of revenue | Gross profit Rp bn | % of gross profit | Gross margin |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Pharmaceuticals | 4,810.9 | 34.4% | 2,383.3 | 44.8% | 49.5% |
| Consumer Products & Cosmetics | 4,859.7 | 34.7% | 2,596.5 | 48.8% | 53.4% |
| Distribution Services | 4,334.4 | 30.9% | 344.7 | 6.5% | 8.0% |
| **Total** | **14,005.0** | **100%** | **5,324.5** | **100%** | **38.0%** |

Two-thirds of the sales produce nearly all the profit. The remaining third produces almost none.
Understanding Tempo Scan means understanding those two halves separately, because they have almost
nothing in common except a warehouse.

## 2.3 Supply chain and purchase — what the business owns and buys

### The factories

Seventeen plants run by ten subsidiaries, all in Greater Jakarta or East Java, grouped three ways.

| Group | Companies | Facilities | What they make |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Pharmaceutical Manufacturing | Tempo Scan Pacific, Supra Ferbindo Farma, Tempo Scan Industry, Tempo Natural Products | 6 | Tablets, caplets, capsules, effervescent powders, syrups, herbal liquids, creams and gels |
| Consumer & Cosmetics Manufacturing | Rudy Soetadi, Tempo Utama Sejahtera | 6 | Personal care, household care, fragrances, antiseptics, wet wipes, plus in-house plastic packaging |
| Beverage & Nutritional | Polari Limunusainti, Kian Mulia Manunggal | 5 | Energy and health drinks, UHT milk, and powdered milk for infant, follow-on and growing-up formula |

Two supporting plants sit alongside: **PT Pritho** makes plastic packaging at Cengkareng and
Mojokerto, and **PT Tempo Nagadi** makes bar soap at Pulogadung. Making your own bottles and caps is
unusual for a company this size and it is deliberate — it takes packaging cost and packaging supply
risk in-house.

On how hard the plants are working, the FY2025 report is unusually candid. Tablet, effervescent and
traditional-medicine lines are running at what it calls optimal utilisation. Liquid herbals, syrups
and semi-solids are running below optimal — there is room to grow without spending. The medicated-oil
lines are near maximum and expansion projects are under way. Bar soap, hand sanitiser and face-mask
lines are under-used, which is the tail of the pandemic still working through.

in a small town keeps a shelf of it.

## 2.5 Distribute — how the goods reach the buyer

The physical network, at the end of 2025:

| | |
|---|---|
| Sales branches | 46, covering 5,294 sub-districts |
| Total network points | 238 — 4 distribution centres, 46 branches and warehouses, 13 sales offices, 173 sales points, 2 e-commerce fulfilment centres |
| Vehicles | ~400 own vehicles, plus 100+ third-party logistics partners; four transport pools handle ~60% of long-haul volume |
| Active customers | ~100,000 — including 18,000+ pharmacies, 1,610+ hospitals, ~7,000 institutions, 4,600+ modern-trade outlets, 3,140+ wholesalers, 60,100+ retail stores |
| Deliveries | 1.8 million+ delivery notes in 2025; the two e-commerce centres handle 3.5 million+ orders a year |
| Licences | 49 good-distribution certifications for ambient goods, 36 for cold chain; halal certification across all sites |

This is the real asset, and it is the reason the reselling business exists at all. Danone, Roche and
Estée Lauder do not need Tempo Scan's factories; they need its trucks and its 100,000 customer
relationships. Building an equivalent network in Indonesia — an archipelago of 17,000 islands with
poor roads and fragmented retail — would take a decade. That is what makes the network defensible.

It is also what makes the reselling margin so thin. The network is valuable, but the principals know
exactly what a distribution service costs, negotiate it annually, and can move it. §08 shows what
has happened to that margin.

Sales by geography, FY2025: the pharmaceuticals division is 93.3% domestic, and the consumer division
95.0% domestic. Overseas operations — Thailand, the Philippines, Malaysia and Nigeria — are small but
growing quickly: consumer exports rose 25.9% and pharmaceutical exports 21.2%, driven by MY BABY in
Malaysia and the Philippines and by hemaviton in Malaysia.

# 03 — DEMAND

Indonesia has about 285 million people enrolled in the national health insurance scheme, a median
age around 30, and per-capita health spending of roughly US$161 a year — seventh out of eleven
countries in South-East Asia, far behind Malaysia at US$487 and Thailand at US$364. The room to grow
is obvious and has been obvious for twenty years.

But almost none of Tempo Scan's demand comes from that insurance scheme. Its prescription business —
the part that would sell into hospitals and the government formulary — is **Rp 82.9 billion**, which
is 1.7% of the pharmaceuticals division and about six-tenths of one percent of group revenue.
Everything else is bought by a person, at a pharmacy or a shop, with their own money.

That is the demand to understand. Indonesian households pay a large share of their health costs
directly out of pocket — official statistics put it at 61.8% of health spending in 2023, well above
the level the World Health Organization considers healthy. Someone with a headache, a cough, aching
joints or low energy typically does not see a doctor. They go to a pharmacy or a warung and ask for
a name they recognise. That is why a fifty-year-old brand with 46% of the painkiller market is worth
more here than a patent would be.

The consequences run through the whole model:

- **Demand is defensive but not price-insensitive.** People keep buying painkillers in a downturn.
  They switch to a smaller sachet, a refill pack, or a cheaper brand. Management spent 2025 building
  exactly those cheaper formats — "economic pack sizes, extra fill, refill packs" — and explicitly
  described consumers trading down in everyday household goods.
- **Demand is bought, not won once.** Because the purchase is a habit rather than a prescription, it
  can be un-taught. That is why advertising is the largest discretionary cost in the business, and
  why cutting it works for a few years and then stops working.
- **Demand is growing slowly in real terms.** Revenue grew at about 4.1% a year compounded over
  2019–2025 `[calculated]`, against inflation that averaged roughly 2–3%. In volume terms, this
  business is barely growing.

The macro backdrop management cites for 2025: GDP up 5.11%, household consumption up 4.98%, but
inflation rising to 2.92% from an unusually low 1.57% in 2024, and consumer sentiment cautious in
the first half. Interest rates fell from 6.00% to 4.75% through September. In short: a slightly
weaker consumer than the headline growth suggests.

One category is genuinely shrinking. The infant formula market fell 6.2% in 2025 and the growing-up
milk market fell 8.28%. Indonesia's birth rate is declining. Tempo Scan gained share in both — its
formula share rose from 2.7% to 3.5% and growing-up milk from 4.98% to 5.52% — but it is gaining
share of a market that is getting smaller. That matters because the company has just built
domestic milk-powder capacity and is now buying dairy cattle. §08 sets out that tension.

# 04 — SUPPLY

Three supply questions matter here, and they are quite different from each other.

## 4.1 Raw materials — the import problem

Indonesia makes roughly 95% of its own finished medicines but imports about **85% of the active
ingredients** that go into them, mostly from China and India, according to the Minister of Industry
in November 2025. A government plan set in 2020 aimed to cut that dependence to 74% by 2024; the
actual figure is higher than when the plan started.

For Tempo Scan this shows up as currency exposure without any currency borrowing. Raw materials are
42.6% of cost of goods sold, priced substantially in dollars; nearly all revenue is in rupiah. The
company has no long-term debt and only modest dollar credit lines, so a weakening rupiah does not
threaten it financially — it simply compresses margin. Management named exactly this in 2025: "the
fluctuation of foreign currency exchange rates affecting key raw materials", and said it responded
with procurement changes, supplier diversification and product reformulation rather than price rises.

Reformulation is worth pausing on. Changing what is in the product to cope with input costs is a
lever a branded consumer business can pull once or twice without the customer noticing. Pull it too
often and the brand promise erodes. It is a margin defence with a limit.

## 4.2 Finished goods — the concentration problem

More than half the cost base is bought-in finished goods, and one supplier is most of it. This is
covered fully in §08.

## 4.3 Milk — the vertical integration attempt

Tempo Scan's nutrition brands need milk powder, which Indonesia largely imports. The company's
answer has been to move backwards up the chain twice:

- **2022–2025:** built and commissioned domestic powdered-milk production at PT Kian Mulia Manunggal,
  across three sites, with 65% local content. The FY2025 report says the facilities are "operating at
  optimal levels" and that a new line was commissioned during the year. Press reporting puts the
  investment at about Rp 2.5 trillion, which sits oddly against the company's own statement that
  *total* manufacturing capital spending across all seventeen plants over five years was about
  Rp 1.3 trillion — the two figures cannot both be measuring the same thing, and the filings do not
  break out the plant's cost. `EDITED:` the press figure is reported here but not relied on.
- **September 2025:** incorporated **PT Tempo Agri Nusantara**, a dairy-farming venture whose first
  step is importing dairy cattle from Australia. Management is explicit that this is "a long-term
  commercial investment, not merely a CSR activity", and equally explicit that it is "in its early
  development phase".

The stated logic is supply security and cost stability. The unstated risk is that the company is
building supply into a category whose demand is falling — see §03. There is a partial offset:
management notes that UHT milk volumes rose toward the end of 2025 because of the government's free
school-meals programme, which is a new and potentially large source of demand for domestic dairy
processing.

The free-float rule is the one that matters most, and it has nothing to do with medicines.

The national insurance scheme, by contrast, is almost irrelevant to this company — which is itself a
form of protection. The scheme's technical deficit widened to Rp 17.1 trillion in 2025, more than
double the year before, and its net assets fell 38%. A company earning its money from tender-priced
formulary drugs would be exposed to that. Tempo Scan, earning almost everything at retail from
consumers paying cash, is not.

# 05 — KEY VARIABLES

Five things move this business. They are listed in order of how much they move it.

### 1. Advertising and promotion spend — 12.6% of revenue, and the entire earnings story

This is the finding that explains the last six years, and it is not stated anywhere in the filings.

Between 2019 and 2025 Tempo Scan's revenue grew 27%. Its profit attributable to shareholders grew
**154%**, from Rp 554 billion to Rp 1,407 billion. Gross margin did not improve — it went from 38.6%
to 38.0%. So the profit did not come from selling more, and it did not come from making things more
cheaply.

It came mostly from advertising less.

| | FY2019 | FY2025 | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Net sales (Rp bn) | 10,993.8 | 14,005.0 | +27.4% |
| Advertising and promotion (Rp bn) | 1,790.0 | 1,770.7 | **−1.1%** |
| Advertising as % of sales `[calculated]` | 16.3% | 12.6% | −3.7 points |
| Operating profit (Rp bn) | 797.1 | 1,699.6 | +113% |

Advertising spend in 2025 was, in rupiah, *slightly lower than in 2019* — after six years in which
sales rose by more than a quarter and prices generally rose by 15–20%.

Here is the full decomposition of the Rp 902.5 billion of extra operating profit, holding each item
at its 2019 share of sales and letting one thing change at a time `[calculated]`:

| Source of the operating profit increase, FY2019 → FY2025 | Rp bn | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Advertising falling from 16.3% to 12.6% of sales** | **512.1** | **57%** |
| Sales volume growth, at 2019 margins and cost ratios | 228.8 | 25% |
| Other operating income turning from an expense to income | 137.0 | 15% |
| All other selling and administrative costs falling as a share of sales | 101.5 | 11% |

*(The Rp 4.5 billion difference from the actual Rp 902.5 billion increase is the interaction between
the items, which is not separated here.)*

So advertising is not the whole story, but it is the largest single piece of it — bigger than volume
growth, margin and every other cost line put together. Measured to the 2024 peak instead of 2025 the
effect is larger still: advertising fell to 11.3% of sales that year, worth Rp 682.7 billion, or
about two-thirds of the Rp 1,011.8 billion increase in operating profit from 2019 `[calculated]`.

This is not necessarily wrong. Advertising an established brand has diminishing returns, and 16% of
sales may simply have been too much. But it is a lever with a finite length, and 2025 is the year it
started pulling back: advertising rose 15.1% while sales rose 2.6%, and profit fell for the first
time in the period. Whether the 2019–2024 saving was efficiency or under-investment will be answered
by what happens to market share over the next two or three years, not by anything in the accounts.

### 2. Bought-in finished goods — 52.6% of cost of goods sold

More than half the cost base is product purchased from third parties, and the great majority of that
is from one company. This is both the largest cost line and the largest single-party dependency in
the business. Fully treated in §08.

### 3. Raw material prices and the rupiah — 42.6% of cost of goods sold

Roughly 85% of Indonesia's pharmaceutical inputs are imported. Revenue is rupiah. There is no
hedging disclosure and no matching dollar debt. A 10% weakening of the rupiah, applied to the whole
raw-material line with no offset, would cost about Rp 370 billion of gross profit `[calculated]` —
around 22% of operating profit. In practice the company offsets part of it through reformulation and
sourcing, and it holds part of its cash in foreign currency, which produced a foreign-exchange gain
in 2025. But the direction is unambiguous.

### 4. Consumer purchasing power — the volume driver

Sales volume tracks how much money Indonesian households have to spend on non-essentials. In 2025
management described visible trading down in daily necessities, softer discretionary demand, and
built cheaper pack formats in response. The consumer division still grew 6.7%; the reselling division
fell 0.4%. The branded portfolio holds up better than the third-party one, which is what you would
expect — a habit is stickier than a shelf listing.

### 5. The free float rule — no effect on profit, large effect on the share

The regulatory deadline described in §10 does not change a single line of the income statement. It
may change what the shares are worth more than any of the four variables above.

# 06 — VICISSITUDES

What the business has actually lived through, and what it did.

**2019 — the high-spending base year.** Advertising at 16.3% of sales, operating margin 7.3%, the
weakest profitability in the period. This is the "before" picture.

**2020 — the pandemic year, and a good one.** Sales were flat, down 0.2%, but operating profit rose
35%. The gain came entirely from cutting: advertising fell by Rp 423 billion, headcount fell from
5,510 to 5,190, and the company recorded restructuring expense for the second straight year. Demand
for vitamins and supplements was strong; demand for cosmetics was not, with the consumer division's
sales falling.

**2021 — the pandemic tailwind fades.** Sales grew 2.4% and profit 5%. Headcount fell again to
4,725. The distribution segment's gross profit actually fell despite the health-product boom.

**2022 — the worst operating year, rescued below the line.** Operating profit fell 34% because
advertising jumped 36.5% to Rp 1,925 billion, the highest in the period, as the company fought to
restart volume growth after the pandemic. Reported net profit nevertheless rose 22% — because of a
Rp 640.9 billion one-off gain on selling most of its stake in Beiersdorf Indonesia. Free cash flow
was negative that year, and the dividend was paid anyway.

**2023 — recovery, plus a second one-off.** Sales grew 7.1% and operating profit 48% as advertising
came back down. A further Rp 524.3 billion gain landed from selling the rest of Beiersdorf. The last
long-term bank loan was repaid in March and the facility cancelled in June. The distribution segment's
gross margin dropped sharply, from 13.0% to 10.1%.

**2024 — peak profitability.** Operating margin reached 13.2%, the highest in the period, with
advertising at its lowest share of sales in seven years (11.3%). Net profit of Rp 1,447.7 billion.
No one-off gains: this was the underlying business at its best.

**2025 — the lever runs out.** Sales grew 2.6%; advertising rose 15.1%; operating profit fell 6.0%
and net profit fell 2.8% — the only decline in the period. Gross margin actually improved slightly.
The entire miss sits in selling expense, which went from 20.4% of sales to 21.6%. Management frames
the spending as brand investment behind campaign programmes it itemises individually — Rp 21 billion
of media value for a MY BABY mothers' programme, Rp 12 billion for a hemaviton festival,
Rp 11.7 billion for a Marina running event — and is explicit that growth was "not driven by
aggressive discounting or excessive channel loading".

**Through all of it — the ownership creep never paused.** In the pandemic, in the bad operating year,
in the peak year, in the down year, PT Bogamulia Nagadi bought shares. Between 30.6 million and
129.1 million shares a year, every year, without comment.

# 08 — THE NUTRICIA QUESTION

This section exists because one undisclosed commercial relationship is larger than any other single
item in Tempo Scan's cost base, and the accounts describe it in exactly two sentences a year.

## What the filings actually say

Every annual report from FY2020 to FY2025 carries the same disclosure in the cost-of-goods note,
required by accounting rules whenever purchases from one supplier exceed 10% of consolidated revenue.
The FY2025 wording:

> "During the years ended 31 December 2025 and 2024, one (1) year cumulative purchase amount
> exceeding 10% of consolidated net sales was from **PT Nutricia Indonesia Sejahtera** amounting to
> **Rp 3.5 trillion** and **Rp 3.7 trillion**, respectively."

And in the trade-payables note, Nutricia is the largest single name the company owes money to.
That is the entire disclosure. There is no contract date, no duration, no exclusivity clause, no
termination provision, no minimum purchase commitment, no pricing basis, and no description of what
the products are.

One inference has to be flagged before going further. The filings say Tempo Scan *purchases* from
Nutricia; they never say those purchases are finished goods bought for resale. That reading rests on
two things — Nutricia makes finished nutrition products rather than pharmaceutical ingredients, and
bought-in finished goods are far and away the largest line in cost of goods sold, of which Nutricia
is roughly three-quarters. Web searching did not turn up any public confirmation that Tempo Scan is
Danone's Indonesian distributor. The size and the direction of the money are facts; the description
of the arrangement as distribution is an inference.

Compare that with how the same set of notes treats other outside relationships. The Estée Lauder
distribution agreement is named, dated to 1986, and its extension to 2026/27 is stated. The Revlon
licence is named, dated to 1993, and its assignment to Revlon B.V. in 2006 is recorded. Both are far
smaller. The largest one is the one that goes undescribed.

## The scale of it

| | FY2019 | FY2020 | FY2021 | FY2022 | FY2023 | FY2024 | FY2025 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Purchases from Nutricia (Rp bn, as disclosed) | 2,650 | 3,130 | 3,270 | 3,700 | 4,000 | 3,700 | 3,500 |
| — % of total cost of goods sold `[calculated]` | 39.2% | 44.4% | 45.3% | 45.7% | 47.5% | 43.6% | 40.3% |
| — % of the bought-in goods line `[calculated]` | 60.8% | 66.6% | 74.2% | 73.4% | 80.7% | 78.3% | 76.7% |
| Amount owed at year end (Rp bn) | 385.1 | 379.2 | 469.4 | 491.5 | 413.4 | 618.6 | 600.3 |

Between two-fifths and nearly half of everything Tempo Scan spends on goods, every year for seven
years, goes to one company. Roughly three-quarters of all the finished product it buys to resell
comes from that one company.

PT Nutricia Indonesia Sejahtera is Danone's Indonesian specialised-nutrition business — the maker of
Bebelac, Nutrilon and Nutrinidrink. It was folded into Danone's PT Tirta Investama in an internal
reorganisation in March 2025, with no change of ownership. Tempo Scan's filings never say any of
this; they never name a parent, a brand or a product category for Nutricia at all. Note also that a
second Danone company, PT Sarihusada Generasi Mahardhika — the maker of SGM — appears on the FY2025
payables list for the first time.

## Why it matters

**It is most of the reselling business.** Tempo Scan's Distribution segment reported Rp 4,334 billion
of revenue in 2025. Purchases from Nutricia were Rp 3,500 billion. Those two figures are not directly
comparable — the purchase disclosure is group-wide and the segment revenue line excludes Tempo
Scan's own goods — but the orders of magnitude tell you that carrying Danone's nutrition products is
close to being what the Distribution segment *is*.

**The margin on it has halved.** This is the part that should concern a shareholder.

| Distribution segment | FY2019 | FY2020 | FY2021 | FY2022 | FY2023 | FY2024 | FY2025 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue (Rp bn) | 4,490.8 | 4,697.7 | 4,322.2 | 4,852.9 | 4,937.8 | 4,351.3 | 4,334.4 |
| Gross profit (Rp bn) | 578.6 | 591.6 | 543.8 | 630.7 | 498.1 | 363.9 | 344.7 |
| **Gross margin** | **12.9%** | **12.6%** | **12.6%** | **13.0%** | **10.1%** | **8.4%** | **8.0%** |
| Share of group gross profit | 13.6% | 15.1% | 13.6% | 15.1% | 10.6% | 7.0% | 6.5% |

The segment's gross margin held around 13% for four years and then fell in a straight line from 2022
to 2025, ending at 8.0%. Gross profit in rupiah has fallen by 45% from its 2022 peak while revenue
fell only 11%. Management explains the 2025 step as "lower operating leverage due to softer
non-pharmaceutical volumes and competitive trade conditions" — which is a description, not a cause.

There are two readings and the filings do not let you choose between them. One is operating
leverage: fixed distribution costs spread over falling volume compress the margin mechanically, and
volume has indeed fallen since 2022. The other is that Tempo Scan's commercial terms as a reseller
have been getting worse — which is what normally happens when a large principal renegotiates with a
distributor that needs the volume more than the principal needs that particular pair of hands.

The reason it matters which one it is: operating leverage reverses when volume recovers, and worse
terms do not. Segment operating profit would separate the two, and the company does not disclose it
(§10, red flag 6). What can be said is that a 5-point fall in gross margin over three years is large
for a distribution book, and that gross profit in rupiah has fallen four times faster than revenue —
which is more than pure volume mix would usually produce.

**And Tempo Scan competes with it.** This is the genuinely awkward part. Tempo Scan owns *vidoran*
and *vidoran MY BABY* — its own infant, follow-on and growing-up milk brands — and has just built
factories to make the powder for them. Those brands sit in the Pharmaceuticals segment, at a 49.5%
gross margin. Meanwhile it buys and resells Danone's competing
infant nutrition at an 8% gross margin.

So the company is on both sides of the same shelf: it buys and resells the market leader's products
and it manufactures a challenger to them. Its own share is small — 3.5% of infant formula against
Danone's position as one of the two largest players — and it is growing that share while the market
shrinks. Every point of share *vidoran* takes from Bebelac makes Tempo Scan a little more valuable as
a manufacturer and a little less welcome as a distributor.

**What would happen if the relationship ended.** Danone could bring distribution in-house, split it,
or move it to a competitor. If that happened, Tempo Scan would lose roughly Rp 3.5 trillion of
revenue and, at the current 8.0% segment margin, roughly Rp 280 billion of gross profit
`[calculated]`. Against operating profit of Rp 1,700 billion that is material but survivable — perhaps
a sixth of operating profit, before whatever fixed distribution costs could be taken out. The company
would emerge smaller, considerably more profitable as a percentage, and far more concentrated on its
own brands.

That is the honest way to hold this. The Nutricia relationship is not a hidden liability; it is a low-
margin, high-revenue arrangement of unknown duration that flatters the top line, drags the margin, and
could disappear. A reader who values Tempo Scan on revenue is being misled by it. A reader who values
it on the branded business is barely exposed to it at all.

## 9.1 The Beiersdorf exit — Rp 1.17 trillion realised

Tempo Scan held 20% of PT Beiersdorf Indonesia, the local arm of the German company that makes Nivea,
and sold all of it in two tranches straddling a year end.

| | Date | What | Gain booked |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Tranche 1 | 16 Dec 2022 | 11 percentage points sold to Beiersdorf Aktiengesellschaft; the residual 9% reclassified out of associates | **Rp 640.9bn in FY2022** |
| Tranche 2 | 11 Jan 2023 | Remaining 9% sold to Beiersdorf AG and Phanex Handelsgesellschaft mbH | **Rp 524.3bn in FY2023** |
| | | **Total** | **Rp 1,165.2bn** |

The transaction was a good one — a Rp 1.17 trillion gain on a stake carried at a small fraction of
that. But splitting the sale across a year end put a large gain into each of two consecutive income
statements, and FY2022 in particular badly needed it: without the gain that year's net profit would
have fallen by roughly half rather than rising 22% `[calculated]`. Nothing improper about the timing —
the two tranches went to different buyers — but the reported earnings line for 2022 and 2023 does not
describe the underlying business.

Beiersdorf Indonesia still appears on the FY2025 supplier list, owed Rp 4.8 billion. Tempo Scan sold
the equity and kept the trading relationship.

## 9.3 An arbitration that appeared, settled, and took its note with it

From FY2020 to FY2023 the accounts disclosed an arbitration before the Singapore International
Arbitration Centre. The reporting reads as a sequence: no final decision (FY2020–21), an execution
determination issued (FY2022–23), settled between the parties (FY2024). No amount was ever given, and
the counterparty was never named.

What makes it worth recording is what happened next. In FY2025 the entire "contingent liabilities"
note — the note that had housed the arbitration — disappeared from the accounts, and the remaining
notes were renumbered. A disclosure category the company maintained for at least five years simply
stopped existing once the matter inside it was resolved. Nothing improper about that, and the case
itself appears closed, but it is a small illustration of a pattern that runs through this section:
these accounts disclose what is required, for exactly as long as it is required.

# 10 — RED FLAGS

Ranked by how much they should change a decision.

### 1. The free-float collision — a hard deadline eighteen months away

The exchange raised the minimum public float to 15% effective 31 March 2026, with 12.5% required by
31 March 2027 and 15% by 31 March 2028 for companies of Tempo Scan's size. Tempo Scan is at
approximately 8.96% and its controlling shareholder is still buying.

| To reach | Shares needed in public hands | Gap from ~404m | Value at Rp 2,740 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 12.5% by Mar 2027 | 563,733,038 | ~160m | ~Rp 438bn `[calculated]` |
| 15.0% by Mar 2028 | 676,479,645 | ~272m | ~Rp 747bn `[calculated]` |

Alternatively, issuing about 321 million new shares to the public would reach 15% while raising
roughly Rp 880 billion `[calculated]` — which would dilute existing holders by about 7%.

The penalties for non-compliance escalate from warnings and fines through suspension to the special
monitoring board and ultimately delisting. There is no public statement from the company on how it
intends to comply. This is the single largest uncertainty attached to the shares, it is not a
business risk at all, and it resolves one way or another by early 2028.

### 2. Half the cost base rests on one undisclosed contract

Purchases from PT Nutricia Indonesia Sejahtera have been 39–48% of cost of goods sold every year for
seven years, with no contract term, duration, exclusivity or termination provision disclosed anywhere
— while much smaller agreements with Estée Lauder and Revlon are named and dated in the same note.
The margin on that business has fallen from 13% to 8% since 2022. See §08.

### 3. A decade of tax disputes with no amount attached to any of them

Every annual report from FY2020 to FY2025 describes an ongoing series of tax assessments, objections,
appeals and Supreme Court reviews covering tax years from 2014 through 2021, at subsidiaries that are
never named. The most recent development is an assessment received in December 2025 relating to 2021.

**Not one rupiah figure is attached to any individual case, in any of the six filings.** This was
checked systematically across all six years; it is a constant omission, not a disclosure that
appeared once and vanished. The contingent tax exposure of this group is therefore unquantifiable
from public information. There is no provision identifiable in the accounts, and the auditor
issued a clean opinion in each of the three years whose opinion could be read (FY2023–FY2025), which
suggests management does not consider a material loss probable — but a reader cannot check that.

### 4. The earnings engine of the last six years has stopped

About two-thirds of the operating profit growth from 2019 to 2024 came from advertising falling from
16.3% to 11.3% of sales rather than from volume, price or gross margin `[calculated]`. In 2025 that
reversed and profit fell. If the
2019–2024 saving was under-investment rather than efficiency, the cost of restoring brand support
lands on future earnings. Watch the market-share figures the company publishes each year: they are
the only early warning available.

### 5. The reported profit line for 2022 and 2023 is not the business

Two-thirds of a trillion rupiah of one-off disposal gains sit inside those two years. Underlying
profit was far lower and far bumpier than the reported series suggests. Anyone extrapolating a smooth
earnings trend off the printed numbers is extrapolating a disposal.

### 6. Nothing is disclosed by segment below gross profit

The company gives revenue, gross profit and assets by segment, and stops. There is no segment
operating profit in any year. So the actual profitability of the reselling business — thin gross
margin, unknown share of the distribution network's cost — cannot be worked out. It could be earning
a small profit or losing money; the filings do not permit an answer.

### 7. Construction in progress going backwards

33.0% complete with an October 2024 target in the FY2023 accounts; 32.6% complete with a January 2027
target in the FY2025 accounts. A project that has become less complete while slipping more than two
years is either a reclassification or a problem, and no note explains which.

### 8. The falling tax rate

The effective tax rate has declined every year, from 25.3% in 2019 to 18.7% in 2025 — below the 22%
statutory rate — with no explanation offered. Given the unquantified tax disputes running alongside
it, this deserves a question rather than an assumption.

### What is *not* a red flag here

Worth stating plainly, because family-controlled Indonesian groups usually earn several: related-party
dealing is small and shrinking, the balance sheet holds more cash than debt every year, reported
profit converts into cash at 97 cents on the rupiah over seven years, the auditor has been the same BDO member firm
throughout with no change of firm, and issued clean opinions in the three years whose reports are
machine-readable, there are no treasury shares, and
the one share issuance in the period was a modest employee scheme. The FY2025 report is also the
first to name its signing partner and to publish a key audit matter — revenue recognition — which is
a step up in disclosure, not down. The governance risk in this name is
concentrated almost entirely in what the controlling shareholder is doing with the stock, not in what
management is doing with the company.

# MISSING DATA AND OPEN ITEMS

Ranked by what each would change.

1. **The Nutricia contract.** Term, duration, exclusivity, termination rights, pricing basis,
   minimum volumes. Would decide whether roughly a quarter of revenue is secure or cancellable at
   short notice. Likely lives in the agreement itself; a court filing, a Danone disclosure, or a
   material-agreement announcement to the exchange would be the only public routes. Separately, no
   public source was found confirming that Tempo Scan is Danone's Indonesian distributor at all — the
   description in §08 is inferred from the accounts, not corroborated. Danone Indonesia's own
   published materials, or a trade-press profile of its supply chain, would settle it.
2. **The amount at stake in the tax disputes.** Currently unquantifiable. Would size a contingent
   liability that has been running for over a decade. Would appear in tax-court decisions published
   by Pengadilan Pajak, searchable by subsidiary name — but the subsidiaries are never named.
3. **Segment operating profit.** Would settle whether the reselling business earns anything at all
   after its share of the distribution network's costs. The company has the number; it chooses not to
   publish it.
4. **Bogamulia Nagadi's intention on the free float.** Whether it plans to sell down, support a share
   issue, or seek an exemption or a going-private transaction. Would be disclosed in an exchange
   filing when it happens, and not before.
5. **How Bogamulia Nagadi is funding the purchases.** Roughly Rp 1.0–1.5 trillion of stock has been
   bought since 2019 `[calculated]`. Dividends received over the same period were roughly
   Rp 2.7 trillion `[calculated]`, so it is plausibly self-funded — but Bogamulia Nagadi files no
   accounts and this cannot be confirmed.
6. **Why PT Tempo Digital Nusantara became a subsidiary in January 2025** at an unchanged 50% stake.
   Would explain a consolidation-scope change that reshaped the related-party disclosure.
7. **The construction-in-progress project** — what it is, why the completion percentage fell, and why
   the date slipped 27 months.
8. **Halal certification coverage** of Tempo Scan's own supplement and herbal range against the
   17 October 2026 deadline. The plants are certified; the product count is never given. BPOM and
   BPJPH registers would show it.
9. **The commissioner/director split of board pay.** Only a combined figure is ever given.
9b. **The number of shareholders of record** is published only for FY2025 (5,947). The exchange's new
    rule requires at least 1,000 shareholders, so the current count clears it comfortably — but with
    no history, there is no way to see whether the holder count has been falling alongside the float.
